ADHD Barrier → Support Cheat Sheet

For parents, recovery coaches, and school teams in Arizona

START HEREDo not choose supports from the diagnosis. Choose the school problem first. Find the matching barrier, mark a few supports, and bring the short codes to the team. The team must individualize the plan.

Use this guide in 4 moves

1. Notice: Write what happens at school, where it happens, and what seems to make it better or worse.

2. Match: Choose the functional barrier that best explains the access problem. More than one may fit.

3. Shortlist: Circle a few support codes, such as 3-B. Ask the student what is useful, realistic, or embarrassing.

4. Define and review: State who provides each support, where, when, how often, and how the team will know it happened and helped.

IMPORTANTThese are options to discuss, not automatic entitlements. A support belongs in a plan only when evaluation data and the student’s needs support it. A student may need access accommodations and specialized instruction at the same time.

Barrier 1: Directions do not become action

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEStarts the wrong task, misses steps, or understands better one-to-one.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Get attention before an important direction. Use the student’s agreed private cue, then give the direction.
Show directions as well as saying them. Use written steps, pictures, or a quick example.
Keep directions short. Give one step at a time or a short numbered list.
Check that the student knows how to start. Ask them to say or show the first step.
Give the important notes. Provide an outline, guided notes, or a copy of the teacher’s key notes.

Barrier 2: Working memory loses steps or materials

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEForgets later steps, assignments, materials, or submission routines.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Use simple checklists for routines like getting started, packing up, and turning in work.
Put assignments in one reliable place. Use the same paper or digital system every time.
Plan regular organization check-ins. Decide who will check the binder, backpack, or online system and how often.
Give materials a regular home. Use labels, the same locations, or color coding.
Do not make copying the only way to get assignment information. Give the student the details in writing.

Barrier 3: Task initiation and completion break down

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKESits without starting, abandons long work, or needs an adult beside them.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Make the beginning and end clear. Show the first thing to do and what “finished” looks like.
Break big work into smaller parts. Number the sections and add brief check-ins.
Give a private reminder to start if needed. Agree on the cue and how soon staff will use it.
Add smaller due dates for long assignments. Make the checkpoints easy to see.
Plan work periods ahead of time. Make clear how long to work and when to check back, instead of making the student ask each time.

Barrier 4: Time and long-term planning are unreliable

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEUnderestimates time, waits for urgency, or misses project deadlines.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Make time visible. Use a visual timer or written time reminders.
Make a project map that shows the steps, needed materials, due dates, and who will check progress.
Split one big deadline into smaller due dates and mark when each part is finished.
Set a reasonable homework time limit. Decide ahead of time what the family should do when the time is up.
Teach planning when the student does not yet know how to do it. Practice the skill with support instead of only reminding them to plan.
Keep home-school communication simple. Pick one way to share important updates and decide how often it is really needed.

Barrier 5: Written output hides knowledge

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEOral explanations are stronger than written work; mechanics consume the task.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Let the student use helpful typing tools such as word processing, spell check, or word prediction.
Help organize ideas before writing with a graphic organizer, outline, or sentence starter.
When handwriting is not the skill being tested, let the student use speech-to-text, dictation, or an oral answer.
When spelling or handwriting is not the goal, grade the ideas separately from writing mechanics.
Once the student has shown the skill, reduce extra repeated writing that does not add useful information.

Barrier 6: Environmental load disrupts access

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKESound, movement, visual clutter, or crowded spaces pull attention or cause overload.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Choose a seat based on what actually distracts the student and where they can best access instruction.
Give the student a specific quieter place for work that needs fewer distractions. Be clear about which tasks and where.
Reduce distracting sound during activities where noise gets in the way. Use approved noise-reduction tools when helpful.
Show less at once and keep the immediate work area visually simple.
Allow standing or another work position when it clearly helps the student participate and work.
Make busy pages easier to look at. Use a line guide, reading window, or blank paper to cover parts of the page.
Use a digital copy when it helps. Zoom in, highlight, or use read-aloud when the goal is not to test reading.
Make important details stand out. Highlight key words, math signs, decimal points, or place values that are easy to miss.

Notes for this barrier

Barrier 7: Movement, transitions, or regulation interrupt participation

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEWaiting, changes, or long sitting lead to leaving, conflict, or loss of learning.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Give a schedule reminder and countdown before transitions that are hard for the student.
Plan movement before the student is usually overloaded, and use a clear routine for returning.
Make waiting easier by shortening long waits or giving the student something specific to do.
Use an agreed private signal to help the student regulate without calling attention to them.
Plan extra adult support for difficult parts of the day such as arrival, lunch, passing periods, or dismissal.
Remind the student about supports they already have. Do not expect them to notice or ask for help every time on their own.

Barrier 8: Testing format distorts performance

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKERushing, inattention, memory load, or response format hides learned content.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Be specific about extra time: how much, for which kinds of work or tests, and where.
Break tests into smaller sections and give one section at a time.
Use a quieter testing place when needed, and state which assessments it applies to.
Allow planned short breaks during testing. Decide how long, how often, and whether the test clock stops.
When the skill being tested allows it, let the student answer in the booklet, type, or respond aloud.
Allow a math-fact sheet or multiplication chart when the test is about solving the problem, not memorizing facts.
Show what the test will look like. Give a sample or practice question before the test starts.

Home support is separate from the school plan

BOUNDARYAn IEP or 504 plan governs school responsibilities, not parenting at home. Home observations can help explain patterns, but school barriers should be based on school access and performance.

Use one visible home routine at a time.

Make the first action small and concrete.

Share patterns with the school without accepting responsibility for school implementation.

Vocabulary legend

Access: The student can reach, understand, participate in, and benefit from education.

Adverse educational impact: A disability-related effect on school performance, broadly understood; not limited to report-card grades.

Evaluation: Information collected in all suspected areas to identify needs and support eligibility decisions.

FAPE: Free appropriate public education. IDEA and Section 504 use related but not identical standards.

IDEA: Federal special-education law for eligible students who need special education and related services.

IEP: The written IDEA program with present levels, goals, services, supports, placement, and progress reporting.

MET: Arizona term often used for the multidisciplinary evaluation team and evaluation process.

PWN: Prior written notice under IDEA explaining a proposed or refused action and the reasons and data behind it.

Section 504: Federal civil-rights law barring disability discrimination and requiring individualized FAPE in public elementary and secondary schools.

SDI: Specially designed instruction: adapting content, method, or delivery to meet unique disability-related needs.
