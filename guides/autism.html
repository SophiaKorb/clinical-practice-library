School Help for Autistic Students

What is making school harder to access?

Start with what the student is experiencing at school. Choose a few changes that may improve communication, predictability, sensory access, participation, or safety.

START WITH ACCESSDo not assume every difficulty is something the student needs to change about themselves. Look at communication, sensory load, predictability, task demands, social expectations, and safety. Then choose a few changes to try.
1What keeps happening?
2What in the environment may be getting in the way?
3Pick 2–3 supports to try.
4Ask the student and check the data: did they help?
IMPORTANTThese are options to discuss, not automatic entitlements. A support belongs in a plan only when evaluation data and the student’s needs support it. A student may need access accommodations and specialized instruction at the same time.

Barrier 1: Communication demands block access

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEThe student may not understand indirect language, may need more processing time, or may communicate without speech.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Try direct language. State exactly what to do and avoid relying on hints, sarcasm, or implied rules.
Give visual communication. Pair speech with written, picture, modeled, or AAC-supported information.
Let the student processing time. Wait ___ seconds after a question or direction before repeating it.
Honor AAC and other ways of communicating. Ensure the system is available across settings and accepted as the student’s voice.
Check meaning privately. Ask the student to show the first step rather than demand eye contact or public repetition.

Barrier 2: Uncertainty and change disrupt participation

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEUnexpected changes, vague tasks, or transitions cause distress or loss of functioning.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Try a current visual schedule. Keep the schedule visible and mark completed activities.
Preview meaningful changes. Tell and show the student what will change, what stays the same, and what happens next.
Try transition warnings. Give consistent cues at agreed time points before the transition.
Make the beginning and end of the task clear. Show the start, finish, amount, and expected product.
Give a change plan. Teach and use a brief routine for surprise changes.

When sound, light, touch, crowds, or other sensory input is too much

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKENoise, lighting, touch, crowds, smell, or body-state needs interfere with learning.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Look at the sensory environment across the school day. Identify triggers, early signs, and supports across settings.
Adjust sound or lighting when it is getting in the way. Name the tool and activities, such as ear protection or reduced glare.
Offer a lower-sensory-load place. Name where the student may work or recover and how return occurs.
Keep useful regulation tools available. List approved tools and keep them available without making access a reward.
Plan ahead for high-load events. Plan seating, entry, exit, and adult support for assemblies, lunch, drills, or transport.

When planning, starting, or shifting gets in the way

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEPlanning, starting, shifting, organizing, or holding steps in mind limits performance.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Break work into visible steps. Use a short numbered checklist with completion marks.
Give a concrete first step. Staff point to or model the first action.
Try one organization system. Keep assignments, materials, and turn-in routines in named locations.
Plan check-ins. State who checks initiation or progress, at what times.
teach the skill. Use explicit instruction and guided practice if independence is an IEP goal.

When social expectations make learning or belonging harder

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEGroup work, hidden rules, teasing, or rapid peer interaction create confusion or exclusion.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Make group roles clear. Assign clear roles, turn rules, and a visible finished product.
Explain hidden expectations directly and without shame. Explain the actual rule and context without shaming.
Offer a less public way to participate when the learning goal allows it. Allow written, paired, or previewed participation when the target permits.
Name a safe adult. Name whom the student can contact and how during peer problems.
address the environment. Use adult supervision and anti-bullying response; do not place the whole burden on social-skills training.

When the student shuts down or cannot keep participating

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEThe student freezes, becomes unable to speak or work, hides, sleeps, or appears to refuse.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Name early signs and triggers. Document what occurs before shutdown and which early support helps.
Make the barrier, not the learning goal. Change sensory, language, size, or uncertainty demands while preserving instruction.
Try a low-language response plan. Staff reduce talking, show the next safe step, and allow the agreed recovery time.
Create a supported return plan. Name the adult, location, shortened re-entry task, and missed-work plan.
Do not punish a student for shutting down when they are overloaded or unable to respond. Use data and team review to distinguish skill, overload, escape, and safety needs.
Before you move on

When leaving a supervised area creates a safety risk

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEThe student runs, leaves supervised areas, hides, or moves toward unsafe locations.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Create an individualized safety plan. Name triggers, early signs, supervision, staff roles, communication, and reunification steps.
Increase preventive supervision where the risk is known. State the locations and times requiring proximity or handoff.
Use clear adult handoffs during high-risk transitions. Use an adult-to-adult handoff and verified arrival for identified routes.
Teach a safer, faster way to ask for a break, help, or an exit. Explicitly teach and practice a break, help, or exit request that works quickly.
Review every safety incident and update the plan when needed. Record antecedent, location, response, injury/near miss, and plan changes; do not rely on restraint as routine prevention.

When the way a student has to respond hides what they know

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKESpeech, handwriting, reading, speed, or unfamiliar settings distort the intended measure.

Things that may help

PickIdea to ask about
Match the way the student responds to the skill being measured. Allow typing, AAC, speech-to-text, pointing, or oral response when valid.
Preview the format. Provide a sample item and routine before testing.
Try a familiar low-load setting. Define location, staff, and sensory supports.
Break the assessment into sections with planned pauses. Present sections separately with defined breaks.
report supported and independent performance. Keep data clear about the conditions under which the skill was shown.

Home support is separate from the school plan

BOUNDARYAn IEP or 504 plan governs school responsibilities, not parenting at home. Home observations can help explain patterns, but school barriers should be based on school access and performance.

Provide predictable routines and a usable communication method.

Practice safety and break requests when calm.

Share triggers and successful supports, while asking the school to collect its own data.

Vocabulary legend

Access: The student can reach, understand, participate in, and benefit from education.

Adverse educational impact: A disability-related effect on school performance, broadly understood; not limited to report-card grades.

Evaluation: Information collected in all suspected areas to identify needs and support eligibility decisions.

FAPE: Free appropriate public education. IDEA and Section 504 use related but not identical standards.

IDEA: Federal special-education law for eligible students who need special education and related services.

IEP: The written IDEA program with present levels, goals, services, supports, placement, and progress reporting.

MET: Arizona term often used for the multidisciplinary evaluation team and evaluation process.

PWN: Prior written notice under IDEA explaining a proposed or refused action and the reasons and data behind it.

Section 504: Federal civil-rights law barring disability discrimination and requiring individualized FAPE in public elementary and secondary schools.

SDI: Specially designed instruction: adapting content, method, or delivery to meet unique disability-related needs.
