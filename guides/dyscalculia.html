Dyscalculia Barrier → Support Cheat Sheet

For parents, recovery coaches, and school teams in Arizona

START HEREDo not choose supports from the diagnosis. Choose the school problem first. Find the matching barrier, mark a few supports, and bring the short codes to the team. The team must individualize the plan.

Use this guide in 4 moves

1. Notice: Write what happens at school, where it happens, and what seems to make it better or worse.

2. Match: Choose the functional barrier that best explains the access problem. More than one may fit.

3. Shortlist: Circle a few support codes, such as 3-B. Ask the student what is useful, realistic, or embarrassing.

4. Define and review: State who provides each support, where, when, how often, and how the team will know it happened and helped.

IMPORTANTThese are options to discuss, not automatic entitlements. A support belongs in a plan only when evaluation data and the student’s needs support it. A student may need access accommodations and specialized instruction at the same time.

Barrier 1: Number sense and quantity are not stable

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEThe student struggles to compare amounts, estimate, subitize, or connect quantities to numerals.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Provide explicit number-sense instruction. Teach quantity, magnitude, composition, and comparison with a clear sequence.
Use concrete-representational-abstract teaching. Move from objects to drawings to symbols and connect each step.
Use number lines and ten frames. Keep accurate visual quantity models available during instruction.
Teach estimation explicitly. Model reasonable ranges and check answers against them.
Measure strategy and transfer. Record how the student solved unfamiliar quantity tasks.

Barrier 2: Math facts are not retrieved reliably

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEKnown facts are slow, inconsistent, or lost under pressure.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Teach efficient fact strategies. Use number relationships, decomposition, and derived facts.
Provide cumulative retrieval practice. Use brief, spaced practice without public speed pressure.
Allow a fact reference. Provide a table or chart when fact recall is not the target.
Allow a calculator. Use when computation fluency is not the measured skill.
Measure accuracy separately from speed. Do not treat timed sheets as the whole fact profile.

Barrier 3: Procedures and sequences break down

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEThe student loses steps, changes their order, or cannot generalize a procedure.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Provide a numbered procedure card. Keep the exact steps visible during learning and independent work.
Model and think aloud. Show why each step follows the last.
Use worked examples. Place a correct model beside similar practice.
Fade prompts by data. Remove a step cue only after accurate independent use.
Teach for transfer. Practice mixed problems and ask the student to select the procedure.

Barrier 4: Working memory overload causes errors

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEThe student forgets partial results, signs, regrouping, or multistep directions.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Externalize intermediate work. Require and provide space to record partial results.
Give one direction at a time. Keep directions visible and check off steps.
Reduce simultaneous load. Show fewer problems or one problem section at a time.
Allow reference tools. Provide formula, symbol, or procedure references when memory is not the target.
Provide additional processing time. Define the amount and task types.

Barrier 5: Math language is a barrier

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEWords such as difference, factor, fewer, or per obscure the mathematical relationship.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Preteach math vocabulary. Use student-friendly definitions, symbols, and examples.
Mark relationship words. Highlight units, comparison terms, and the question asked.
Use multiple representations. Pair words with equations, diagrams, tables, or manipulatives.
Ask for a restatement. Have the student explain the situation before choosing an operation.
Separate language from computation data. Check whether difficulty remains when the problem is read or rephrased.

Barrier 6: Visual-spatial layout causes errors

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKENumbers drift out of columns, place value is lost, or diagrams and graphs are confusing.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Use graph paper or place-value templates. Provide aligned spaces for each digit and operation.
Reduce visual crowding. Increase spacing and present fewer items at once.
Color-code structure. Use consistent, purposeful cues for columns, axes, signs, or units.
Provide enlarged diagrams. Allow annotation and direct labeling.
Evaluate related needs. Consider vision, motor, or assistive-technology evaluation when data suggest another barrier.

Barrier 7: Math anxiety and avoidance reduce practice

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEThe student freezes, rushes, refuses, or expects failure.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Use supported entry problems. Begin with an accessible item, then move to the target skill.
Give strategy-based feedback. Name the useful step and the exact correction.
Avoid public speed comparison. Do not use public rankings or loss of access for slow work.
Use planned brief resets. Define the break and return problem.
Track engagement and learning separately. Measure attempted items, strategy use, accuracy, and support level.

Barrier 8: Assessment format hides math knowledge

WHAT THIS MAY LOOK LIKEMemory, reading, layout, or speed changes the result.

Clarify before choosing: In which classes, tasks, people, times, or settings does this happen? What changes when the demand or support changes?

Supports to discuss

PickCodeSupport and implementable wording
Identify the math construct. Clarify whether the target is reasoning, facts, calculation, language, or procedure.
Allow approved tools. Use a calculator, fact sheet, formula card, or manipulatives when the target permits.
Read or clarify directions. Do not explain the mathematical answer.
Provide a clean workspace. Use graph paper, enlarged space, or one problem at a time.
Define extended time and breaks. State amount, setting, and eligible assessments.

IEP or Section 504?

KEY RULE A medical diagnosis, including Dyscalculia, does not automatically create IDEA eligibility or guarantee a 504 plan. The school team must evaluate the student’s individual needs. Grades are relevant, but they are not the only evidence of educational impact.

A Section 504 plan may be enough when

A physical or mental impairment substantially limits a major life activity, and the student needs regular or special education, related aids, services, or adjustments for equal access.

The student can access and benefit from general education when the individualized supports are reliably provided.

The written plan can clearly state each support and how implementation will be checked.

An IDEA evaluation may be important when

The disability may fit an IDEA category, adversely affects educational performance, and creates a need for special education.

The student needs specially designed instruction, measurable goals, progress monitoring, related services, assistive technology, or a behavior intervention program to receive FAPE.

Accommodations improve access but do not teach the missing skill or produce adequate progress.

PLAIN LANGUAGE504 is not automatically the “smaller” plan and an IEP is not awarded for having a serious diagnosis. The practical question is what the student needs: access supports alone, or individualized special education and IDEA services. Section 504 can also require related aids or services when needed for FAPE.

Know what kind of support you are discussing

Accommodation: Changes access or response without changing the learning target.

Modification: Changes what the student is expected to learn or the level of the target.

Specialized instruction: Adapts content, method, or delivery to meet unique disability-related needs.

Related service: A service needed to benefit from special education, such as OT, PT, speech-language, counseling, or transportation, when eligibility rules are met.

Assistive technology: A device or service that improves functional access or performance.

Behavior support: Prevention, explicit skill teaching, reinforcement, and response procedures linked to behavior data.

General teaching strategy: Good instruction for many students; it becomes an individualized plan support only when written, assigned, and implemented for this student.

Make the request in writing

ARIZONA TIMELINEFor an IDEA evaluation request, Arizona guidance says the school must act within a reasonable time, no more than 15 school days after receiving the parent’s written request: begin by reviewing existing data or give prior written notice refusing the evaluation. The 60-day evaluation period begins when the school receives informed written consent. A written extension of up to 30 days is allowed only when it is in the child’s best interest and both sides agree.

Simple process

1. Send the request: Email the principal, special education contact, or 504 coordinator. Keep a dated copy and delivery proof.

2. Describe school impact: Use concrete examples from attendance, work, behavior, communication, safety, grades, tests, discipline, or time out of instruction.

3. Request evaluation, not a predetermined label: Ask for evaluation in all suspected areas and for an IDEA and/or Section 504 eligibility decision.

4. Review the proposed evaluation: Ask what areas, records, observations, and tools will be used. Share outside records, but the school must make its own individualized decision.

5. Participate in the meeting: Bring the barrier/support shortlist and ask the team to connect each decision to data.

6. Get the decision in writing: For IDEA proposals or refusals, ask for prior written notice. For Section 504, ask for the district’s written decision and procedural safeguards.

7. Check implementation: Compare the written plan with what actually happens and reconvene if data show it is unclear or ineffective.

Copyable evaluation request

EMAIL TEMPLATE Subject: Written request for school evaluation I am requesting an evaluation of [student] in all areas of suspected disability. At school, I am concerned about [brief examples]. These difficulties affect [learning, participation, communication, regulation, attendance, safety, or performance]. Please consider eligibility and needed services under IDEA and Section 504. Please confirm the date the school received this request and provide the next steps in writing. If the school refuses any part of this request, please provide the written notice and procedural safeguards that apply. Thank you, [Name and contact information]

Copyable meeting request

EMAIL TEMPLATE I am requesting a team meeting to review whether [student]’s current plan is being implemented and is effective. Please bring implementation and progress data for [support codes or plan items]. I would like the team to discuss who provides each support, where and when it occurs, frequency or amount, how missed implementation is recorded, and the date for reviewing results.

Common school responses and useful replies

School responsePossible parent reply
“The student has good grades.”“Please consider the full educational impact, including effort, time, attendance, behavior, communication, safety, and the supports already keeping the grades up.”
“We need to try interventions first.”“I support immediate interventions. Please confirm whether the school is also evaluating. Interventions should not be used to delay an evaluation when disability is suspected.”
“The diagnosis does not qualify.”“Agreed that diagnosis alone does not decide eligibility. Please evaluate the student’s functional limitations, educational impact, and need for services.”
“The student can ask for the support.”“The disability may affect noticing or asking. Please discuss who will prompt or initiate the support and under what conditions.”
“We do not offer that.”“Please consider the student’s individual need rather than a standard menu. What evaluation data support the school’s decision, and what effective alternative is proposed?”
“That is a home or medical issue.”“The concern may have several causes. Please address the part that affects school access, participation, safety, or learning.”
“The team said no.”“Please identify the action refused, the data used, other options considered, and the reason. For an IDEA decision, please include this in prior written notice.”

If a request is refused

Ask whether the refusal is an IDEA decision, a Section 504 decision, or both. The procedures are not identical.

For IDEA, request prior written notice that describes the refusal, explains why, lists the data used, and identifies options considered and rejected.

For Section 504, request the written decision, the district’s procedural safeguards, records-review information, and hearing or grievance procedures.

Ask for the data supporting the refusal and propose another way to meet the same documented need.

Consider reconvening, mediation, an IDEA state complaint or due process complaint, a district Section 504 grievance, or an OCR complaint, depending on the issue and deadlines. A parent center or attorney can help select the right route.

BEST PRACTICE, NOT A LEGAL MAGIC SPELLWritten requests create a clear record and make follow-up easier. They do not force the team to agree. Parents are team participants, but they do not have unilateral veto power over IEP decisions.

Review implementation and effectiveness

Implementation: Did the support happen as written? Use logs, work samples, schedules, observations, device records, or other practical evidence.

Effectiveness: Did access, participation, independence, safety, skill, or performance improve? Compare the same outcome over a defined period.

Student experience: Was it helpful, hard to use, stigmatizing, or unavailable at the moment it was needed?

Decision: Keep it, clarify it, teach its use, change it, increase it, request evaluation, or discontinue it with a documented reason.

AVOID OPTIONAL WORDING Replace “as needed,” “when feasible,” or “when appropriate” with a trigger, schedule, amount, setting, responsible person, and response. Example: “At the start of independent writing longer than one paragraph, the teacher provides the student’s outline template and confirms the first step.”

Home support is separate from the school plan

BOUNDARYAn IEP or 504 plan governs school responsibilities, not parenting at home. Home observations can help explain patterns, but school barriers should be based on school access and performance.

Use real quantities in cooking, games, money, and time.

Allow tools that reduce overload.

Avoid public or family speed comparisons.

Vocabulary legend

Access: The student can reach, understand, participate in, and benefit from education.

Adverse educational impact: A disability-related effect on school performance, broadly understood; not limited to report-card grades.

Evaluation: Information collected in all suspected areas to identify needs and support eligibility decisions.

FAPE: Free appropriate public education. IDEA and Section 504 use related but not identical standards.

IDEA: Federal special-education law for eligible students who need special education and related services.

IEP: The written IDEA program with present levels, goals, services, supports, placement, and progress reporting.

MET: Arizona term often used for the multidisciplinary evaluation team and evaluation process.

PWN: Prior written notice under IDEA explaining a proposed or refused action and the reasons and data behind it.

Section 504: Federal civil-rights law barring disability discrimination and requiring individualized FAPE in public elementary and secondary schools.

SDI: Specially designed instruction: adapting content, method, or delivery to meet unique disability-related needs.
