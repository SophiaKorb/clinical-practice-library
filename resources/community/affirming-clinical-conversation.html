Affirming conversations without diagnostic shortcuts

A practical starting point when LGBTQ+ identity intersects with a mental-health referral. Clarify the actual clinical question, protect agency, and keep contextual stress distinct from diagnosis.

1. Begin with the person's goal

  • Ask what they want help with before deciding which aspect of identity or experience is relevant.
  • Invite—but never require—a chosen name, pronouns, and preferences for communication in this setting. Ask whether records or communications create privacy concerns.
  • Use questions such as “What part of this situation would you most like to change?” and “What support has helped before?”

2. Formulate rather than presume

  • Assess the presenting symptoms and functioning using ordinary clinical standards. Separately ask about family responses, stigma, safety at school or work, supportive relationships, and obstacles to care.
  • Sexual orientation or gender identity is not a symptom, diagnosis, or a treatment target. The distress may relate to discrimination, concurrent mental-health conditions, interpersonal conflict, medical concerns, or several factors.
  • Document actual observations and the individual's reported priorities, not inferred identity or disclosure status.

3. Collaborative options

  • Offer different pathways: psychotherapy for the presenting concern, practical accommodations, supportive caregiver conversation, peer/community resources, school coordination, or consultation with a qualified medical or legal professional when requested.
  • Assess immediate safety as indicated without assuming every LGBTQ+ person is in crisis. Avoid treating group-level risk statistics as an individual determination.
  • Ask permission before contacting family, school or community providers; explain confidentiality rules and limits accurately, especially with minors.

4. Review whether help is helping

  • Choose person-defined markers such as school attendance, isolation, distress, preferred social connections, functional engagement or sense of safety.
  • Check for unintended harms: unwanted disclosure, pressure to choose a label, inaccessible services, or invalidating interactions. Revise the plan accordingly.
