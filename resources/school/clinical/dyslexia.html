School Access Toolkit · Clinical

Dyslexia
Barrier → Support

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Clinical version for functional formulation, differential questions, implementable plan wording, and implementation review. Barrier numbers and support codes match the Plain Language version.

Clinical and educational formulation

  • Define the barrier in a specific task, setting, and demand.
  • Generate competing hypotheses instead of relying on diagnosis alone.
  • Compare conditions, supports, settings, and response formats.
  • Separate access supports from instruction for missing skills.
  • Operationalize who, where, when, how often, and how success will be reviewed.

Barrier 1

Phonemic awareness is weak

Observable presentation: The student has difficulty blending, segmenting, deleting, or changing speech sounds.

1-AProvide explicit phonemic instruction. Teach the exact sound skill with modeling, guided practice, and feedback.
1-BConnect sounds to print. Link phonemes to graphemes as soon as the task permits.
1-CUse visible sound mapping. Use sound boxes or chips, then connect them to letters.
1-DPractice briefly and often. Schedule cumulative practice and record accuracy.
1-ESet skill-specific goals. Measure the named phonemic skill, independence, retention, and transfer.
Consider

Phonemic awareness is the ability to notice and manipulate sounds in spoken words. Weakness here can impair later decoding.

Data to review

Use direct measures of blending, segmenting, and manipulating sounds rather than relying on spelling or reading grade alone.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Access accommodations do not remediate a phonological skill deficit.

Barrier 2

Sound-symbol knowledge and decoding are insecure

Observable presentation: The student guesses, relies on pictures, or cannot read unfamiliar words with taught patterns.

2-AProvide systematic phonics instruction. Use an explicit, cumulative sequence matched to assessed needs.
2-BTeach through the whole word. Model attention to every grapheme and blending through the word.
2-CUse aligned decodable text. Practice connected text containing patterns already taught.
2-DGive immediate corrective feedback. Name the exact pattern and have the student retry.
2-EMeasure transfer. Check unfamiliar words, not only a memorized list.
Consider

Differentiate letter-sound knowledge, blending, word attack, orthographic learning, and guessing from context.

Data to review

Review accuracy on unfamiliar and nonsense words as well as familiar words.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Explicit, systematic decoding instruction may be needed in addition to access supports.

Barrier 3

Long words and morphology break down

Observable presentation: The student omits syllables, misreads affixes, or avoids academic words.

3-ATeach a word-analysis routine. Identify vowels, syllables, base words, prefixes, suffixes, and roots.
3-BTeach morphology. Connect meaningful word parts to pronunciation, spelling, and meaning.
3-CPractice curriculum words. Use academic words from current subjects.
3-DUse visible word tools. Provide word matrices, word sums, or syllable marking.
3-EMonitor untaught words. Measure use of the routine on new multisyllabic words.
Consider

Long-word reading draws on syllable structure, morphology, decoding, and vocabulary.

Data to review

Examine errors on affixes, roots, vowel patterns, and multisyllabic words.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Morphology can support both decoding and meaning, but it should not substitute for foundational phonics when that is weak.

Barrier 4

Accurate reading is slow and effortful

Observable presentation: Decoding takes so much effort that stamina, phrasing, or meaning suffers.

4-ATreat decoding first. Address unresolved accuracy problems before speed becomes the main target.
4-BUse supported repeated reading. Model and reread an appropriate passage with feedback.
4-CUse phrase support. Use echo, partner, or phrase-cued reading matched to need.
4-DProtect dignity. No cold public reading or public speed ranking.
4-EMeasure a full fluency profile. Track accuracy, rate, phrasing, comprehension, stamina, and experience.
Consider

Fluency reflects accuracy plus automaticity and prosody. Slow reading may remain effortful even when accuracy improves.

Data to review

Use rate and accuracy data with appropriately leveled passages.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Extended time helps access, but fluency instruction may still be required.

Barrier 5

Spelling and encoding are weak

Observable presentation: Spellings are inconsistent, sounds are omitted, or taught patterns do not transfer to writing.

5-ATeach spelling with reading. Use phoneme-grapheme mapping, syllables, and morphology.
5-BUse cumulative dictation. Practice sounds, words, phrases, and sentences with feedback.
5-CExplain patterns. Teach why spellings work instead of visual memorization alone.
5-DProvide access tools. Allow spelling references, word prediction, or speech-to-text when spelling is not tested.
5-EAnalyze errors. Track patterns, retention, and transfer to real writing.
Consider

Encoding/spelling provides information about phonology, orthography, and morphology.

Data to review

Compare dictated spelling, sentence writing, and spontaneous writing.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Spell-check is an access tool, not a replacement for explicit spelling instruction when needed.

Barrier 6

Print access limits grade-level learning

Observable presentation: Listening comprehension is stronger than independent reading of content.

6-AProvide text-to-speech or audio. Use for grade-level content when decoding is not the target.
6-BProvide accessible digital text. Allow highlighting, zoom, pacing, and replay.
6-CPreteach essential vocabulary. Build knowledge before print-heavy instruction.
6-DAllow accessible responses. Change response mode when it does not change the intended skill.
6-EContinue remediation. Access tools do not replace explicit reading instruction.
Consider

Heavy decoding demand can depress comprehension and content learning even when oral language is stronger.

Data to review

Compare listening comprehension with independent reading comprehension and note the effect of text-to-speech or read-aloud access.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Separate access to grade-level content from the separate need to teach reading skills.

Barrier 7

Assessment format hides content knowledge

Observable presentation: Decoding, fluency, or spelling affects a test meant to measure another subject.

7-AName the construct. Document what the test is intended to measure.
7-BUse read-aloud or text-to-speech. Use only when decoding is not the measured skill and rules allow.
7-CDefine extended time. State amount, setting, and eligible tests.
7-DAllow accessible response. Use typing, speech-to-text, or oral response when valid.
7-ESeparate scores. Report content separately from reading or spelling when possible.
Consider

Assessment validity depends on what the test is intended to measure.

Data to review

When reading is not the target, consider audio/text-to-speech or other access supports if allowed and appropriate.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

When decoding itself is the target, an accommodation that removes decoding may invalidate the measure.

Clinical documentation

Diagnosis can be relevant evidence, but it does not by itself determine IEP or Section 504 eligibility. Describe functional impairment, likely school impact, and why school-based evaluation or review is recommended.
