School Access Toolkit · Clinical

Persistent Conflict & ODD
Barrier → Support

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Clinical version for functional formulation, differential questions, implementable plan wording, and implementation review. Barrier numbers and support codes match the Plain Language version.

Clinical and educational formulation

  • Define the barrier in a specific task, setting, and demand.
  • Generate competing hypotheses instead of relying on diagnosis alone.
  • Compare conditions, supports, settings, and response formats.
  • Separate access supports from instruction for missing skills.
  • Operationalize who, where, when, how often, and how success will be reviewed.

Barrier 1

Escalation blocks learning

Observable presentation: Correction, frustration, or perceived threat quickly becomes argument, refusal, or aggression.

1-AComplete a functional behavior assessment. Use data to identify triggers, function, skills, and consequences when behavior impedes learning.
1-BUse a positive behavior support plan. Specify prevention, teaching, reinforcement, and safe response steps.
1-CCorrect privately and briefly. Use neutral language and avoid public power struggles.
1-DTeach regulation skills. Provide explicit instruction and practice outside crisis.
1-EUse a recovery and return plan. Name the location, adult, time limit, and next doable task.
Consider

Use functional assessment rather than treating escalation as a trait. Consider task difficulty, humiliation, peer conflict, sensory load, fatigue, reinforcement, and adult interaction patterns.

Data to review

Track antecedents, escalation sequence, adult responses, recovery, and missed instruction.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Prevention and replacement skills usually provide more information than consequences alone.

Barrier 2

Demand for autonomy creates power struggles

Observable presentation: The student resists commands, loses control when choices disappear, or argues over minor details.

2-AOffer bounded choices. Give two acceptable options that meet the same learning goal.
2-BUse collaborative planning. Involve the student in routines, goals, and support selection.
2-CState nonnegotiables clearly. Separate safety or learning requirements from flexible details.
2-DUse neutral start prompts. Give the agreed prompt and processing time, without repeated debate.
2-EReinforce independent follow-through. Notice completion and repair, not submission or obedience as personality traits.
Consider

Autonomy-seeking can become a repeated coercive cycle when adults and student escalate around control.

Data to review

Identify where meaningful choice is possible and where limits are genuinely nonnegotiable.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Choice should be real and bounded, not a disguised demand.

Barrier 3

Executive-function demands look like defiance

Observable presentation: The student argues or avoids when work is unclear, too long, or hard to start.

3-AClarify the first step. Show the exact action and finished product.
3-BChunk the task. Use numbered sections and checks.
3-CKeep directions visible. Provide short written or modeled directions.
3-DUse scheduled organization support. Name staff and frequency.
3-EAssess skill gaps. Evaluate learning, language, attention, and motor barriers rather than assuming motivation.
Consider

Initiation, working memory, planning, language, or processing problems can be mislabeled as defiance.

Data to review

Test whether clearer first steps, reduced verbal load, visual supports, or skill instruction change behavior.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Do not infer motive before checking task accessibility.

Barrier 4

Inflexibility makes changes or errors intolerable

Observable presentation: Unexpected changes, losing, correction, or another person’s idea causes conflict.

4-APreview change. State what changes, what stays the same, and the next step.
4-BTeach a flexibility routine. Practice language and actions for plan changes outside conflict.
4-CUse predictable repair. Provide a brief, consistent process for correcting errors or harm.
4-DReduce unnecessary confrontation. Change adult wording or timing without removing the learning expectation.
4-ETrack triggers and successful flexibility. Use data to revise supports.
Consider

Consider cognitive flexibility, frustration tolerance, perfectionism, autism/anxiety features, and learning history.

Data to review

Identify triggers involving mistakes, rule changes, losing, unexpected correction, or ambiguity.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Prepare for predictable changes and teach recovery/repair rather than demanding immediate compliance during peak arousal.

Barrier 5

Communication difficulty fuels conflict

Observable presentation: The student cannot explain needs, interpret language, or solve disagreements efficiently.

5-AAssess language and communication. Consider receptive, expressive, pragmatic, and AAC needs.
5-BTeach replacement communication. Practice help, break, disagreement, and repair language.
5-CUse visual problem-solving steps. Keep a brief conflict routine available.
5-DAllow processing time. Pause before expecting an answer.
5-EProvide structured mediation. Use a trained adult and avoid forced apologies during escalation.
Consider

Receptive language, expressive language, pragmatics, and emotion communication can all contribute to conflict.

Data to review

Compare what happens when directions are brief, neutral, visual, and checked for understanding.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Escalation may communicate confusion or threat even when the words sound oppositional.

Barrier 6

Trauma or threat responses affect participation

Observable presentation: Adult tone, proximity, unpredictability, or loss of control triggers fight, flight, freeze, or shutdown.

6-AUse a trauma-informed plan. Identify triggers and supportive responses without requiring disclosure of trauma details.
6-BIncrease predictability. Use consistent routines and advance notice.
6-CRespect physical space. Define safe proximity and avoid blocking exits except for immediate safety.
6-DUse a trusted contact. Name a regulated adult for early support.
6-ECoordinate safety and behavior plans. Ensure plans do not rely on humiliation, seclusion, or escalating confrontation.
Consider

Trauma, chronic threat, coercion, bullying, and unsafe environments can produce fight/flight responses that resemble oppositional behavior.

Data to review

Assess safety and context before behavior plans that increase demand or consequence intensity.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Do not use a behavior plan to require compliance with abuse, coercion, or unsafe conditions.

Clinical documentation

Diagnosis can be relevant evidence, but it does not by itself determine IEP or Section 504 eligibility. Describe functional impairment, likely school impact, and why school-based evaluation or review is recommended.
