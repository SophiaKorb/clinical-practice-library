Dyscalculia
Barrier → Support

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Clinical version for functional formulation, differential questions, implementable plan wording, and implementation review. Barrier numbers and support codes match the Plain Language version.

Clinical and educational formulation

  • Define the barrier in a specific task, setting, and demand.
  • Generate competing hypotheses instead of relying on diagnosis alone.
  • Compare conditions, supports, settings, and response formats.
  • Separate access supports from instruction for missing skills.
  • Operationalize who, where, when, how often, and how success will be reviewed.

Barrier 1

Number sense and quantity are not stable

Observable presentation: The student struggles to compare amounts, estimate, subitize, or connect quantities to numerals.

1-AProvide explicit number-sense instruction. Teach quantity, magnitude, composition, and comparison with a clear sequence.
1-BUse concrete-representational-abstract teaching. Move from objects to drawings to symbols and connect each step.
1-CUse number lines and ten frames. Keep accurate visual quantity models available during instruction.
1-DTeach estimation explicitly. Model reasonable ranges and check answers against them.
1-EMeasure strategy and transfer. Record how the student solved unfamiliar quantity tasks.
Consider

Consider magnitude comparison, subitizing, cardinality, number line understanding, and symbolic/non-symbolic quantity links.

Data to review

Use concrete tasks and developmental data rather than a single computation score.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Weak number sense can affect later facts and procedures, but not every math difficulty reflects dyscalculia.

Barrier 2

Math facts are not retrieved reliably

Observable presentation: Known facts are slow, inconsistent, or lost under pressure.

2-ATeach efficient fact strategies. Use number relationships, decomposition, and derived facts.
2-BProvide cumulative retrieval practice. Use brief, spaced practice without public speed pressure.
2-CAllow a fact reference. Provide a table or chart when fact recall is not the target.
2-DAllow a calculator. Use when computation fluency is not the measured skill.
2-EMeasure accuracy separately from speed. Do not treat timed sheets as the whole fact profile.
Consider

Differentiate retrieval from conceptual understanding and strategy use.

Data to review

Check whether the student can derive facts using known relationships even when rapid retrieval is weak.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Timed fact performance should not be the sole measure of mathematical competence.

Barrier 3

Procedures and sequences break down

Observable presentation: The student loses steps, changes their order, or cannot generalize a procedure.

3-AProvide a numbered procedure card. Keep the exact steps visible during learning and independent work.
3-BModel and think aloud. Show why each step follows the last.
3-CUse worked examples. Place a correct model beside similar practice.
3-DFade prompts by data. Remove a step cue only after accurate independent use.
3-ETeach for transfer. Practice mixed problems and ask the student to select the procedure.
Consider

Procedural errors can reflect sequencing, conceptual gaps, working memory, attention, or language.

Data to review

Analyze error patterns rather than simply counting wrong answers.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

External step supports can improve access while instruction targets the underlying concept or procedure.

Barrier 4

Working memory overload causes errors

Observable presentation: The student forgets partial results, signs, regrouping, or multistep directions.

4-AExternalize intermediate work. Require and provide space to record partial results.
4-BGive one direction at a time. Keep directions visible and check off steps.
4-CReduce simultaneous load. Show fewer problems or one problem section at a time.
4-DAllow reference tools. Provide formula, symbol, or procedure references when memory is not the target.
4-EProvide additional processing time. Define the amount and task types.
Consider

Math places high demands on holding intermediate values, operations, and instructions in mind.

Data to review

Compare performance with written steps, scratch space, calculators when appropriate, and reduced memory load.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Working-memory supports should not obscure whether conceptual understanding is present.

Barrier 5

Math language is a barrier

Observable presentation: Words such as difference, factor, fewer, or per obscure the mathematical relationship.

5-APreteach math vocabulary. Use student-friendly definitions, symbols, and examples.
5-BMark relationship words. Highlight units, comparison terms, and the question asked.
5-CUse multiple representations. Pair words with equations, diagrams, tables, or manipulatives.
5-DAsk for a restatement. Have the student explain the situation before choosing an operation.
5-ESeparate language from computation data. Check whether difficulty remains when the problem is read or rephrased.
Consider

Vocabulary, syntax, comparative terms, and word-problem language can block math performance.

Data to review

Compare numeric problems with linguistically complex versions of the same concept.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Consider language disorder or multilingual-learning factors before attributing language-heavy math errors to dyscalculia.

Barrier 6

Visual-spatial layout causes errors

Observable presentation: Numbers drift out of columns, place value is lost, or diagrams and graphs are confusing.

6-AUse graph paper or place-value templates. Provide aligned spaces for each digit and operation.
6-BReduce visual crowding. Increase spacing and present fewer items at once.
6-CColor-code structure. Use consistent, purposeful cues for columns, axes, signs, or units.
6-DProvide enlarged diagrams. Allow annotation and direct labeling.
6-EEvaluate related needs. Consider vision, motor, or assistive-technology evaluation when data suggest another barrier.
Consider

Alignment, place value columns, graphs, geometry, and crowded notation can create visual-spatial errors.

Data to review

Look for reversals, lost place, misaligned columns, or difficulty extracting information from diagrams.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Provide layout supports without assuming a visual-spatial disorder from math errors alone.

Barrier 7

Math anxiety and avoidance reduce practice

Observable presentation: The student freezes, rushes, refuses, or expects failure.

7-AUse supported entry problems. Begin with an accessible item, then move to the target skill.
7-BGive strategy-based feedback. Name the useful step and the exact correction.
7-CAvoid public speed comparison. Do not use public rankings or loss of access for slow work.
7-DUse planned brief resets. Define the break and return problem.
7-ETrack engagement and learning separately. Measure attempted items, strategy use, accuracy, and support level.
Consider

Anxiety can reduce working memory and practice, while repeated failure can also create anxiety secondarily.

Data to review

Compare supported practice with timed/evaluative conditions.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Reduce threat and improve access without removing all opportunities to build mathematical competence.

Barrier 8

Assessment format hides math knowledge

Observable presentation: Memory, reading, layout, or speed changes the result.

8-AIdentify the math construct. Clarify whether the target is reasoning, facts, calculation, language, or procedure.
8-BAllow approved tools. Use a calculator, fact sheet, formula card, or manipulatives when the target permits.
8-CRead or clarify directions. Do not explain the mathematical answer.
8-DProvide a clean workspace. Use graph paper, enlarged space, or one problem at a time.
8-EDefine extended time and breaks. State amount, setting, and eligible assessments.
Consider

Separate speed, memory, language, layout, and anxiety effects from mathematical knowledge.

Data to review

Use multiple response formats and review whether timed conditions are essential to the construct being tested.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Accommodations should improve validity without changing the mathematical target.

Clinical documentation

Diagnosis can be relevant evidence, but it does not by itself determine IEP or Section 504 eligibility. Describe functional impairment, likely school impact, and why school-based evaluation or review is recommended.
