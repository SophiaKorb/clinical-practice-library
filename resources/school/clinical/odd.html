School Access Toolkit · Clinical draft

Oppositional Patterns
Barrier → Support

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Functional formulation guide for students whose school participation includes recurrent refusal, argument, escalation, or conflict. Do not infer function from an ODD diagnosis. Barrier numbers and support codes match the Plain Language version.

Clinical and educational formulation

  • Describe the demand, interaction, setting, and observable response.
  • Test competing hypotheses: skill deficit, overload, anxiety, autonomy threat, reinforcement history, communication, trauma, learning difficulty, or relationship rupture.
  • Do not use “won’t” when the available data only establish “didn’t.”
  • Separate safety limits from power struggles and access supports from consequences.
  • Review whether adult responses inadvertently intensify or reinforce the pattern.

Barrier 1

Demands trigger refusal or prolonged negotiation

Observable presentation: The student argues, delays, bargains, leaves, or does not begin when a demand is presented.

1-AClarify the demand. State the required outcome, the available choices, and the point of completion.
1-BReduce verbal escalation. Give the direction briefly, allow processing time, and avoid repeated argument.
1-COffer bounded choice. Provide two or more acceptable ways to begin or complete the requirement.
1-DCheck task accessibility. Assess whether academic, language, executive-function, sensory, or anxiety demands exceed current capacity.
1-EReinforce task entry. Provide specific feedback for beginning or re-engaging rather than waiting for perfect completion.
Consider / data to review

Consider whether refusal changes with task difficulty, adult, setting, audience, predictability, or degree of choice.

Avoid assuming

Do not treat the behavior label as its explanation. Test function and context before selecting an intervention.

Barrier 2

Loss of autonomy becomes a control struggle

Observable presentation: The interaction shifts from the original task to who is in charge, often with escalating demands and counter-demands.

2-APreserve meaningful autonomy. Identify which parts of the task are nonnegotiable and which can be chosen.
2-BUse neutral limit language. State the boundary and next step without debate, threat, or moralizing.
2-CAvoid public contests. Move correction or problem-solving out of the peer audience when feasible.
2-DBuild proactive choice. Offer control before escalation rather than adding choices only after refusal.
2-EReturn to the original goal. After conflict, restate the task or need instead of adding unrelated demands.
Consider / data to review

Distinguish developmentally appropriate autonomy seeking from a pervasive pattern of conflict.

Avoid assuming

Do not treat the behavior label as its explanation. Test function and context before selecting an intervention.

Barrier 3

Correction or perceived unfairness rapidly escalates emotion

Observable presentation: The student reacts strongly to feedback, consequences, rule enforcement, losing, or perceived inconsistency.

3-ACorrect privately and specifically. Address the behavior or work product without global character judgments.
3-BSeparate validation from agreement. Acknowledge the student’s perspective while maintaining the limit.
3-CUse a regulation pause. Define a brief pause and return routine before problem-solving continues.
3-DMake rules predictable. Apply stated expectations consistently across adults and settings when possible.
3-EReview the event later. Problem-solve after arousal decreases rather than requiring insight during escalation.
Consider / data to review

Consider rejection sensitivity, trauma, anxiety, mood symptoms, language processing, rigid thinking, and prior experiences of inconsistent discipline.

Avoid assuming

Do not treat the behavior label as its explanation. Test function and context before selecting an intervention.

Barrier 4

Transitions and stopping preferred activities produce conflict

Observable presentation: The student resists ending, shifting, handing over materials, or moving to the next setting.

4-APreview the transition. Give a clear warning, endpoint, and next activity.
4-BMake the stopping point visible. Use a timer, checklist, or concrete finish marker.
4-CUse a consistent transition routine. Keep the cue and sequence stable across repeated transitions.
4-DPlan for unfinished work. State what happens to an incomplete preferred activity or task after the transition.
4-EReinforce flexible shifting. Notice successful transitions, especially when the student stops without prolonged negotiation.
Consider / data to review

Compare preferred-to-nonpreferred, nonpreferred-to-nonpreferred, social, sensory, and unstructured transitions.

Avoid assuming

Do not treat the behavior label as its explanation. Test function and context before selecting an intervention.

Barrier 5

Conflict with adults disrupts access to instruction

Observable presentation: The student becomes preoccupied with a particular adult interaction, tone, consequence, or prior conflict.

5-AAssign a repair pathway. Identify how the student and adult can reset after conflict.
5-BUse one lead communicator during escalation. Reduce multiple adults giving simultaneous directions or corrections.
5-CStart from observable facts. Describe what happened without attributing motive, disrespect, manipulation, or intent.
5-DSchedule neutral contact. Build brief positive interactions that are not contingent on behavior problems.
5-EChange the interaction pattern when needed. Review adult prompts, proximity, tone, timing, and reinforcement as intervention variables.
Consider / data to review

A relationship pattern can maintain behavior even when the original trigger is no longer present.

Avoid assuming

Do not treat the behavior label as its explanation. Test function and context before selecting an intervention.

Barrier 6

Peer audience or social status amplifies the behavior

Observable presentation: Refusal, argument, humor, provocation, or escalation is more likely when peers are watching.

6-AReduce the audience. Handle correction and re-entry privately when feasible.
6-BProvide a face-saving path back. Offer a brief, neutral way to rejoin without a public apology or debate.
6-CReinforce prosocial status. Create legitimate roles for leadership, humor, competence, or contribution.
6-DTrack peer contingencies. Note whether laughter, attention, avoidance, or group dynamics follow the behavior.
6-ETeach alternatives explicitly. Practice ways to disagree, decline, save face, or seek help without escalating.
Consider / data to review

Do not assume peer attention is the function without observing what reliably changes the pattern.

Avoid assuming

Do not treat the behavior label as its explanation. Test function and context before selecting an intervention.

Barrier 7

After escalation, shame or rupture prevents re-entry

Observable presentation: The student stays withdrawn, angry, avoidant, or oppositional after the immediate conflict has ended.

7-AUse a predictable re-entry routine. Define the first small step back into instruction.
7-BDo not require immediate processing. Allow regulation before discussion, reflection, or repair.
7-CKeep repair proportionate. Focus on restoring safety, participation, and any concrete harm rather than extracting remorse.
7-DRestore access after consequences. Ensure the student can return to instruction and needed supports.
7-EReview patterns collaboratively. At a calm time, identify triggers, helpful adult responses, and an improved plan for next time.
Consider / data to review

Repeated exclusion after conflict can strengthen avoidance, alienation, and adversarial identity.

Avoid assuming

Do not treat the behavior label as its explanation. Test function and context before selecting an intervention.
