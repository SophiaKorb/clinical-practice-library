School Access Toolkit · Clinical

Anxiety
Barrier → Support

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Clinical version for functional formulation, differential questions, implementable plan wording, and implementation review. Barrier numbers and support codes match the Plain Language version.

Clinical and educational formulation

  • Define the barrier: describe observable performance in a specific task, setting, and demand.
  • Generate competing hypotheses: consider skill, language, attention/executive function, sensory load, anxiety/mood, trauma/safety, environment, and reinforcement as relevant.
  • Use data to discriminate: compare conditions, task types, supports, settings, and response formats rather than relying on diagnosis alone.
  • Separate access from instruction: an accommodation can improve access without teaching a missing skill. When a skill deficit is present, specially designed instruction or intervention may also be needed.
  • Operationalize the support: specify provider, setting, trigger/frequency, student role, implementation evidence, outcome, and review date.
  • Review implementation before declaring failure: determine whether it happened as written, whether the student could use it, and whether it matched the hypothesized barrier.

Barrier 1

Worry blocks attention and working memory

Observable presentation: The student misses instruction, rereads, freezes, or cannot hold steps in mind.

1-AKeep directions visible. Provide short written steps and a model.
1-BReduce public pressure. Use private checks instead of public correction.
1-CPreview the lesson structure. Show what will happen, how long it will last, and how it ends.
1-DProvide brief grounding access. Use a taught, time-limited strategy with a clear return cue.
1-ECheck understanding after regulation. Ask for the first step once the student can process information.
Consider

Anxiety can consume working-memory and attentional capacity even when underlying attention is intact.

Data to review

Compare performance in low-threat vs evaluative settings and before/after predictable supports.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Do not automatically interpret anxious inattention as ADHD, or vice versa.

Barrier 2

Fear of mistakes blocks starting or finishing

Observable presentation: Erasing, reassurance seeking, perfectionism, or refusal consumes work time.

2-ADefine “done”. Provide a rubric, model, item limit, and stopping point.
2-BLimit repeated checking. Agree on a small number of checks, then prompt submission.
2-CChunk and submit sections. Use brief checkpoints rather than one high-stakes finish.
2-DGive neutral feedback. Correct the specific task without global praise or alarm.
2-ETeach flexible responding. Use explicit instruction and supported practice with tolerable mistakes.
Consider

Consider perfectionism, fear of negative evaluation, obsessive doubt, unclear criteria, and actual skill gaps.

Data to review

Measure start latency, checking/restarting, erasing, reassurance seeking, and completion patterns.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Support access without creating unlimited reassurance or escape from all uncertainty.

Barrier 3

Speaking or being observed blocks participation

Observable presentation: The student knows the answer but cannot respond in groups, presentations, or unfamiliar settings.

3-ABuild a participation ladder. Move from private or recorded response toward the required skill in planned steps.
3-BGive preview and preparation. Provide questions, roles, or presentation expectations in advance.
3-CAllow an initial alternate response. Use written, paired, AAC, or recorded response while working toward the goal.
3-DAvoid surprise public performance. Do not use cold calling or unannounced reading when it is not essential.
3-EMeasure progress by steps. Track the planned participation behavior, not whether anxiety disappears.
Consider

Consider social anxiety, performance anxiety, selective mutism, language demands, and prior adverse social experiences.

Data to review

Identify which forms of speaking/observation are hardest and whether alternative participation can bridge toward access.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Do not force public performance as an improvised exposure.

Barrier 4

Separation, arrival, or transitions delay entry

Observable presentation: The student cannot enter, remains with a caregiver, or misses class during transitions.

4-AUse a brief arrival routine. Name the staff greeter, location, sequence, and caregiver exit.
4-BProvide a visual transition plan. Show the next activity and the first easy action.
4-CUse a planned check-in. Schedule one short staff contact rather than unlimited reassurance.
4-DReinforce approach. Notice completion of the agreed step, not escape from it.
4-EReview attendance data. Track arrival time, time in instruction, and which supports improve entry.
Consider

Consider separation anxiety, school refusal, transition demands, sleep, transportation, and arrival routines.

Data to review

Track the specific point where entry breaks down and what helps the student cross it.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

A graded, coordinated arrival plan is often more useful than either full avoidance or abrupt removal of supports.

Barrier 5

Avoidance reduces access to learning

Observable presentation: The student escapes tests, classes, peers, or tasks and falls further behind.

5-AUse graded exposure with qualified planning. Create small, repeatable steps toward participation; do not improvise flooding.
5-BKeep a return expectation. A break has a time, location, support, and next step.
5-CAdjust excess demand. Reduce unrelated sensory, language, or workload barriers while retaining the core goal.
5-DPrevent accidental reward for escape. Offer support before escalation and return to a doable step.
5-ECoordinate with treatment carefully. With consent, align school supports with clinical recommendations without making school attendance depend on therapy.
Consider

Avoidance can reduce distress short term while shrinking access long term, but some avoidance is protective when a setting is unsafe or inaccessible.

Data to review

Distinguish fear-based avoidance from sensory overload, bullying, skill deficit, trauma, and medical problems.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Supports should increase workable access without using accommodation as a blanket exemption from all feared tasks.

Barrier 6

Panic or physical symptoms interrupt the day

Observable presentation: Nausea, dizziness, pain, rapid breathing, or fear leads to repeated nurse visits or leaving school.

6-ACreate a symptom response plan. List health red flags, regulation steps, adult roles, duration, and return criteria.
6-BAllow brief access to water or restroom. Define access while protecting instruction and dignity.
6-CUse one calm response. Staff avoid repeated questioning or escalating reassurance.
6-DProvide a low-stimulation recovery place. Name the location and maximum routine time before review.
6-ETrack time out of instruction. Review patterns and effectiveness, not only number of visits.
Consider

Rule out medical contributors when physical symptoms are new, severe, or atypical.

Data to review

Identify early warning signs, duration, recovery needs, and whether the student can communicate before symptoms peak.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Teach and plan for coping before crisis; avoid making the student earn help by escalating.

Barrier 7

Testing anxiety distorts performance

Observable presentation: Blanking, rushing, repeated checking, or physical symptoms hide learning.

7-AUse familiar testing conditions. Specify location, routine, and eligible tests.
7-BDefine extended time. State amount and whether it includes breaks.
7-CSegment the test. Give one section at a time with a brief reset.
7-DProvide a practice item. Confirm format comprehension before scoring begins.
7-EAvoid unnecessary time displays. Use private time cues if visible countdowns worsen performance.
Consider

Separate anxiety effects from knowledge gaps, slow processing, attention, and perfectionistic checking.

Data to review

Review practice vs test performance and timing/setting effects.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Testing accommodations should reduce access barriers while preserving what the test is intended to measure.

IEP, Section 504, and clinical documentation

A medical or psychological diagnosis can be relevant evidence, but it does not by itself determine IEP or Section 504 eligibility. Clinical documentation is most useful when it describes diagnosis, functional impairment, likely school impact, and why school-based evaluation or review is recommended.

Avoid promising eligibility, prescribing a particular legal plan, or using diagnosis as a substitute for school data.

Scope

Supports are planning options, not automatic entitlements. Recheck current official guidance when law, policy, or district procedure is material to a decision.
