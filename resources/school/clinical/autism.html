School Access Toolkit · Clinical

Autism
Barrier → Support

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Clinical version for functional formulation, differential questions, implementable plan wording, and implementation review. Barrier numbers and support codes match the Plain Language version.

Clinical and educational formulation

  • Define the barrier: describe observable performance in a specific task, setting, and demand.
  • Generate competing hypotheses: consider skill, language, attention/executive function, sensory load, anxiety/mood, trauma/safety, environment, and reinforcement as relevant.
  • Use data to discriminate: compare conditions, task types, supports, settings, and response formats rather than relying on diagnosis alone.
  • Separate access from instruction: an accommodation can improve access without teaching a missing skill. When a skill deficit is present, specially designed instruction or intervention may also be needed.
  • Operationalize the support: specify provider, setting, trigger/frequency, student role, implementation evidence, outcome, and review date.
  • Review implementation before declaring failure: determine whether it happened as written, whether the student could use it, and whether it matched the hypothesized barrier.

Barrier 1

Communication demands block access

Observable presentation: The student may not understand indirect language, may need more processing time, or may communicate without speech.

1-AUse direct language. State exactly what to do and avoid relying on hints, sarcasm, or implied rules.
1-BProvide visual communication. Pair speech with written, picture, modeled, or AAC-supported information.
1-CAllow processing time. Wait ___ seconds after a question or direction before repeating it.
1-DHonor AAC and other communication. Ensure the system is available across settings and accepted as the student’s voice.
1-ECheck meaning privately. Ask the student to show the first step rather than demand eye contact or public repetition.
Consider

Differentiate receptive, expressive, pragmatic, figurative, and AAC-related communication demands.

Data to review

Review whether the barrier changes with visual language, reduced ambiguity, processing time, or alternative communication.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Do not equate limited spoken response with lack of understanding.

Barrier 2

Uncertainty and change disrupt participation

Observable presentation: Unexpected changes, vague tasks, or transitions cause distress or loss of functioning.

2-AUse a current visual schedule. Keep the schedule visible and mark completed activities.
2-BPreview meaningful changes. Tell and show the student what will change, what stays the same, and what happens next.
2-CUse transition warnings. Give consistent cues at agreed time points before the transition.
2-DClarify task boundaries. Show the start, finish, amount, and expected product.
2-EProvide a change plan. Teach and use a brief routine for surprise changes.
Consider

Consider intolerance of uncertainty, cognitive flexibility, transition load, anxiety, and predictability of routines.

Data to review

Identify which kinds of change matter: timing, people, location, task, rules, or missing information.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

The goal is usable predictability and coping, not eliminating all change.

Barrier 3

Sensory load causes pain, overload, or loss of access

Observable presentation: Noise, lighting, touch, crowds, smell, or body-state needs interfere with learning.

3-AComplete a sensory-environment review. Identify triggers, early signs, and supports across settings.
3-BProvide sound or light adjustments. Name the tool and activities, such as ear protection or reduced glare.
3-COffer a lower-load location. Name where the student may work or recover and how return occurs.
3-DPermit regulation tools. List approved tools and keep them available without making access a reward.
3-EAdjust high-load events. Plan seating, entry, exit, and adult support for assemblies, lunch, drills, or transport.
Consider

Consider sensory intensity, cumulative load, pain, migraine, fatigue, and medical contributors.

Data to review

Map specific inputs and recovery time rather than assuming one sensory tool works everywhere.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Do not require aversive sensory exposure as an accommodation.

Barrier 4

Executive-function load blocks work

Observable presentation: Planning, starting, shifting, organizing, or holding steps in mind limits performance.

4-ABreak work into visible steps. Use a short numbered checklist with completion marks.
4-BProvide a concrete first step. Staff point to or model the first action.
4-CUse one organization system. Keep assignments, materials, and turn-in routines in named locations.
4-DSchedule check-ins. State who checks initiation or progress, at what times.
4-ETeach the skill. Use explicit instruction and guided practice if independence is an IEP goal.
Consider

Separate planning, initiation, sequencing, working memory, flexible problem solving, and task-switching demands.

Data to review

Check whether performance improves with visual structure, reduced steps, modeling, or clearer completion criteria.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Executive-function support is not the same as lowering expectations.

Barrier 5

Social demands interfere with learning or belonging

Observable presentation: Group work, hidden rules, teasing, or rapid peer interaction create confusion or exclusion.

5-AMake group roles explicit. Assign clear roles, turn rules, and a visible finished product.
5-BTeach hidden expectations directly. Explain the actual rule and context without shaming.
5-COffer a nonpublic participation route. Allow written, paired, or previewed participation when the target permits.
5-DIdentify a safe adult. Name whom the student can contact and how during peer problems.
5-EAddress the environment. Use adult supervision and anti-bullying response; do not place the whole burden on social-skills training.
Consider

Consider social-communication load, group-role ambiguity, bullying, masking, belonging, and fatigue.

Data to review

Ask what part of the social demand blocks access rather than targeting 'normal' social behavior.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Supports should improve participation and safety without requiring eye contact, masking, or forced peer interaction.

Barrier 6

Avoidance or shutdown blocks participation

Observable presentation: The student freezes, becomes unable to speak or work, hides, sleeps, or appears to refuse.

6-AIdentify early signs and triggers. Document what occurs before shutdown and which early support helps.
6-BReduce the barrier, not the learning goal. Change sensory, language, size, or uncertainty demands while preserving instruction.
6-CUse a low-language response plan. Staff reduce talking, show the next safe step, and allow the agreed recovery time.
6-DCreate a supported return. Name the adult, location, shortened re-entry task, and missed-work plan.
6-EDo not punish disability-related shutdown. Use data and team review to distinguish skill, overload, escape, and safety needs.
Consider

Differentiate overload/shutdown, anxiety, task escape, communication breakdown, fatigue, and learned avoidance.

Data to review

Look at antecedents, early signs, recovery time, and what changes when demands are reduced or clarified.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Avoid treating shutdown as willful noncompliance; also avoid assuming every avoidance episode has the same function.

Barrier 7

Leaving or attempted elopement creates safety risk

Observable presentation: The student runs, leaves supervised areas, hides, or moves toward unsafe locations.

7-ACreate an individualized safety plan. Name triggers, early signs, supervision, staff roles, communication, and reunification steps.
7-BIncrease preventive supervision. State the locations and times requiring proximity or handoff.
7-CSecure transitions. Use an adult-to-adult handoff and verified arrival for identified routes.
7-DTeach a safer replacement. Explicitly teach and practice a break, help, or exit request that works quickly.
7-EReview every incident. Record antecedent, location, response, injury/near miss, and plan changes; do not rely on restraint as routine prevention.
Consider

Use individualized safety assessment. Consider communication, supervision, environmental triggers, access to exits, body-signal awareness, and functional behavior data.

Data to review

Track where, when, and what precedes leaving or attempted leaving.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Safety planning should be preventive and nonpunitive; emergency procedures should not substitute for understanding the function.

Barrier 8

Testing or output format hides knowledge

Observable presentation: Speech, handwriting, reading, speed, or unfamiliar settings distort the intended measure.

8-AMatch response mode to the target. Allow typing, AAC, speech-to-text, pointing, or oral response when valid.
8-BPreview the format. Provide a sample item and routine before testing.
8-CUse a familiar low-load setting. Define location, staff, and sensory supports.
8-DSegment and pause. Present sections separately with defined breaks.
8-EReport supported and independent performance. Keep data clear about the conditions under which the skill was shown.
Consider

Separate motor/output, language, processing speed, sensory environment, executive load, and knowledge.

Data to review

Compare oral, typed, selected-response, visual, and extended-time performance when appropriate.

Avoid assuming / implementation note

Use the format that best measures the intended skill while preserving the academic target.

IEP, Section 504, and clinical documentation

A medical or psychological diagnosis can be relevant evidence, but it does not by itself determine IEP or Section 504 eligibility. Clinical documentation is most useful when it describes diagnosis, functional impairment, likely school impact, and why school-based evaluation or review is recommended.

Avoid promising eligibility, prescribing a particular legal plan, or using diagnosis as a substitute for school data.

Scope

Supports are planning options, not automatic entitlements. Recheck current official guidance when law, policy, or district procedure is material to a decision.
