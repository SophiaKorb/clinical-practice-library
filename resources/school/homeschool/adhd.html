Homeschool Barrier → Support

ADHD

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A problem-first guide for learning at home. Start with the task that is hard, not the diagnosis. Pick one or two barriers, change a small number of things, and watch what happens over time.

Barrier 1

Directions do not turn into action

What this may look like at home: The child appears to understand a direction but starts the wrong thing, stalls, or loses the instruction before acting.

Try first

  • Use one or two steps at a time and make them visible.
  • Ask the child to show the first action rather than repeat the whole direction.
  • Reduce competing talk and wait long enough for processing.

Track: Compare spoken-only directions with written/visual directions and note how much prompting is needed.

Consider referral when

Seek evaluation when missed directions occur across settings or persist despite clear visual supports, especially if receptive language, hearing, sleep, or broader developmental concerns are possible.

Barrier 2

Steps, materials, or routines are forgotten

What this may look like at home: The child repeatedly loses the next step, forgets where materials belong, or needs the parent to hold the whole routine in mind.

Try first

  • Use one consistent checklist for recurring routines.
  • Keep materials in fixed, labeled places.
  • Move memory out of the child’s head with visible assignment and turn-in systems.

Track: Record which part is lost: during the task, after a delay, or during transitions.

Consider referral when

Consider ADHD/learning evaluation when working memory and organization problems are broad and impair independence; consider OT when routines, motor organization, or daily occupations are also affected.

Barrier 3

Starting or finishing work is hard

What this may look like at home: The child knows the content but cannot begin, works only with an adult beside them, or abandons work before completion.

Try first

  • Shrink the first step until it is obvious and doable.
  • Use short work periods with planned return points.
  • Define what “finished” looks like before starting.

Track: Record latency to start, adult prompting, work duration, and whether the barrier changes with task size or interest.

Consider referral when

Refer when initiation problems are severe across preferred and nonpreferred tasks, or when anxiety, depression, sleep, language, learning, or motor demands may be contributing.

Barrier 4

Time and long projects are hard to manage

What this may look like at home: Deadlines appear suddenly, time is consistently underestimated, or large projects happen only under crisis-level urgency.

Try first

  • Make time visible with written time markers or a timer if helpful.
  • Break projects into dated checkpoints.
  • Use a project map showing steps, materials, and the next review point.

Track: Track estimated versus actual time and whether interim deadlines improve completion.

Consider referral when

Consider ADHD/executive-function evaluation when the pattern is persistent and broad. If time-management failure reflects reading, writing, or math difficulty, evaluate the underlying skill too.

Barrier 5

Writing may hide what the child knows

What this may look like at home: Oral explanations are much stronger than written work, handwriting is slow or painful, or mechanics consume the child’s attention.

Try first

  • Use typing or speech-to-text when handwriting is not the learning target.
  • Separate content from mechanics when scoring practice work.
  • Provide planning templates and reduce copying.

Track: Compare oral, typed, dictated, and handwritten performance.

Consider referral when

OT is appropriate when handwriting is painful, unusually slow, fatiguing, or tied to fine-motor/visual-motor problems. Consider learning or language evaluation when written expression remains weak even with motor demands reduced.

Barrier 6

Noise, movement, clutter, or sensory load gets in the way

What this may look like at home: The child works much better in a quiet/low-clutter setting or becomes overloaded by sound, light, touch, movement, or crowded environments.

Try first

  • Change one sensory variable at a time.
  • Offer a lower-load workspace and allow movement that improves attention.
  • Keep regulation tools available without turning them into rewards.

Track: Note specific triggers and whether the change improves access rather than merely looking calmer.

Consider referral when

Consider OT when sensory-motor or regulation needs interfere with learning, self-care, community participation, or daily routines. Pediatric evaluation is warranted for pain, sleep, hearing, or medical contributors.

Barrier 7

Movement, waiting, changes, or big feelings interrupt learning

What this may look like at home: The child leaves, argues, becomes silly or explosive, or cannot return after a transition or wait.

Try first

  • Build movement in before predictable overload.
  • Preview changes and reduce empty waiting.
  • Teach a quick break/help signal and a clear return routine.

Track: Record what happened immediately before escalation and what preventive support changes the outcome.

Consider referral when

Consider ADHD/mental-health evaluation when dysregulation is severe, frequent, or unsafe. OT may help when motor/sensory regulation is a major functional driver.

Barrier 8

Tests or performance checks do not show what the child knows

What this may look like at home: The child rushes, blanks, loses place, or performs far below normal learning during timed or dense assessments.

Try first

  • Use a quieter setting and smaller sections.
  • Allow planned breaks and accessible response methods when the skill being measured permits.
  • Use practice items so the format itself is not novel.

Track: Compare performance across format, timing, setting, and response mode.

Consider referral when

Seek comprehensive learning/attention evaluation if testing inconsistently underestimates skills or there is a marked gap between learning and demonstration.

Who to call first

Start with the pediatrician or primary-care clinician for an ADHD evaluation or medical contributors. A child psychologist or neuropsychologist can help with complex differential questions. Use OT for handwriting, fine-motor, sensory-motor, self-care, or functional-routine concerns. Use an SLP when language comprehension, expression, or communication appears to be part of the barrier.

How to make the referral

Use the shared guide 00 Getting Outside Help When You Homeschool – Referral Guide. Bring a short concern summary, representative work samples, what you have already tried, and specific questions you want the evaluator to answer. If insurance is involved, ask whether a physician referral or prior authorization is required.

Public evaluation while homeschooling

A private clinical evaluation is not the only route. Arizona explicitly includes homeschool students in Child Find. California recognizes home schools as private schools for IDEA equitable-services purposes. Use the Arizona or California homeschool guide in this library for the current state-specific pathway and service rules.

Important boundary

Do not force a child through pain, panic, unsafe behavior, or severe dysregulation in order to “collect more data.” Urgent medical or safety concerns come before educational troubleshooting.

Scope

This guide is educational information and a planning aid, not a diagnosis or individualized treatment plan.
