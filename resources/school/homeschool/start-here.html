Homeschool Barrier-to-Support Guides

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A problem-first toolkit for learning at home.

What these guides are for

Homeschooling gives families unusual flexibility, but flexibility does not erase disability-related barriers. These guides help families identify the actual learning or participation problem, adjust instruction and the environment, track whether the change helped, and recognize when home strategies are not enough.

Use the guides in four steps

  1. Describe the barrier in observable terms. What task becomes hard, where does it break down, and what changes when you alter the demand?
  2. Try a small number of supports. Do not change ten things at once.
  3. Track response. Keep work samples, brief notes, timing, accuracy, distress, independence, and what level of prompting was needed.
  4. Escalate when needed. Persistent, severe, painful, unsafe, or developmentally unexpected problems may warrant professional evaluation.

A note about professional referrals

Each guide includes condition-specific referral triggers. Use Getting Outside Help When You Homeschool for practical instructions on contacting OT, SLP, psychology/neuropsychology, pediatrics, mental-health clinicians, reading specialists, and other professionals.

Public evaluation while homeschooling

Homeschool students can still have Child Find rights, but the exact pathway and service rules depend on state law. Arizona explicitly includes homeschool students in Child Find. California recognizes home schools as private schools for IDEA equitable-services purposes. Use the Arizona and California homeschool guides in this library for current state-specific procedures.

Scope

These guides are educational planning tools, not diagnosis or individualized medical advice. A homeschool schedule can be adjusted immediately, but ongoing difficulty still deserves the same careful differential thinking expected in any other educational setting.
