Homeschool Barrier → Support

Dysgraphia

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A problem-first guide for learning at home. Start with the task that is hard, not the diagnosis. Pick one or two barriers, change a small number of things, and watch what happens over time.

Barrier 1

Handwriting is slow, tiring, or painful

What this may look like at home: The child grips tightly, tires quickly, complains of hand/arm pain, or writes far less than they can say.

Try first

  • Shorten handwriting demands while preserving content.
  • Try ergonomic pencil/desk position and brief practice.
  • Allow typing or speech-to-text for longer content work.

Track: Record pain, duration, speed, legibility, and fatigue.

Consider referral when

OT referral is strongly indicated when handwriting is painful, unusually effortful, or associated with fine-motor/visual-motor concerns. Medical evaluation is warranted for persistent pain, weakness, or neurologic symptoms.

Barrier 2

Letters, spacing, or page organization reduce legibility

What this may look like at home: Letter size, spacing, alignment, reversals, or line use make writing difficult to read.

Try first

  • Use appropriate lined or graph paper and visual spacing cues.
  • Teach one formation pattern at a time.
  • Increase writing space and reduce copying.

Track: Keep dated handwriting samples to see whether legibility changes with support.

Consider referral when

OT can assess fine-motor, visual-motor, motor-planning, posture, and functional handwriting factors.

Barrier 3

Getting words onto the page hides ideas

What this may look like at home: The child has rich oral ideas but written sentences are short, disorganized, or incomplete.

Try first

  • Separate idea generation from transcription.
  • Use oral rehearsal, graphic organizers, typing, or dictation.
  • Teach sentence/paragraph structure explicitly.

Track: Compare oral language, dictated writing, typed writing, and handwriting.

Consider referral when

A learning evaluator can assess written-expression disorder. SLP is useful when language formulation, grammar, narrative, or word retrieval is also weak.

Barrier 4

Copying and note taking cause lost information

What this may look like at home: The child falls behind, loses place, copies inaccurately, or cannot listen and write at the same time.

Try first

  • Provide source material instead of requiring copying.
  • Use templates or guided notes.
  • Pause instruction before note taking when needed.

Track: Compare copying with direct composition and with provided notes.

Consider referral when

OT may help with visual-motor or motor demands; evaluate attention, language, or visual access when copying remains disproportionately difficult.

Barrier 5

Organizing written ideas is hard

What this may look like at home: The child has facts but cannot sequence them into a coherent paragraph, essay, or response.

Try first

  • Use visual planning maps and sentence frames.
  • Build from oral explanation to written outline to draft.
  • Teach revision as separate steps rather than all at once.

Track: Track organization independent of spelling/handwriting.

Consider referral when

Consider SLP or learning evaluation when language organization and written expression are persistently weak.

Barrier 6

Written tests hide what the child knows

What this may look like at home: Handwriting speed, pain, spelling, or written formulation lowers scores despite good understanding.

Try first

  • Allow typing, speech-to-text, oral response, or extra time when the target permits.
  • Reduce copying and unnecessary written repetition.
  • Score content separately from mechanics when appropriate.

Track: Compare the same knowledge across response modes.

Consider referral when

Formal evaluation is useful when accommodations are repeatedly needed or diagnostic clarification is important.

Who to call first

OT is often the first referral when handwriting, fine-motor, visual-motor, posture, motor planning, or pain is central. A psychologist/neuropsychologist/educational evaluator is appropriate for a broader written-expression or learning-disorder question. SLP is important when language formulation, narrative, grammar, or word retrieval contributes. Ask specifically whether the professional evaluates handwriting versus written expression, because those are not the same problem.

How to make the referral

Use the shared guide 00 Getting Outside Help When You Homeschool – Referral Guide. Bring a short concern summary, representative work samples, what you have already tried, and specific questions you want the evaluator to answer. If insurance is involved, ask whether a physician referral or prior authorization is required.

Public evaluation while homeschooling

A private clinical evaluation is not the only route. Arizona explicitly includes homeschool students in Child Find. California recognizes home schools as private schools for IDEA equitable-services purposes. Use the Arizona or California homeschool guide in this library for the current state-specific pathway and service rules.

Important boundary

Do not force a child through pain, panic, unsafe behavior, or severe dysregulation in order to “collect more data.” Urgent medical or safety concerns come before educational troubleshooting.

Scope

This guide is educational information and a planning aid, not a diagnosis or individualized treatment plan.
