Homeschool Barrier → Support

Dyscalculia

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A problem-first guide for learning at home. Start with the task that is hard, not the diagnosis. Pick one or two barriers, change a small number of things, and watch what happens over time.

Barrier 1

Number size and quantity are not stable

What this may look like at home: The child struggles to compare amounts, place numbers on a number line, or connect symbols to quantity.

Try first

  • Use concrete objects and visual number lines before abstract symbols.
  • Teach magnitude relationships directly.
  • Return to stable quantities rather than increasing worksheet volume.

Track: Record which number ranges and representations are secure.

Consider referral when

Seek math-learning evaluation when number sense remains markedly weak despite explicit instruction.

Barrier 2

Math facts are hard to recall

What this may look like at home: Basic facts require repeated counting or are forgotten from day to day.

Try first

  • Build meaning and strategies before speed.
  • Use fact families, patterns, and visual models.
  • Allow a fact chart when recall is not the target.

Track: Separate conceptual understanding from retrieval speed.

Consider referral when

Evaluation is warranted when fact retrieval is one part of a broader persistent math-learning pattern.

Barrier 3

Steps in math problems get mixed up

What this may look like at home: The child understands individual steps but loses the sequence or applies the wrong procedure.

Try first

  • Use a visible step checklist.
  • Teach one procedure at a time and compare similar procedures explicitly.
  • Have the child explain why each step is used.

Track: Note whether errors are conceptual, memory-based, or procedural.

Consider referral when

Psychological/educational evaluation can help distinguish math disability from working-memory, attention, or language barriers.

Barrier 4

Holding math information in mind causes errors

What this may look like at home: Mental calculation collapses, partial results are lost, or multi-step problems are much harder than single steps.

Try first

  • Write intermediate values down.
  • Reduce unnecessary mental-memory demands.
  • Use worked examples and external scratch space.

Track: Compare performance with and without external memory supports.

Consider referral when

Consider evaluation for ADHD/executive-function or learning disorders when working-memory demands impair math broadly.

Barrier 5

Math language and word problems are confusing

What this may look like at home: The child can calculate but misreads comparison terms, sequence words, or problem language.

Try first

  • Teach math vocabulary explicitly.
  • Rewrite dense word problems in simpler language without changing the math.
  • Use diagrams to map relationships.

Track: Compare pure computation with language-heavy math.

Consider referral when

SLP evaluation may be useful when language comprehension problems extend beyond math. A learning evaluator can assess math reasoning.

Barrier 6

Spacing and visual layout cause errors

What this may look like at home: Numbers drift out of columns, place value is lost, or crowded pages cause mistakes.

Try first

  • Use graph paper or lined columns.
  • Show fewer problems at a time.
  • Increase workspace and align place value visually.

Track: Compare conceptual accuracy with layout supports.

Consider referral when

OT may help when visual-motor, handwriting, fine-motor, or spatial organization significantly affects written math. Vision care is appropriate for genuine visual acuity/eye-health concerns.

Barrier 7

Math anxiety and avoidance reduce practice

What this may look like at home: The child freezes, refuses, rushes, or becomes distressed before difficult math starts.

Try first

  • Reduce time pressure and public performance.
  • Use short successful practice before harder work.
  • Separate error correction from shame or urgency.

Track: Track anxiety level and whether avoidance is expanding.

Consider referral when

Mental-health support is warranted when anxiety becomes severe or generalized. Learning evaluation is important when anxiety may be secondary to an unrecognized math disability.

Barrier 8

Tests may hide math knowledge

What this may look like at home: Timed or dense assessments produce much worse performance than guided work.

Try first

  • Use untimed/extended conditions when speed is not the target.
  • Provide sufficient scratch space and accessible format.
  • Allow tools such as a calculator when calculation fluency is not being measured.

Track: Compare conceptual performance across testing conditions.

Consider referral when

Seek formal evaluation if the family needs diagnostic clarification, documentation, or a more precise intervention plan.

Who to call first

Look for a psychologist, neuropsychologist, educational evaluator, or learning specialist with specific experience in mathematical learning disabilities. A skilled math interventionist can provide targeted instruction. SLP is relevant when language is a major contributor. OT is appropriate when fine-motor, visual-motor, spatial layout, or functional tool-use problems are materially affecting math output.

How to make the referral

Use the shared guide 00 Getting Outside Help When You Homeschool – Referral Guide. Bring a short concern summary, representative work samples, what you have already tried, and specific questions you want the evaluator to answer. If insurance is involved, ask whether a physician referral or prior authorization is required.

Public evaluation while homeschooling

A private clinical evaluation is not the only route. Arizona explicitly includes homeschool students in Child Find. California recognizes home schools as private schools for IDEA equitable-services purposes. Use the Arizona or California homeschool guide in this library for the current state-specific pathway and service rules.

Important boundary

Do not force a child through pain, panic, unsafe behavior, or severe dysregulation in order to “collect more data.” Urgent medical or safety concerns come before educational troubleshooting.

Scope

This guide is educational information and a planning aid, not a diagnosis or individualized treatment plan.
