Homeschool Barrier → Support

Anxiety

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A problem-first guide for learning at home. Start with the task that is hard, not the diagnosis. Pick one or two barriers, change a small number of things, and watch what happens over time.

Barrier 1

Worry makes it hard to focus or remember

What this may look like at home: The child rereads, loses track, asks repeated reassurance questions, or cannot hold steps in mind when worried.

Try first

  • Reduce unnecessary uncertainty and keep directions visible.
  • Use brief grounding or regulation, then return to the task.
  • Ask what the child is predicting rather than adding repeated reassurance.

Track: Record the worry trigger, time lost, reassurance cycles, and whether learning returns after regulation.

Consider referral when

Seek mental-health evaluation when anxiety is frequent, escalating, or interferes with learning, sleep, friendships, separation, or normal activities.

Barrier 2

Fear of mistakes makes it hard to start or finish

What this may look like at home: Perfectionism, erasing, redoing, checking, or refusal consumes more time than the work itself.

Try first

  • Define “done” before starting.
  • Limit checking and use planned submission points.
  • Practice tolerable mistakes in small steps.

Track: Measure time spent checking/redoing and the ability to submit imperfect work.

Consider referral when

CBT-oriented therapy can help when perfectionism or obsessive worry significantly limits functioning. Evaluate for OCD when repetitive thoughts/rituals are prominent.

Barrier 3

Speaking or being watched makes participation hard

What this may look like at home: The child can speak/perform at home but freezes in co-ops, classes, community activities, presentations, or unfamiliar settings.

Try first

  • Use a gradual participation ladder.
  • Preview roles and allow lower-pressure response routes initially.
  • Avoid surprise public performance.

Track: Track the smallest successful participation step and avoid measuring success only by absence of anxiety.

Consider referral when

Refer to a mental-health clinician for social anxiety/selective mutism concerns. An SLP may be part of selective-mutism or communication assessment, especially when language or speech concerns also exist.

Barrier 4

Separation, leaving home, or transitions are hard

What this may look like at home: Activities outside the home, drop-off, classes, appointments, or changes in routine become difficult to enter or sustain.

Try first

  • Use a short, predictable transition routine.
  • Preview the first easy action after arrival.
  • Avoid endless reassurance and long departures.

Track: Track arrival latency, time participating, and which supports improve entry.

Consider referral when

Seek mental-health evaluation when separation anxiety or avoidance limits age-appropriate activities or family functioning.

Barrier 5

Avoidance shrinks learning and life

What this may look like at home: Subjects, community activities, peers, appointments, or new experiences are gradually dropped because anxiety falls when they are avoided.

Try first

  • Keep demands small enough to approach rather than escape.
  • Use planned, repeatable steps toward the activity.
  • Reduce unrelated sensory or workload barriers while preserving the core approach goal.

Track: Track what the child is no longer doing and whether the world is becoming smaller.

Consider referral when

Refer when avoidance is expanding, the child cannot engage in normal activities, or the family is reorganizing life around anxiety.

Barrier 6

Panic or physical symptoms interrupt learning

What this may look like at home: Nausea, dizziness, pain, rapid breathing, trembling, or fear stops lessons or causes repeated retreat to bed/bathroom/parent.

Try first

  • Use a consistent symptom-response plan.
  • Rule out medical concerns rather than assuming anxiety.
  • Use one calm response and a clear return path when medically safe.

Track: Record symptom pattern, triggers, duration, and medical findings.

Consider referral when

Start with the pediatrician when symptoms are new, severe, unexplained, or physical red flags are present. Mental-health treatment is warranted when panic or somatic anxiety is recurrent and impairing.

Barrier 7

Tests or performance checks trigger anxiety

What this may look like at home: The child blanks, rushes, repeatedly checks, or performs far below practice during evaluation.

Try first

  • Use familiar format and setting.
  • Segment the assessment and use private time cues.
  • Confirm instructions with a sample item.

Track: Compare learning during instruction with performance under different assessment conditions.

Consider referral when

Consider comprehensive evaluation when anxiety and possible learning/attention disorders are difficult to disentangle.

Who to call first

A child psychologist or therapist with evidence-based anxiety treatment experience is usually the best first mental-health referral. The pediatrician can rule out medical contributors and coordinate medication/psychiatry when needed. Use SLP when communication or selective-mutism questions are present, and OT when sensory or functional regulation barriers are substantial.

How to make the referral

Use the shared guide 00 Getting Outside Help When You Homeschool – Referral Guide. Bring a short concern summary, representative work samples, what you have already tried, and specific questions you want the evaluator to answer. If insurance is involved, ask whether a physician referral or prior authorization is required.

Public evaluation while homeschooling

A private clinical evaluation is not the only route. Arizona explicitly includes homeschool students in Child Find. California recognizes home schools as private schools for IDEA equitable-services purposes. Use the Arizona or California homeschool guide in this library for the current state-specific pathway and service rules.

Important boundary

Do not force a child through pain, panic, unsafe behavior, or severe dysregulation in order to “collect more data.” Urgent medical or safety concerns come before educational troubleshooting.

Scope

This guide is educational information and a planning aid, not a diagnosis or individualized treatment plan.
