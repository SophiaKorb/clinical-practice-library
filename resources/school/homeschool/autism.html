Homeschool Barrier → Support

Autism

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A problem-first guide for learning at home. Start with the task that is hard, not the diagnosis. Pick one or two barriers, change a small number of things, and watch what happens over time.

Barrier 1

Communication makes learning harder to access

What this may look like at home: The child may miss indirect language, need extra processing time, communicate with speech/AAC/gesture, or understand more than they can express quickly.

Try first

  • Use direct language and make important information visible.
  • Accept AAC, gesture, writing, pointing, or other reliable communication.
  • Pause after questions and reduce repeated verbal prompting.

Track: Note which communication mode produces the clearest understanding and expression.

Consider referral when

Refer to an SLP when receptive, expressive, social-pragmatic, speech, or AAC needs limit participation. Audiology is appropriate when hearing is uncertain.

Barrier 2

Changes and uncertainty make participation harder

What this may look like at home: Unexpected schedule shifts, vague tasks, new places, or unclear endings lead to distress, freezing, or refusal.

Try first

  • Preview meaningful changes and show what stays the same.
  • Show start, finish, amount, and next step.
  • Practice a simple change plan during calm periods.

Track: Record which kinds of uncertainty matter and how much preview is needed.

Consider referral when

Consider autism/developmental evaluation if rigidity and distress are broad and longstanding. Mental-health referral is useful when anxiety becomes a major driver.

Barrier 3

Sensory input causes pain or overload

What this may look like at home: Sound, lighting, touch, movement, smell, clothing, or body-state sensations interfere with learning or recovery.

Try first

  • Reduce the specific sensory load rather than assuming behavior is oppositional.
  • Offer regulation options the child already knows.
  • Schedule demanding work when the child is physically regulated.

Track: Identify triggers, early signs, recovery time, and supports that reliably help.

Consider referral when

OT is appropriate when sensory-motor differences interfere with learning, self-care, sleep routines, community participation, or daily occupations. Medical review is appropriate for pain, sleep, GI, or other physical concerns.

Barrier 4

Planning and task demands make work harder

What this may look like at home: The child understands the concept but gets lost in multistep work, starting, switching, or organizing materials.

Try first

  • Break work into visible steps.
  • Give a concrete first action and clear stopping point.
  • Use one consistent organization system.

Track: Separate concept knowledge from planning/organization demands.

Consider referral when

Consider OT or psychology/neuropsychology when executive-function needs are severe or broad; evaluate for ADHD or learning disorders when indicated.

Barrier 5

Social demands interfere with learning or belonging

What this may look like at home: Group classes, co-ops, rapid conversation, teasing, hidden rules, or friendship problems create confusion or exclusion.

Try first

  • Make roles and expectations explicit.
  • Offer lower-pressure participation routes.
  • Address bullying and unsafe environments directly rather than treating the child as the problem.

Track: Ask the child which social contexts feel meaningful, confusing, exhausting, or unsafe.

Consider referral when

Refer to an SLP for social-communication evaluation when appropriate, and to a therapist when bullying, trauma, anxiety, exploitation risk, or relationship safety is affecting functioning.

Barrier 6

Shutdown or avoidance blocks participation

What this may look like at home: The child freezes, hides, cannot speak, sleeps, leaves the task, or appears to refuse when overloaded.

Try first

  • Reduce language and demand during shutdown.
  • Use a known recovery space and supported return.
  • Look for pain, sensory overload, uncertainty, language, or task mismatch.

Track: Record early signs and the conditions that precede shutdown.

Consider referral when

Professional assessment is warranted when shutdown is frequent, prolonged, associated with skill loss, severe anxiety, depression, catatonia-like symptoms, or major loss of daily functioning.

Barrier 7

Leaving the area creates a safety risk

What this may look like at home: The child bolts, wanders, hides, or moves toward unsafe locations during distress or high interest.

Try first

  • Use an individualized safety plan with supervision and handoff rules.
  • Teach a fast break/help/exit request that actually works.
  • Review every incident and change prevention, not only consequences.

Track: Record location, trigger, early signs, adult response, near misses, and what prevented recurrence.

Consider referral when

Seek professional help promptly when elopement creates injury risk. Consider behavioral/developmental, OT, and medical contributors; use emergency planning when immediate safety cannot be maintained.

Barrier 8

Standard response formats hide knowledge

What this may look like at home: Speech, handwriting, speed, unfamiliar settings, or sensory load obscure what the child knows.

Try first

  • Match response mode to the learning target.
  • Preview format and allow familiar supports.
  • Report the conditions under which the child demonstrated the skill.

Track: Compare performance across spoken, written, typed, AAC, demonstration, and low-load settings.

Consider referral when

Refer for speech/language, motor/OT, or learning evaluation when the response-mode gap is persistent and functionally significant.

Who to call first

For diagnostic clarification, consider a psychologist, developmental-behavioral pediatrician, neuropsychologist, or other autism-experienced clinician. Use SLP for communication/AAC/language, OT for sensory-motor/self-care/handwriting participation, and mental-health care for anxiety, depression, trauma, or relationship safety. Use urgent safety services when elopement or self-injury cannot be managed safely.

How to make the referral

Use the shared guide 00 Getting Outside Help When You Homeschool – Referral Guide. Bring a short concern summary, representative work samples, what you have already tried, and specific questions you want the evaluator to answer. If insurance is involved, ask whether a physician referral or prior authorization is required.

Public evaluation while homeschooling

A private clinical evaluation is not the only route. Arizona explicitly includes homeschool students in Child Find. California recognizes home schools as private schools for IDEA equitable-services purposes. Use the Arizona or California homeschool guide in this library for the current state-specific pathway and service rules.

Important boundary

Do not force a child through pain, panic, unsafe behavior, or severe dysregulation in order to “collect more data.” Urgent medical or safety concerns come before educational troubleshooting.

Scope

This guide is educational information and a planning aid, not a diagnosis or individualized treatment plan.
