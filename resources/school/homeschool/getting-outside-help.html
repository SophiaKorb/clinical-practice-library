Homeschool · Referral Guide

Getting Outside Help When You Homeschool

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

How to decide when to refer, who to call, and what to ask for.

Start with function, not a professional label

A useful referral starts with a concrete concern such as “handwriting is painful after five minutes,” “reading has not improved after structured practice,” “panic stops math three times a week,” or “the child cannot follow spoken directions unless they are written.” That description helps the receiving professional decide whether they are the right fit.

When home support is no longer enough

  • The problem persists despite several weeks of well-matched instruction or environmental support.
  • The child is making little progress compared with their own prior learning.
  • Pain, fatigue, sensory distress, communication difficulty, or motor problems interfere with daily learning.
  • A skill loss or major change appears suddenly.
  • The concern affects more than academics, such as sleep, self-care, friendships, mobility, feeding, safety, or family functioning.
  • The parent has to provide constant one-to-one rescue for the child to participate.
  • The child or parent is increasingly avoiding the subject because the experience has become distressing.
  • There are safety concerns, self-harm concerns, severe aggression, elopement, or inability to maintain basic daily functioning.

Occupational Therapy (OT)

Consider OT when the main barrier involves handwriting, fine-motor control, visual-motor demands, motor planning, sensory access, self-care, positioning, tool use, or participation in everyday routines. OT may also help when executive-function or regulation problems are strongly tied to daily occupations and routines.

Speech-Language Pathology (SLP)

Consider an SLP when the barrier involves understanding language, expressing ideas, word retrieval, speech clarity, narrative organization, social communication, phonological awareness, communication-device use, selective mutism in coordination with mental-health care, or feeding/swallowing concerns.

Psychologist, neuropsychologist, or educational evaluator

Consider comprehensive evaluation when the pattern is unclear, several domains are involved, a specific learning disorder is suspected, progress is unexpectedly slow, or the family needs diagnostic clarification and an instructional roadmap.

Pediatrician or primary care clinician

Start with the pediatrician when there may be a medical contributor, medication issue, ADHD, sleep problem, pain, fatigue, developmental concern, regression, hearing/vision concern, or need for specialty referral.

Mental-health clinician

Consider a qualified child mental-health professional when anxiety, depression, trauma symptoms, severe irritability, persistent aggression, self-harm thoughts, major avoidance, or family conflict are interfering with daily life or learning.

Reading and dyslexia specialists

A structured-literacy specialist or dyslexia therapist can provide targeted instruction. For diagnostic questions, consider an appropriately trained psychologist, educational evaluator, SLP, or other professional with dyslexia expertise.

Math-learning specialist

For persistent number sense, calculation, or mathematical reasoning problems, seek an evaluator with specific experience in math learning disabilities and a tutor/interventionist who can teach concepts explicitly and systematically.

Hearing and vision

Refer for audiology when hearing may be contributing to language or learning difficulties. Refer for routine eye care when visual acuity or eye-health concerns are present.

Public Child Find while homeschooling

Arizona explicitly includes homeschool students in Child Find. California recognizes home schools as private schools for IDEA equitable-services purposes. Federal IDEA rules determine how Child Find applies based on state law.

What to bring to an evaluation

  • A one-page concern summary.
  • Relevant developmental and medical history.
  • Two or three representative work samples.
  • Curricula/interventions already tried and for how long.
  • Notes about what makes the task easier or harder.
  • Prior evaluations or therapy records when relevant.
  • A short list of questions you want the evaluation to answer.

Urgent concerns

Do not wait for an educational referral process when there is immediate danger, serious injury, suicidal intent, severe self-harm, acute psychosis, suspected abuse, or another emergency. Use emergency or crisis services appropriate to the situation.
