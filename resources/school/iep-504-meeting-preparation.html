School Access · Meeting Preparation

Preparing for an IEP or 504 Meeting

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A parent and recovery-coach guide for arriving with priorities, examples, questions, and a clear follow-up plan.

Start With Access, Not Labels

Describe what the student has difficulty accessing, doing, communicating, tolerating, or showing at school. A diagnosis can provide context, but supports should connect to functional educational needs.

Before the Meeting

  • Write down three to five priority concerns.
  • For each concern, record a specific example, when it happens, what has been tried, and what better access would look like.
  • Gather relevant evaluations, progress data, attendance information, work samples, disciplinary records, and communication from school.
  • Ask the student what helps, what makes school harder, and what they want adults to understand.
  • Send important questions in writing before the meeting when possible.

Turn Complaints Into Team Questions

Instead of: “They are lazy.”
Ask: “What happens between receiving the assignment and beginning it?”

Instead of: “They refuse transitions.”
Ask: “Which transitions are difficult, what warning is provided, and what support changes the outcome?”

Instead of: “They know the work but will not do it.”
Ask: “How are initiation, working memory, writing, regulation, and task length affecting performance?”

Choose the Three Things That Matter Most

Meetings can generate a long list of concerns. Before the meeting, identify the three changes that would make the biggest difference in access, safety, participation, or progress. Keep lower-priority items visible without letting them crowd out the main decisions.

During the Meeting

  • Ask what data support the team's conclusion.
  • Ask who will provide each support, where, how often, and how progress will be measured.
  • Request plain-language explanations for unfamiliar terms.
  • Take notes. It is reasonable to ask for time to review documents rather than making every decision immediately.
  • Keep the student's strengths, preferences, culture, communication, and disability access in view.

Recovery-Coach Role

Help the parent organize concerns and documents, practice questions, distinguish observations from conclusions, and track follow-up. Do not present yourself as the parent's attorney or make promises about eligibility or outcomes.

Before the Meeting Ends

  • Ask what the team has actually decided, not only what was discussed.
  • Clarify any wording that could be interpreted several ways.
  • Identify who owns each next step and the expected timeline.
  • Write down unresolved questions rather than allowing them to disappear into the meeting.

After the Meeting

  • Compare the written plan with what was discussed.
  • List action items, responsible people, and follow-up dates.
  • Monitor whether the support is actually usable and whether it improves access, participation, safety, or progress.

Important Limitation

This guide provides general educational advocacy support, not individualized legal advice. IDEA, Section 504, state procedures, district practices, eligibility standards, and dispute options require current, case-specific review.
