School Access · Planning

From School Barrier to Support

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A planning guide for parents, recovery coaches, and clinicians

School supports work best when the conversation starts with access: What is the student having difficulty doing, under what conditions, and what change could reduce that barrier? A diagnosis can provide context, but the useful planning unit is the functional barrier.

1. Describe the Barrier Without Blame

Instead of: “He is unmotivated.”
Try: “He starts independent written work only after repeated adult prompting and often does not finish within the class period.”

Instead of: “She refuses transitions.”
Try: “When an activity ends without advance notice, she may remain in place, become distressed, or need extended time before beginning the next activity.”

My observable concern: ____________________________________
When/where it happens: ____________________________________
What makes it better or worse: ______________________________

2. Ask What Demand Is Underneath

  • Attention or task initiation
  • Working memory or multi-step directions
  • Reading, writing, math, or language demands
  • Processing speed
  • Motor or handwriting demands
  • Sensory load
  • Transitions and predictability
  • Communication or social ambiguity
  • Anxiety or uncertainty
  • Fatigue, health, pain, or medication effects
  • Organization and materials
  • Emotional regulation or recovery time

Likely barrier(s) to investigate: ______________________________

3. Match Support to the Barrier

Misses multi-step oral directions: written or visual steps, chunking, check for understanding.

Slow written output: reduced copying, keyboarding, alternative response format, or additional time when it addresses the actual barrier.

Overload in noisy settings: quieter work location, hearing protection when appropriate, seating/environment changes, planned recovery access.

Difficulty starting independent work: first-step cue, visual start routine, brief adult check-in, task chunking.

Transition difficulty: preview, visual schedule, useful warnings, clear stopping point, first-step support.

Anxiety about asking for help: private help signal, scheduled check-in, written question option.

4. Make the Plan Observable

Who will provide the support? _______________________________
When/where? _____________________________________________
How often? _______________________________________________
How will the student know how to use it? ______________________
How will the team know whether it is helping? __________________

5. Bring Data That Answers a Question

Useful information can include work samples, attendance patterns, grades over time, observations across settings, examples of directions or tasks that break down, student report, teacher report, and response to supports already tried. More data is not automatically better. Bring information that helps the team understand access.

Questions for the School Team

  • What skill or access barrier do you think is driving this difficulty?
  • Does it occur across classes or mainly under certain demands?
  • What has already been tried, how consistently, and what happened?
  • What support could be implemented consistently enough to evaluate?
  • How will progress or access be measured?
  • What does the student say helps?

Important Limits

This planner is for organizing functional concerns, not determining legal eligibility. IEP and Section 504 decisions depend on applicable law, evaluation data, and the student's individual circumstances. Do not use a generic accommodation list as a substitute for individualized assessment.
