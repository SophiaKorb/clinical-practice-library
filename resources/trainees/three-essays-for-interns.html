Three Essays for Interns – Reading & Discussion Guide

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

THREE ESSAYS FOR INTERNS

Reading & Discussion Guide

Purpose

These are not treatment manuals and they are not assigned because every claim is empirically established. They are useful because they make certain patterns unusually easy to see.

Read them as provocations for supervision:

1. What pattern is the author pointing to?

2. Where have you seen something similar?

3. What evidence would you need before deciding the pattern applies to a particular patient, family, workplace, or treatment system?

4. What would be dangerous about becoming too certain?

READING 1

Issendai, “Sick Systems: How to Keep Someone With You Forever”

Read:

https: //issendai.com/psychology/sick-systems/

Why interns should read it

The essay gives memorable language for systems that keep people trapped through chronic urgency, exhaustion, emotional investment, intermittent reinforcement, and the promise that everything will improve after the next crisis.

It is useful far beyond abusive relationships. Interns may recognize analogous dynamics in families, workplaces, treatment systems, caregiving arrangements, and even clinical relationships.

Clinical ideas worth carrying forward

  • Repeated crises can become part of the system rather than exceptions to it.
  • Exhaustion reduces a person’s practical ability to evaluate alternatives or make changes.
  • Intermittent success can maintain enormous effort even when the overall arrangement is harmful.
  • A person’s strongest virtues, including loyalty, patience, hope, work ethic, and willingness to help, can sometimes keep them stuck.
  • “Why don’t they just leave?” is often the least interesting clinical question. A better question is: What makes leaving cognitively, emotionally, financially, relationally, or practically difficult?

Do not turn it into

  • a diagnosis;
  • proof that a particular relationship is abusive;
  • an explanation that replaces assessment;
  • a reason to tell a patient what their relationship “really is.”

Supervision prompts

1. What is being reinforced in this system?

2. What keeps the person too overloaded to reconsider the arrangement?

3. What would create enough slack for genuine choice?

4. Am I trying to rescue someone from a system they have not yet decided to leave?

5. Could the treatment system itself be reproducing any of these dynamics?

Optional companion:

Issendai, “Qualities That Keep You in a Sick System”

https: //issendai.com/psychology/sick-systems/sick-systems-qualities-that-keep-you-stuck/

READING 2

The Last Psychiatrist, “Just Because You See It, Doesn’t Mean It’s Gone”

Read:

https: //thelastpsychiatrist.com/2012/08/just_because_you_see_it_doesnt.html

Why interns should read it

The central clinical problem is one interns encounter constantly: insight is not the same thing as change.

Patients can become extremely accurate narrators of their own patterns. Therapists can also become extremely impressed by accurate narration. Neither fact guarantees that anything outside the consulting room has changed.

Clinical ideas worth carrying forward

  • Being able to name a defense, pattern, diagnosis, or relational dynamic does not mean it has stopped organizing behavior.
  • Insight can occasionally become another way to avoid the harder task of behaving differently.
  • A therapy can feel deep because the conversation is psychologically sophisticated while the patient’s life remains remarkably stable.
  • The clinically useful question is not only “Does the patient understand?” but also “What can the patient now do that was previously difficult or impossible?”

Do not turn it into

  • contempt for insight-oriented therapy;
  • a demand for visible behavioral change on the therapist’s timetable;
  • an excuse to dismiss reflection, ambivalence, or slow change;
  • a claim that every interpretation in the essay is established clinical science.

Supervision prompts

1. What has changed outside the session?

2. Is insight increasing freedom of action, or mainly increasing vocabulary?

3. Are therapist and patient rewarding each other for having sophisticated conversations?

4. What experiment could test whether the new understanding changes behavior?

READING 3

The Last Psychiatrist, “The Most Important Article On Psychiatry You Will Ever Read”

Read:

https: //thelastpsychiatrist.com/2007/07/the_most_important_article_on.html

Why interns should read it

Ignore the title’s modesty problem. The useful lesson is epistemic: clinicians see patients from a particular vantage point, through particular categories, under particular institutional conditions. Perspective changes what appears obvious.

The essay is useful for teaching interns to notice the difference between:

  • what they observed;
  • how they interpreted it;
  • what their diagnostic or theoretical framework made salient; and
  • what another observer might have seen.

Clinical ideas worth carrying forward

  • Perspective is not the same thing as reality.
  • Familiar categories can make an interpretation feel more certain than the underlying evidence warrants.
  • The same behavior can look different when viewed developmentally, culturally, relationally, behaviorally, neurologically, or systemically.
  • Good assessment requires active attempts to disconfirm the first compelling story.

Do not turn it into

  • nihilism about diagnosis;
  • “everything is subjective”;
  • reflexive contrarianism;
  • permission to replace evidence with cleverness.

Supervision prompts

1. What did I actually observe?

2. What am I inferring?

3. What alternative formulation would explain the same facts?

4. What data would make me revise my preferred formulation?

5. Which part of this case may look different from the patient’s, caregiver’s, teacher’s, physician’s, or partner’s vantage point?

HOW TO USE THESE IN SUPERVISION

A useful format is 15 minutes:

5 minutes: What idea from the reading felt immediately recognizable?

5 minutes: Apply it to a deidentified case without deciding in advance that it fits.

5 minutes: Argue the opposite formulation.

The goal is not to make interns cynical. It is to make them harder to fool, including by patients, institutions, theories, supervisors, and themselves.
