Clinical Practice · Practical Toolkit

Neurodevelopmental & Executive-Function Adaptations

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Practical tools for finding the access barrier, changing the demand or environment, and preserving autonomy while keeping the clinical target clear. Use the smallest support that makes participation more accurate and sustainable.

Clinician Tool: Barrier → Access Map

1. Demand: What does this moment require: speaking, shifting, remembering, initiating, waiting, tolerating uncertainty, reading social meaning, filtering sensory input, or coordinating several steps?

2. Signal: What do you observe: delay, repeated questions, shutdown, leaving, joking, arguing, forgetting, freezing, rushing, scripting, movement, or a change from baseline?

3. Hypothesis: Name more than one possible explanation before acting.

4. Access test: Change one variable: language load, sensory load, predictability, response mode, processing time, task size, cueing, or transition support.

5. Learn: Did participation become easier, more accurate, or more self-directed? Keep what helps and revise the formulation when it does not.

Clinician Tool: Therapy Access Check

Before interpreting low participation as avoidance or resistance, ask whether the patient understands the task, can hold the steps in working memory, can generate an answer quickly enough, can tolerate the sensory environment, can identify internal states, and knows what the clinician expects.

Change access first when the barrier is access.

Clinician Tool: Explicit Session Architecture

At the beginning of session, show or state the agenda. Identify what will happen first, what decisions need to be made, and what will be taken home. When changing topics, mark the transition clearly. At the end, summarize the plan in concrete language.

Clinician Tool: Reduce Language Load

Use shorter questions. Ask one question at a time. Replace abstract labels with examples. Allow processing time before rephrasing. Offer written, visual, or choice-based ways to respond when useful.

Clinician Tool: Executive-Function Task Repair

When a between-session task repeatedly fails, inspect the task architecture. Is there a clear first step? Is the material visible? Is there a cue? Is the timing realistic? Does the patient need another person present? Is the task too large? Is there an immediate competing reward? Is the plan stored somewhere the patient actually looks?

External supports are treatment tools, not evidence that the patient “did not really learn” the skill.

Clinician Tool: Capacity Before Compliance

When a patient repeatedly does not do something they can sometimes do, avoid collapsing the question into “can” versus “won’t.” Performance can change with fatigue, novelty, stress, sensory load, competing demands, task initiation, working memory, pain, sleep, and the amount of self-regulation already being used. Ask what conditions make the skill available and build those conditions deliberately.

Clinician Tool: Sensory and Regulation Access

Ask directly about sound, light, movement, temperature, clothing, eye contact, body position, waiting, transitions, and recovery time. Harmless self-regulation should not be removed simply because it looks atypical.

Clinician Tool: Uncertainty and Transition Repair

If distress clusters around changes, endings, waiting, or unclear expectations, make the uncertain part explicit. Separate what is known, what is not known yet, what will stay the same, what choices remain available, and what happens if the plan changes. Predictability is an access support; the longer-term goal can still include building flexible responses to uncertainty.

Clinician Tool: Interests, Stimming, and Regulation

Do not automatically remove an intense interest or repetitive movement because it is prominent. Ask what it does. It may support pleasure, identity, expertise, connection, attention, regulation, or recovery. If it is creating a specific functional problem, target that problem rather than “looking less autistic.”

Patient Handout: Make Therapy Easier to Use

Therapy works better when the way information is given matches the way you can use it.

You can ask your clinician to slow down, write things down, explain a vague question, give you time to think, show the plan for the session, reduce sensory distractions, or break a task into smaller steps.

Using supports is not cheating. Glasses do not prevent someone from learning to read, and reminders do not prevent someone from learning a routine.

When to Re-Formulate

Reassess the formulation when there is a sudden change from baseline; loss of previously reliable skills; new aggression, self-injury, shutdown, or withdrawal; a support that used to work no longer works; distress is strongly setting-specific; or the intervention requires increasing amounts of prompting or coercion. Consider pain/medical issues, sleep, trauma, anxiety/OCD, mood, medication effects, bullying or exploitation, communication mismatch, and environmental demands.

Clinical Boundary

Adaptations should improve access to an appropriate treatment, not replace differential assessment. Autism, ADHD, trauma, language disorder, intellectual disability, sleep problems, mood disorders, psychosis, substance use, and medical conditions can overlap in presentation.
