Neurodevelopmental & Executive-Function Adaptations

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Practical tools for finding the access barrier, changing the demand or environment, and preserving autonomy while keeping the clinical target clear. Use the smallest support that makes participation more accurate and sustainable.

1. Barrier → Access Map

Use this before interpreting delay, shutdown, arguing, forgetting, joking, leaving, or repeated questions as resistance.

DEMAND
What does this moment require?
→
SIGNAL
What changed or became hard?
→
2+ HYPOTHESES
Name more than one explanation.
→
ACCESS TEST
Change one variable.
→
LEARN
Did participation improve?

A support that improves accuracy or independence is clinically informative. A support that does nothing tells you to revise the hypothesis.

2. Task Launch Map

Use when someone knows what they need to do but repeatedly cannot get started. Treat “starting” as a chain with several possible failure points.

1 · FIND ITCan I see the task, materials, or reminder?
→
2 · SHRINK ITWhat is the first action that takes under two minutes?
→
3 · START ITWhat cue, person, place, or timer gets the body moving?
VISIBLEPut it where it is seen.
SMALLReduce the first step.
CUEDAttach it to a reliable trigger.
SUPPORTEDUse body doubling or co-starting.

If a task repeatedly fails, repair the launch architecture before increasing reminders, consequences, or moral pressure.

3. Therapy Access Check

Before asking “Why aren’t they participating?” scan the access channels.

UNDERSTANDDo they understand the task and why it matters?
HOLDCan they keep the steps in working memory?
GENERATECan they produce an answer without excessive language load?
REGULATECan they tolerate the sensory and emotional environment?
IDENTIFYCan they recognize internal states well enough to answer?
PREDICTDo they know what the clinician expects next?

4. Explicit Session Architecture

STARTShow the agenda
What are we doing today?
WORKName the task
What decision or skill are we working on?
SHIFTMark transitions
“We’re changing topics now.”
ENDExternalize the plan
Write down what happens next.

5. Reduce Language Load

ONEAsk one question at a time.
CONCRETEReplace abstractions with examples.
WAITAllow processing time before rephrasing.
ALTERNATIVESOffer point, choose, write, draw, or show.

6. Executive-Function Task Repair

When a between-session task repeatedly fails, inspect the architecture instead of simply repeating the instruction.

☐ First step
Is the first action unmistakably clear?
☐ Visibility
Are materials and reminders where the person actually looks?
☐ Cue
Is there a reliable trigger for starting?
☐ Timing
Does the plan fit the person’s real day and energy?
☐ Task size
Is the task small enough to enter?
☐ Social support
Would co-starting or body doubling help?
☐ Competing reward
Is something immediate consistently winning?
☐ Storage
Is the plan stored somewhere usable rather than merely “written down”?

External supports are treatment tools. They are not evidence that the person “did not really learn” the skill.

8. Sensory and Regulation Access

Ask directly about sound, light, movement, temperature, clothing, eye contact, body position, waiting, transitions, and recovery time. Harmless self-regulation should not be removed simply because it looks atypical.

9. Uncertainty and Transition Repair

KNOWN
What is definitely happening? What will stay the same?
NOT KNOWN YET
What is still uncertain? When might we know more?
CHOICES
What can the person still decide or control?
IF IT CHANGES
What is Plan B, and what support comes with it?

10. Interests, Stimming, and Regulation

Do not automatically remove an intense interest or repetitive movement because it is prominent. Ask what it does. It may support pleasure, identity, expertise, connection, attention, regulation, or recovery. If it is creating a specific functional problem, target that problem rather than “looking less autistic.”

Patient Handout: Make Therapy Easier to Use

SLOW DOWNI need more processing time.
WRITE ITPlease put the plan where I can see it.
MAKE IT CLEARPlease explain the vague part.
CHANGE THE FORMATLet me point, choose, write, or draw.

Using supports is not cheating. Glasses do not prevent someone from learning to read, and reminders do not prevent someone from learning a routine.

When to Re-Formulate

Reassess the formulation when there is a sudden change from baseline; loss of previously reliable skills; new aggression, self-injury, shutdown, or withdrawal; a support that used to work no longer works; distress is strongly setting-specific; or the intervention requires increasing amounts of prompting or coercion. Consider pain or medical issues, sleep, trauma, anxiety/OCD, mood, medication effects, bullying or exploitation, communication mismatch, and environmental demands.

Clinical Boundary

Adaptations should improve access to an appropriate treatment, not replace differential assessment. Autism, ADHD, trauma, language disorder, intellectual disability, sleep problems, mood disorders, psychosis, substance use, and medical conditions can overlap in presentation.
