Clinician instructions & differential depth

Rule out urgent medical change and immediate safety concerns. Establish the person's communication preferences; apparent agreement may conceal misunderstanding. An improved response to one support is contextual evidence, not a diagnosis. Test more than one plausible explanation over time. For anxiety, distinguish access from reassurance or avoidance maintenance before choosing exposure; use appropriate training and consent.

Clinician instructions, worked case & limits

ABC descriptions generate hypotheses; they do not prove function. A consequence is simply what follows, not necessarily a punishment. A reinforcing effect requires evidence of increased future behavior, not an assumption about intention. Compare repeated events, successful contexts, health needs, and the person's account. Qualified teams conduct formal functional assessment; do not provoke dangerous behavior to test a hypothesis.

In the worksheet example, removing work could maintain escape in some contexts; it could also provide needed access or relieve pain. Preserve assistance while gathering information. Teach an accessible request for help or a pause rather than requiring escalation to obtain it. Review dignity, autonomy, safety, engagement, and skill acquisition together.

Possible exit: choose a meaningful, manageable step with appropriate clinical support; learn from what happens.
When this model fits—and when it does not

Use collaboratively when fear-linked avoidance is plausible. Avoidance of unsafe conditions, inaccessible demands, pain, or discrimination may be protective. Do not turn every refusal into an exposure target. Reassurance patterns in OCD need a trained formulation; reduction of distress during a trial is not the only learning outcome.

Worked example & interpretation

Oral math reasoning with difficulty writing suggests an output barrier worth assessing. It does not establish intact written math skill or exclude a learning disability. Move between quadrants as context and evidence change. Use the richer two-track planning guide.

Where do you notice it?

Choose a place—or say “not sure.” Sensations do not have one fixed emotional meaning.

You can point, draw, or describe. You may skip any part.
Clinician instructions & medical boundary

Ask permission; do not require body focus when it increases distress or dissociation. Use external noticing or a pause instead. Avoid interpreting a body location as proof of trauma or a specific emotion. New or severe symptoms need medical evaluation; sudden chest pain or breathing difficulty requires urgent help according to the setting's emergency procedure.

How much support do I need?

Six separate levels. The person can change the labels or decline the scale.

Choose a level to discuss support. This is not a risk score.
Personalize rather than impose

Distress and danger are distinct. A person at a low distress level can still need urgent help; high distress does not automatically establish immediate danger. Agree individual signals, preferred supports, and response roles. Never make assistance contingent on reaching a higher number.

What would help right now?

You can change your mind. A choice here is not consent to treatment.

Supported choice & assent

Offer real, feasible alternatives without rewarding agreement or punishing refusal. Confirm meaning in the person's preferred mode. Assent supports participation but does not replace legally required consent. Where choices are constrained, state the constraint plainly.

Worked example: an appointment transition

Before: preview who will be present and how to request a break. During: offer written choices and quieter space. After: review participation and support burden when the person is ready, not during acute overload. Record which supports enabled participation rather than equating compliance with success.
