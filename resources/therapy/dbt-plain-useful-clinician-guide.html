DBT, PLAIN AND USEFUL

Teaching & Practice Guide

Version 0.2

PURPOSE

This guide turns the four-module curriculum into something a group leader can actually teach. Each lesson follows the same rhythm so participants hear the idea, see it, try it, use it on something real, and leave with one small practice.

THE LESSON RHYTHM

TEACH, 5–10 minutes

Explain one useful idea in ordinary language. Keep theory only when it helps someone use the skill.

SHOW IT, 3–5 minutes

Use a short example and think through it out loud.

TRY IT TOGETHER, 10–15 minutes

Everyone practices on the same low-stakes example. No personal disclosure required.

MAKE IT YOURS, 10–15 minutes

Participants apply the skill to a real situation. They can write privately, use a fictional example, or share only what they want.

DEBRIEF, 5–10 minutes

Ask what changed, what was hard, what did not fit, and what would make the skill easier to use.

TRY IT THIS WEEK, 2–5 minutes

Choose one small real-world practice. The goal is information, not perfect completion.

The handout supports the lesson. It is not the lesson.

REUSABLE MODULE OPENER: IS THIS WORTH LEARNING?

Use at the beginning of each module, especially when motivation is mixed. Nobody has to pretend to be excited about a skills group.

Ask: What problem would make this module worth your time? What would you like to get easier? What do you not want these skills used to tell you? What is your best reason not to practice them? What would count as enough benefit to keep going?

If ambivalence is high, spend a few minutes on actual pros and cons. The purpose is informed participation, not a sales pitch.

OPTIONAL ORIENTATION

LESSON A: TRY IT AND SEE

TEACH

Skills are experiments. A useful question is not “Did I do it right?” but “What happened when I tried it?”

SHOW IT

Compare “I failed to use the skill” with “I remembered it after the argument ended. Retrieval is the problem.”

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give the group three outcomes: helped, did not help, could not use it in the moment. Ask what each result teaches.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one tiny behavior to test this week.

DEBRIEF

What would make the experiment easier to remember or easier to try?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Run one small experiment and bring back the result.

LESSON B: HOW DID WE GET HERE?

TEACH

Big reactions usually have a sequence. If we can find the sequence, we can often find an earlier place to intervene.

SHOW IT

Text arrives → stomach drops → “they are rejecting me” → reread five times → send angry reply → immediate relief → later regret.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Put a sample chain on cards or a whiteboard and have the group place the events in order.

MAKE IT YOURS

Map one low-stakes chain from the past week.

DEBRIEF

Where was the earliest realistic place something could have changed?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Notice one chain in real time and mark the first link you caught.

MODULE 1: MINDFULNESS

Goal: Notice what is happening clearly enough to choose what to do next.

LESSON 1: AIM YOUR ATTENTION

TEACH

Attention gets pulled. The skill is noticing where it went and choosing where to put it next. Internal attention is not automatically better than external attention.

SHOW IT

Spend 20 seconds noticing sounds, then 20 seconds noticing visual details, then compare.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Run three one-minute rounds: external details, movement, and one concrete task.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose which attention style works best when stressed, scattered, numb, or overloaded.

DEBRIEF

Which target made you more present? Which made things worse?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Use one chosen attention target during one mildly stressful moment.

LESSON 2: FACTS, STORY, GUESS

TEACH

What happened, what we think it means, and what we predict will happen next are different things.

SHOW IT

“They did not answer my text” is a fact. “They are angry with me” is a story. “They are going to end the relationship” is a guess.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give six statements and have the group sort each into fact, story, or guess. Include one genuinely ambiguous statement.

MAKE IT YOURS

Take one current situation and write one line for each category.

DEBRIEF

Which part felt most certain before you separated it?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Catch one story that initially felt like a fact.

LESSON 3: ONE THING AT A TIME

TEACH

The point is not moral purity about multitasking. The point is reducing unnecessary switching when split attention is making a task harder.

SHOW IT

Read a short paragraph while answering simple questions, then read another without switching and compare.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Do a two-minute single-task activity. Allow helpful supports such as movement, music without lyrics, or fidgets.

MAKE IT YOURS

Pick one task that gets harder when attention is fragmented.

DEBRIEF

What counts as a support for you, and what is actually a distraction?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Give one task five minutes of deliberate single-task attention.

LESSON 4: USE THE WHOLE PICTURE

TEACH

Strong feelings contain information. Reasoning contains information. Values, risk, uncertainty, and long-term goals also matter. Better decisions use more of the picture.

SHOW IT

Work through a decision using six questions: What do I know? What do I feel? What might the feeling be telling me? What matters? What do I not know? What action fits the whole picture?

TRY IT TOGETHER

Use a neutral dilemma, such as whether to confront a friend about a canceled plan or wait for more information.

MAKE IT YOURS

Apply the six questions to one real decision.

DEBRIEF

Which source of information do you tend to ignore? Which do you overvalue?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Use the six questions once before making a decision under stress.

LESSON 5: NOTICE WITHOUT OBEYING

TEACH

A thought, urge, memory, image, or feeling can be loud without being an instruction.

SHOW IT

Say “I need to check my phone right now,” then wait 30 seconds while noticing what happens to the urge.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Use a harmless urge, such as checking the time, shifting position, or reaching for a nearby object. Notice, wait, then choose.

MAKE IT YOURS

Name one recurring urge or thought that benefits from a small pause.

DEBRIEF

Did delaying change the urge, the action, or just the sense of choice?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Delay one non-urgent action by 30 seconds to five minutes and notice what happens.

MODULE 2: DISTRESS TOLERANCE

Goal: Get through intense moments without adding avoidable damage, then return to the actual problem.

LESSON 1: PAUSE, PROTECT, DECIDE

TEACH

In a hot moment, the first job may be to keep the next 60 seconds from creating a much bigger problem.

SHOW IT

Use a scenario: an angry text arrives while driving home. Model pausing, protecting against impulsive action, then deciding what can wait.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give three crisis-lite situations and ask: What needs to pause? What needs protection? What decision can safely wait?

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one kind of situation where a 60-second pause would help.

DEBRIEF

What makes pausing hard? What helps create the pause in real life?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Use one physical cue for pause, such as setting down the phone, sitting on your hands, leaving the room, or saying “not yet.”

LESSON 2: GET YOUR BRAIN BACK ONLINE

TEACH

Sometimes the immediate problem is not insight. Your system is too activated, shut down, or overloaded to use the insight you already have.

SHOW IT

Rate activation 0–10, do one minute of paced exhale, cool water, movement, or reduced sensory input, then rate again.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Test two safe state-change methods and compare their effects. No method has to work for everyone.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose two methods worth keeping and one you already know is a bad fit.

DEBRIEF

Which method changed your body? Which changed attention? Which just annoyed you?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Use one state-change tool before problem-solving in a mildly activated moment.

LESSON 3: GET THROUGH THE NEXT BIT

TEACH

Crisis survival has a time limit. The job is to get through the next ten minutes, hour, or night without making the situation harder to repair.

SHOW IT

Build a 30-minute plan for someone who is furious after a breakup but has to wait until morning to make practical decisions.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Create a survival plan with four parts: time window, what not to worsen, what helps attention/body, and when problem-solving resumes.

MAKE IT YOURS

Build one plan for a predictable rough interval.

DEBRIEF

How do you know when temporary distraction has become avoidance?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Use a defined survival interval once and note when you returned to the problem.

LESSON 4: WHAT HELPS WHEN I’M FLOODED?

TEACH

A short personal list beats thirty coping skills you cannot remember when you need them.

SHOW IT

Build a six-item menu: two body tools, two attention tools, two environment tools.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Brainstorm options by category, then cross out anything unrealistic, inaccessible, unsafe, or personally irritating.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose your first three, in order.

DEBRIEF

What makes a coping tool retrievable? What makes it vanish from your brain at exactly the wrong moment?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Put the first-three list somewhere you can actually reach it.

LESSON 5: THIS IS HAPPENING. NOW WHAT?

TEACH

Accepting that something is real is not approving of it. It is stopping the argument with whether the already-existing fact exists so you can decide what to do next.

SHOW IT

Separate three sentences: “This happened.” “I hate that this happened.” “Here is what I can still do.”

TRY IT TOGETHER

Use a neutral fixed fact, such as a flight cancellation or a broken appliance. Sort fact, judgment, and remaining options.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one fact you keep mentally retrying and write what remains changeable.

DEBRIEF

What makes acceptance feel dangerous or like surrender?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

When “this should not be happening” loops, add “and it is happening, so what is still mine to do?”

LESSON 6: MAKE ROOM, THEN CHOOSE

TEACH

Fighting an emotion can become a second problem. Making room means letting the experience be present long enough to choose behavior. It does not mean liking it.

SHOW IT

Notice muscle tension while thinking of a mild annoyance. Loosen one area without trying to become calm.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Practice allowing one mildly uncomfortable sensation, thought, or urge for 30 seconds while choosing posture and attention deliberately.

MAKE IT YOURS

Identify one form of internal struggle that usually escalates things.

DEBRIEF

What changed when the goal was “allow” rather than “get rid of”?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Loosen one physical defense and choose the next action based on the goal, not on whether the feeling disappeared.

MODULE 3: EMOTION REGULATION

Goal: Understand emotions, lower preventable vulnerability, and choose actions that improve the longer trajectory.

LESSON 1: EMOTION IS DATA, NOT AN ORDER

TEACH

Emotions are signals and action systems. They can be useful without being infallible.

SHOW IT

Take anger: What might it signal? What does it make the body do? What action does it prepare? When is that useful, and when is it not?

TRY IT TOGETHER

Assign common emotions to small groups and have them identify signal, body response, urge, useful function, and possible false alarm.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one emotion you often distrust or obey too quickly.

DEBRIEF

What information is worth keeping even if you change the action?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Ask once: “What is this emotion trying to tell me, and do I want to follow the urge?”

LESSON 2: BREAK DOWN THE FEELING

TEACH

An emotional episode is not one giant blob. It has parts, and different parts can be changed.

SHOW IT

Map a mild episode from prompt → meaning → body → attention → urge → action → immediate effect → later effect.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Map a fictional scenario on a whiteboard and let the group suggest different intervention points.

MAKE IT YOURS

Map one recent episode privately.

DEBRIEF

Where was there actual leverage? Where was there not?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Catch one component in real time, body, attention, urge, or meaning.

LESSON 3: FACTS, STORY, GUESS (REVIEW)

TEACH

The same skill matters here because emotional intensity often rises around an interpretation or prediction, not only around the observable event.

SHOW IT

Take “My boss said, ‘See me before you leave.’” Separate fact, possible stories, possible guesses, and confidence in each.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Generate three plausible interpretations of the same ambiguous event without forcing a positive one.

MAKE IT YOURS

Use the worksheet on one emotionally loaded situation.

DEBRIEF

Did the emotion change when certainty changed, even if the situation did not?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Use “I know / I think / I predict” language once.

LESSON 4: CHANGE THE ACTION

TEACH

Sometimes the feeling makes sense and the default action still makes tomorrow worse. Change the behavior without pretending the feeling is wrong.

SHOW IT

Anxiety says avoid the email. Anger says send the email now. Shame says disappear. Compare short-term payoff with delayed cost.

TRY IT TOGETHER

For several emotions, identify an urge, short-term payoff, long-term cost, and one smaller alternative action.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one recurring urge and design the smallest useful alternative.

DEBRIEF

How can you tell the difference between a protective action and an avoidance loop?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Change one small action, then observe what new information you get.

LESSON 5: CHANGE WHAT IS CHANGEABLE

TEACH

Sometimes the situation is the problem. Regulation is not a substitute for making the call, asking for help, changing the plan, filing the form, setting the limit, or leaving.

SHOW IT

Use a concrete problem and sort it into control, influence, and outside control.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give a practical scenario and generate at least three possible actions before choosing one.

MAKE IT YOURS

Pick one current problem and write the next step small enough to do or schedule.

DEBRIEF

Were you trying to regulate around something that actually needs action?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Take one concrete step on one solvable problem.

LESSON 6: WHAT MAKES THIS HARDER?

TEACH

Your threshold changes. Sleep, pain, hunger, hormones, medication, sensory load, conflict, loneliness, executive depletion, and other variables can make the same trigger land very differently.

SHOW IT

Compare the same minor frustration on a rested day and after four hours of sleep plus missed lunch.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Have participants circle possible vulnerability factors, then choose only the top two or three that actually matter for them.

MAKE IT YOURS

Write one early intervention for each high-impact factor.

DEBRIEF

Which “basic self-care” advice is useful for you, and which is just generic wallpaper?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Track one high-impact factor only if the answer could change what you do.

LESSON 7: BUILD UP. PLAN AHEAD.

TEACH

Emotion regulation is easier when life contains reward, competence, connection, meaning, and some preparation for predictable hard moments.

SHOW IT

Separate three levers: something worth looking forward to, something that builds competence, and something worth rehearsing ahead.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Generate small examples that take under 20 minutes and do not require money.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one reward, one competence action, and one situation to rehearse.

DEBRIEF

Which of these actually gives energy back? Which turns into another self-improvement assignment?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Do one thing from the three, not necessarily all three.

MODULE 4: INTERPERSONAL EFFECTIVENESS

Goal: Ask clearly, protect relationships when useful, keep self-respect, and account for power and safety.

LESSON 1: WHAT DO I WANT FROM THIS CONVERSATION?

TEACH

Before choosing how to communicate, know what you are trying to protect: the outcome, the relationship, self-respect, safety, or some combination.

SHOW IT

Use one conversation and rate the importance of each goal from 0–10.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give two scenarios where the priorities differ, such as negotiating with a close friend versus correcting a billing error.

MAKE IT YOURS

Rate the priorities for one upcoming conversation.

DEBRIEF

What tradeoff are you willing to make, and what tradeoff are you not willing to make?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Before one difficult conversation, name the top priority in one sentence.

LESSON 2: ASK CLEARLY. KNOW YOUR LINE.

TEACH

Make one concrete ask. Give enough context to make sense. Stay on the point. Decide in advance what you will do if the answer is no.

SHOW IT

Turn a five-minute complaint into a four-line request.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give the group vague statements and have them rewrite each as a specific ask.

MAKE IT YOURS

Draft one real request in five lines or fewer.

DEBRIEF

Which part is hardest, asking, tolerating the answer, or knowing what comes next?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Make one low-stakes direct request.

LESSON 3: STAY CONNECTED. KEEP YOURSELF.

TEACH

You can understand another person without agreeing, surrendering, or becoming endlessly nice.

SHOW IT

Use the form: “I can see why ___ makes sense to you. I still need / think / want ___.”

TRY IT TOGETHER

Practice validating one reasonable part of a disagreement while keeping a different position.

MAKE IT YOURS

Rewrite one conflict response to include both understanding and your position.

DEBRIEF

What makes validation sound real rather than scripted?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Reflect one person’s actual point before stating your own.

LESSON 4: KEEP YOUR SELF-RESPECT

TEACH

Getting agreement is not always worth the cost. Self-respect here means behaving in a way you can stand behind later.

SHOW IT

Compare an appropriate apology with apologizing for having a need.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Sort sample statements into repair, unnecessary apology, exaggeration, fair limit, and values-based choice.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one conversation where you tend to over-apologize, over-accommodate, bluff, or overstate.

DEBRIEF

What would tomorrow-you respect about how you handled it?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Remove one unnecessary apology or exaggeration from a real conversation.

LESSON 5: POWER, RISK, OPTIONS

TEACH

Communication skill cannot erase who controls the schedule, money, housing, grade, job, transportation, access, or consequences.

SHOW IT

Compare asking a friend for a favor with asking a supervisor for a schedule change when job security matters.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Map: my control, their control, dependence, alternatives, costs, safety, leverage.

MAKE IT YOURS

Map one real situation without requiring disclosure of sensitive details.

DEBRIEF

What advice sounds good only if both people have equal power?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Identify one ally, document, policy, alternative, or timing change that improves your position.

LESSON 6: REQUEST, BOUNDARY, OR DECISION?

TEACH

A request asks someone else to choose. A boundary states what you will do under certain conditions. A decision is an action you choose for yourself.

SHOW IT

Request: “Please text if you’ll be late.” Boundary: “If yelling starts, I’ll end the conversation.” Decision: “I’m not attending.”

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give six muddy statements and rewrite each into the correct category.

MAKE IT YOURS

Take one thing you need to say and decide which type it actually is.

DEBRIEF

Where were you accidentally calling a demand a boundary, or asking permission for a decision?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Use one clean one-sentence request, boundary, or decision.

LESSON 7: BUILD THE RELATIONSHIPS YOU WANT

TEACH

Connection is partly a logistics problem. Relationships are more likely when there is repeated contact, small bids for interaction, enough reciprocity, and time. The goal is not to perform perfectly. It is to create more chances for connection and put effort where the other person gives something back.

SHOW IT

Compare two plans for someone who wants more friends: “wait until I meet someone I instantly click with” versus “go to the same class four times, say hello to the same two people, ask one real question, and invite one person for coffee if there is mutual interest.”

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give fictional profiles with different interests and constraints. For each, identify two places where repeated contact is plausible, one small bid for connection, and one sign of reciprocity.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one place, person, or existing weak tie where one small increase in contact would be realistic. Write the smallest next bid.

DEBRIEF

What counts as reciprocity? How long do you keep trying before effort becomes chasing? What makes a relationship feel like a fit rather than a performance?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Make one low-stakes bid for connection and notice what comes back. The result is information, not a referendum on likability.

LESSON 8: REPAIR, RENEGOTIATE, OR LEAVE IT ALONE

TEACH

When an interaction goes badly, repeating the same conversation more eloquently is not always the answer.

SHOW IT

Sort four outcomes: I caused harm → repair. The plan failed but both still want it → renegotiate. My approach was poor → try differently. Further discussion will not help → stop or act elsewhere.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Give short scenarios and have the group choose repair, renegotiate, try differently, accept, distance, or act elsewhere.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one unresolved interaction and identify the next category.

DEBRIEF

What tells you a conversation is still workable? What tells you it has become a loop?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Take one next step that is different from simply repeating the previous conversation.

CROSS-MODULE TOOLS

LESSON A: WHAT'S GETTING IN THE WAY?

TEACH

When treatment is stuck, do not assume the problem lives in the patient. A barrier can live in the patient, therapist, intervention, relationship, environment, or system. Finding the location changes what to do next.

SHOW IT

Use one outcome, such as repeated missed group. Show six different explanations: avoidance, therapist rupture, poor-fit material, mistrust, transportation failure, or scheduling policy. Same outcome, different target.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Barrier Detective: Give short examples and have the group place each in one of the six domains. Allow more than one answer when the case genuinely has more than one barrier.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one place where treatment or practice has been stuck. Scan all six domains privately and circle the one or two most plausible barriers.

DEBRIEF

How did the proposed solution change when the location of the problem changed? Where do people tend to assign blame too quickly?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Change one plausible barrier by one small amount and see whether participation or usefulness changes.

LESSON B: TRACK WHAT MATTERS

TEACH

Tracking is useful only when the answer could change a decision. More data is not automatically better data.

SHOW IT

Compare rating seven emotions every day because a form has seven rows with tracking sleep for one week because the actual question is whether sleep predicts irritability.

TRY IT TOGETHER

Data or Decoration?: Give the group possible things to track. For each, require a sentence beginning, “If the result is X, we would…” If nobody can finish the sentence, drop the measure.

MAKE IT YOURS

Choose one question worth answering. Name the variable, the decision it could change, the smallest reasonable recording burden, and the stop rule.

DEBRIEF

What are you collecting because it is useful, and what are you collecting because forms have boxes?

TRY IT THIS WEEK

Track one decision-relevant variable for a short defined window, or deliberately track nothing if no current decision needs data.

FACILITATOR NOTES

Keep examples ordinary before they become personal. Practice should come before disclosure.

Do not require participants to defend why a skill does not fit. Treat poor fit as information.

Use pairs only when the group feels safe enough. Writing privately is always a legitimate form of participation.

When a participant gives a better example or wording than the curriculum, use it.

Avoid turning every exercise into a discussion of insight. The target is an action someone could actually perform outside the room.

End each lesson by asking: “What would make this easier to use when you actually need it?”

PLAYFUL PRACTICE OPTIONS

Use these when the group needs more energy, less self-consciousness, or another way into the skill. Humor should lower stakes, not make a participant, diagnosis, symptom, culture, or painful experience the joke. The safest target is usually an absurd situation, an obviously terrible strategy, or the facilitator.

OPTIONAL ORIENTATION

TRY IT AND SEE

Bad Advice Laboratory: Give a harmless problem, such as being ten minutes late to meet a friend. Small groups invent the worst possible coping advice, then convert each bad idea into a testable experiment. “Panic harder” usually has poor outcome data.

HOW DID WE GET HERE?

Human Rube Goldberg Machine: Give the group eight cards describing an escalating but ridiculous chain, for example: vending machine eats dollar → kick vending machine → shoe comes off → stranger laughs → decide stranger has always hated you → send dramatic text. Put the cards in order, then find the earliest useful off-ramp.

MODULE 1: MINDFULNESS

AIM YOUR ATTENTION

Sensory Scavenger Hunt: Find something tiny, something crooked, something making a sound, something with an unexpectedly pleasant texture, and one detail nobody else noticed. Compare which kinds of noticing actually pulled attention into the room.

FACTS, STORY, GUESS

Tabloid Editor: Give a boring fact, such as “Jordan left the meeting five minutes early.” Teams write the most dramatic headline possible, then label what is fact, story, and guess. Bonus points for headlines that would get everyone sued.

ONE THING AT A TIME

Multitasking Olympics: Try a 60-second simple task while also answering random questions, then repeat without the interruptions. Keep score only for amusement, not performance evaluation. Discuss which extra inputs helped, hurt, or made no difference.

USE THE WHOLE PICTURE

Terrible Decision Court: Present a harmless dilemma, such as whether to buy an enormous decorative chair that fits nowhere. One side argues from emotion only, one from logic only, one from long-term goals, then the group builds the decision using the whole picture.

NOTICE WITHOUT OBEYING

Urge Surf, Extremely Low Stakes Edition: Put a bowl of wrapped candy, a mystery object, or a sign saying DO NOT TURN THIS OVER on the table. Notice the urge to interact with it, wait, then choose. Debrief the difference between wanting, deciding, and doing.

MODULE 2: DISTRESS TOLERANCE

PAUSE, PROTECT, DECIDE

The Worst Possible Next 60 Seconds: Give a crisis-lite scenario and have the group generate spectacularly bad immediate moves. Then identify what to pause, what to protect, and what can wait. Keep the examples reversible and non-dangerous.

GET YOUR BRAIN BACK ONLINE

Coping Skill Taste Test: Set up several one-minute stations, paced breathing, cold drink or cool washcloth, stretching, paced walking, sensory reduction, orienting. Participants rate each from “actually useful” to “absolutely not, thank you.” No gold star for liking breathing exercises.

GET THROUGH THE NEXT BIT

The Twenty-Minute Disaster Plan: Use a fake emergency such as “the internet is down five minutes before your favorite show” or “the bakery gave you the wrong pastry.” Build a serious crisis-survival structure around a unserious crisis, then transfer the structure to real situations.

WHAT HELPS WHEN I’M FLOODED?

Coping Draft: Put coping options on cards. Each person drafts a first-round pick, backup, and “do not draft under any circumstances.” Defend choices based on actual usefulness, accessibility, and likelihood of remembering them when upset.

THIS IS HAPPENING. NOW WHAT?

Customer Service With Reality: Give fixed facts no one likes, rain on picnic day, elevator broken, favorite item discontinued. Participants may file one 20-second complaint with the universe, then must answer: “Fine. What is still changeable?”

MAKE ROOM, THEN CHOOSE

Annoyance Museum: Use minor irritants, itchy tag, crooked picture, repetitive neutral sound, pen that clicks too loudly. Practice letting mild annoyance exist without launching a campaign against it. Stop immediately if an exercise is genuinely distressing.

MODULE 3: EMOTION REGULATION

EMOTION IS DATA, NOT AN ORDER

Emotion Publicist: Assign each group an emotion. Their job is to make the best case for why humanity needs it. Then another group identifies when that same emotion gives false alarms or terrible behavioral advice.

BREAK DOWN THE FEELING

CSI: Mild Inconvenience Unit: Investigate an absurdly ordinary emotional event, someone took the good parking space, coffee order was wrong, group chat went silent. Reconstruct the sequence like forensic analysts, but nobody gets arrested.

FACTS, STORY, GUESS (REVIEW)

Three Alternate Universes: Start with one ambiguous event. Groups create three equally plausible explanations, one benign, one annoying, one completely mundane. The point is not optimism. It is remembering that certainty can arrive before evidence.

CHANGE THE ACTION

Emotion’s Lawyer vs. Tomorrow’s Lawyer: One person argues for the immediate urge, one argues for tomorrow’s consequences. The group then picks the smallest action that does not require pretending the emotion disappeared.

CHANGE WHAT IS CHANGEABLE

Coping or Doing Something?: Read out situations. Participants hold up one side of a card for COPE and the other for ACT. Add a few where the answer is BOTH. Example: “The bill is wrong and you are furious.” Correct answer probably includes calming down enough to call billing.

WHAT MAKES THIS HARDER?

Why Is Everyone Suddenly Awful?: Present the same tiny annoyance under different conditions: rested, hungry, sick, overstimulated, after three meetings, after excellent sleep. Rank how much harder the exact same event would hit. The joke is the human nervous system, not the person.

BUILD UP. PLAN AHEAD.

Tiny Good Things Auction: Give everyone an imaginary $20 and a menu of small activities costing $1–$10 in time/effort rather than money, ten-minute walk, text a friend, fix one annoying drawer, listen to one song, sit outside. Bid on what would genuinely add something to the week.

MODULE 4: INTERPERSONAL EFFECTIVENESS

WHAT DO I WANT FROM THIS CONVERSATION?

Conversation Budget: Give participants ten tokens to spend across outcome, relationship, self-respect, and safety for different scenarios. There is no correct allocation. Compare what changes when the person is a friend, landlord, supervisor, stranger, or family member.

ASK CLEARLY. KNOW YOUR LINE.

Complaint Compression Challenge: Provide an absurdly long complaint about something trivial. Teams have 60 seconds to turn it into one specific request. Award imaginary prizes for shortest clear request and most unnecessary backstory successfully removed.

STAY CONNECTED. KEEP YOURSELF.

The Pineapple Pizza Summit: Pick a harmless disagreement people can take either side of. Practice showing that the other position makes sense while keeping your own. The goal is to disagree without either declaring war or pretending you changed your mind.

KEEP YOUR SELF-RESPECT

Apology Tax: Read sample statements and ask whether an apology is owed, optional, or being used as an entrance fee for having a need. Include ridiculous examples such as “Sorry, could you stop standing on my foot?”

POWER, RISK, OPTIONS

Same Words, Different Room: Use the exact same request with a friend, supervisor, police officer, landlord, parent, teenager, and customer-service representative. Ask what changes because of power, dependence, safety, and alternatives, even though the sentence stayed the same.

REQUEST, BOUNDARY, OR DECISION?

Boundary Court: Read statements such as “My boundary is that you have to answer my texts immediately.” The group rules REQUEST, BOUNDARY, DECISION, or SNEAKY DEMAND. Rewrite the sneaky demands into something the speaker actually controls.

BUILD THE RELATIONSHIPS YOU WANT

Where Would You Actually Meet This Person?: Give teams a fictional person with a few ordinary interests, routines, and constraints. They have to find three places where repeated contact is plausible and write one tiny first bid for connection. No soulmate hunting and no networking-event hostage situations.

CROSS-MODULE TOOLS

WHAT'S GETTING IN THE WAY?

Six Suspects: Present one stuck outcome, such as repeated missed practice. Assign six possible suspects: patient, therapist, intervention, relationship, environment, system. Teams make the strongest case for each before deciding what they would test first. The point is to make premature blame slightly embarrassing.

TRACK WHAT MATTERS

Data or Decoration?: Put possible tracking variables on cards. Each variable has to earn its place by completing the sentence, “If this changes, we would change ___.” Anything that cannot finish the sentence gets ceremonially retired from the spreadsheet.

REPAIR, RENEGOTIATE, OR LEAVE IT ALONE

Relationship Mechanic: Give short broken-interaction scenarios and ask whether the needed tool is repair, renegotiation, another attempt, acceptance, distance, or action somewhere else. Include one scenario where the correct intervention is to stop having the same conversation for the seventeenth time.

FACILITATOR HUMOR RULES

Keep the humor optional. Do not force participation in anything performative. Avoid jokes about self-harm, suicide, trauma, psychosis, abuse, bodies, identity, intelligence, poverty, addiction, or a participant’s disclosures. Use absurdity to lower stakes before personal practice. Let participants improve the examples. If the joke becomes more memorable than the skill, bring the skill back to the center.
