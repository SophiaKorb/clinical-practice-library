Clinical Practice · Practical Toolkit

Motivational Interviewing, Substance Use & Harm Reduction

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Original shareable tools for ambivalence, behavior change, relapse review, and harm-reduction-informed care.

Clinician Tool: Ambivalence Map

Name the specific change under discussion. Avoid global questions such as whether the patient is “motivated.”

Ask what the patient likes, values, or gets from the current behavior. Then ask what worries them, what the behavior costs, and what they would want to be different.

Listen for reasons that matter to the patient, not reasons the clinician wishes mattered. Finish by asking what, if anything, the patient wants to do next. Preserve the possibility that the answer is “not yet.”

Clinician Tool: Change Conversation Without the Sales Pitch

Use open questions to understand the patient's perspective. Reflect meaning, not just content. Notice statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment, and steps already taken.

When the patient argues for the status quo, do not automatically provide a stronger counterargument. Provide information with permission when possible, keep it concise, then return the decision to the patient.

Clinician Tool: Relapse or Return-to-Use Review

Avoid turning a return to use into a morality play. Review what happened before the episode, including stress, cues, people, money, access, sleep, pain, medication changes, conflict, shame, isolation, or overconfidence.

Identify the first point where the sequence could realistically have changed. Separate lapse from abandonment of the whole treatment plan.

Clinician Tool: Harm Reduction Safety Check

Ask about overdose history, route of use, mixing substances, using alone, withdrawal risk, fentanyl exposure, medication interactions, driving, pregnancy, medical conditions, and access to emergency help as clinically relevant.

Patient Handout: One Change at a Time

You do not have to solve your entire relationship with alcohol or drugs in one conversation.

Choose one change that matters to you. It might be using less, using less often, not mixing substances, avoiding one high-risk setting, taking medication consistently, carrying overdose-reversal medication, asking for help earlier, or moving toward abstinence.

If you return to an old pattern, use the information to improve the plan instead of deciding that nothing worked.

Clinical Boundary

Alcohol, benzodiazepine, and other withdrawal syndromes can be medically dangerous. Acute intoxication, severe withdrawal, overdose risk, suicidality, psychosis, or significant medical instability may require urgent medical or higher-level care.
