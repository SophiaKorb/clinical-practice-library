Intern and Case Manager Practice Toolkit

Sophia Korb Cohon, PhD

A set of practical routines for supervised clinicians, case managers and early-career behavioral health professionals. Use local approved documentation and referral systems—not this public website—for client details.

1. Start a focused contact

  1. Ask what the person wants help with today; clarify preferred language and communication needs.
  2. Identify urgent medical, psychiatric or environmental safety concerns and follow applicable escalation protocols.
  3. Choose one to three priorities collaboratively instead of generating an unmanageable list.
  4. Agree on next steps and confirm how follow-up will occur.

2. Core case-management skills

  • Resource navigation: confirm eligibility, fees, accessibility, hours and referral requirements rather than promising access.
  • Warm handoffs: obtain permission, connect with the receiving service and check whether the referral succeeded.
  • Coordination: clearly document responsibility, timing, consent and barriers.
  • Benefits and practical support: consider housing, food, insurance, transportation, identification, childcare and digital access.
  • Scope: do not independently diagnose, prescribe, or promise outcomes outside your role.

3. Intern clinical learning cycle

  1. Prepare: identify the clinical question and relevant guidance.
  2. Observe: distinguish reported experience, directly observed behavior and hypotheses.
  3. Formulate: consider alternative explanations and cultural/developmental context.
  4. Plan: use collaborative goals, measured baselines and interventions within training and scope.
  5. Consult: bring de-identified uncertainties, safety issues and ethical questions to supervision.
  6. Reflect: check client feedback, revisit assumptions and follow through.

4. Brief motivational interviewing reminders

Ask permission; use open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries. Explore the person's reasons for change without arguing or treating ambivalence as noncompliance. Avoid using reflective techniques to pressure agreement with a predetermined treatment goal.

5. Harm reduction and substance-use support

Offer overdose education, naloxone access information, safer-care and infection prevention links, treatment options and assistance with medical or social referrals. Respect client-defined goals while assessing acute risk.

6. A reusable supervision agenda

Case question: What decision do I need help making? Observed facts: What actually occurred? Formulation: What explanations fit or do not fit? Risk and ethics: What is urgent or uncertain? Next steps: Who does what, by when, and how will success be checked?

7. Referral and follow-up checklist

  • Client preference and permission documented in an approved system.
  • Appropriate resource verified, including eligibility and access barriers.
  • Receiving service and point of contact identified.
  • Handoff or appointment completed where possible.
  • Follow-up date and contingency plan established.

8. Trauma stewardship and sustainable practice

Repeated exposure to traumatic stories can affect sleep, beliefs, relationships and professional functioning. When a patient or caregiver is unhappy with treatment

Welcome the conversation: It is appropriate for a patient or caregiver to tell their case manager that treatment feels unhelpful, confusing, upsetting, or mismatched. Listen, validate the concern, and help them clarify what they would like to change. When safe and welcome, gently encourage them to discuss the concern with the treating therapist, too. Offer to help them prepare what to say, contact the therapist together, attend a joint conversation with permission, or facilitate a collaborative treatment-review meeting. Do not pressure them or promise a particular response.

Why this matters: A frank conversation may clarify goals and expectations, uncover misunderstandings, allow adjustment of methods or pace, and provide an opportunity to repair a rupture in the therapeutic alliance. For children and families, relationships with trusted clinicians may become important sources of predictability and connection; abrupt, unexplained changes can sometimes disrupt trust or attachment-related expectations. This is a possibility to consider, not a reason to require continued treatment.

Case-manager role: Hear the concern without defensiveness, ask what the person hopes will change, explain options neutrally, and offer to support a conversation with the therapist. Share information only with permission and according to applicable consent and confidentiality requirements. Avoid independently judging a clinician’s care or relaying speculative interpretations.

Important exceptions: If there are allegations of abuse, discrimination, boundary violations, coercion, immediate safety risk, or retaliation—or the person does not feel safe approaching the clinician—do not route them back as the only option. Support access to appropriate supervision, patient advocacy, grievance channels, protective processes, urgent care, or a different clinician as indicated. A patient or authorized decision-maker may request a change of provider or discontinue treatment in accordance with applicable rights and rules.

When changing care: If the person chooses to transfer, support a planned transition when possible: clear communication, continuity of needed services, informed choice, appropriate referral and a respectful goodbye when desired. Never use concerns about attachment to pressure someone to stay.

When concerns involve a prescriber or medication

Be a trusted first listener: Patients may feel more comfortable telling a case manager than a prescriber about side effects, fears, cost, missed doses, uncertainty about benefits, or feeling unheard. Welcome that disclosure without judgment. Ask what they most want their prescriber to understand.

Bridge the conversation: Encourage direct discussion with the prescriber when safe and wanted. Offer to help organize questions, request a medication review, send a message with appropriate consent, or attend a joint appointment if the patient wishes and the care team agrees. The aim is shared understanding, informed choices, and continuity of care—not persuading the patient to accept a medication.

Stay within role: Case managers should not independently recommend starting, stopping, changing doses, or combining prescriptions. Help the patient obtain individualized medical advice rather than offering medication instructions. Do not assume the prescriber knows about side effects or barriers unless the information has been communicated.

Escalate safety concerns: Severe or rapidly worsening symptoms, suspected serious adverse drug reactions, overdose, or immediate danger call for urgent clinical or emergency assessment under local protocols; do not wait for a routine appointment or make speaking to the prescriber the only pathway. If the patient feels unsafe with the prescriber, support second opinions, complaints, advocacy, and changes in care without pressure.
