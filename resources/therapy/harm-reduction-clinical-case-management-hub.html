Harm Reduction: Clinical and Case-Manager Resource Hub

Independently authored and curated by Sophia Korb Cohon, PhD

A substantial, practical guide to reducing avoidable harm, building trust, supporting health and maintaining access to care. This is not an employer protocol, treatment order, or replacement for clinical supervision.

Emergency: If someone is unresponsive or has slow or stopped breathing, call 911 and follow dispatch instructions; give naloxone when opioid overdose is suspected and it is available. This page is for education, not real-time emergency direction.

1. Harm reduction in clinical care

Harm reduction aims to reduce preventable injury, overdose, infection and other harms while preserving dignity, agency and access to care. Abstinence, reducing use, safer use, treatment engagement and improved day-to-day functioning can all be meaningful goals; follow the person's priorities and reassess risks together.

Practical application: Ask permission to discuss substance use. Identify what matters most to the person. Use non-stigmatizing language (for example, 'person who uses drugs' rather than labels). Avoid requiring an abstinence commitment before offering medical or social support.

2. Engagement and a first conversation

Begin with immediate safety, basic needs and the person's own goals. Ask what substances they use, context, other medications, recent changes and any past overdose or withdrawal. Do not assume substance identity, strength or current intoxication from appearance.

Practical application: Useful questions: 'What would make the next week safer?' 'What has helped you stay safe before?' 'Would it be okay if I shared a few options?' Reflect ambivalence without confrontation; offer another conversation if the person declines.

3. Overdose prevention and naloxone

Offer naloxone education and access through appropriate local programs. Explain that an opioid overdose is an emergency, that repeat naloxone doses may be necessary according to product directions, and that 911 should be called. If breathing is absent or inadequate, follow dispatcher instructions and training for CPR/rescue breathing.

Practical application: Review signs of overdose, how to access naloxone and how a trusted person could help. Discuss exposure from unexpected opioids in the drug supply, and avoid implying test strips or other screening can guarantee safety. Review emergency help even when someone awakens after naloxone.

4. Safer-use planning and drug checking

Discuss practical risk-reduction options without suggesting that using an unregulated substance is safe. The risks of potency, contamination, polysubstance exposure, impairment and overdose cannot be eliminated. Drug-checking technology can provide limited information; a negative result is not proof of safety.

Practical application: Offer a personalized lower-risk plan, access to local harm-reduction services and nonjudgmental follow-up. Avoid precise drug-use instructions or claims that a specific technique guarantees protection. Confirm current local laws and service availability.

5. Infections, testing and vaccinations

Discuss hepatitis A and B vaccination, hepatitis B and C testing, HIV testing, sexual-health screening, PrEP or PEP evaluation when appropriate, and prompt care for wounds or injection-related infections. Comprehensive syringe services programs can connect people to sterile equipment, safe disposal, naloxone and care.

Practical application: Case managers can support scheduling, transportation, benefits and follow-through. Clinicians should assess symptoms requiring urgent evaluation, including fever, significant swelling, spreading redness, severe pain or signs of systemic illness.

6. Medication and treatment pathways

Medications for opioid use disorder, including buprenorphine and methadone, are evidence-based treatments. Offer low-barrier linkage when feasible; discuss alcohol-use and tobacco-use treatment options based on clinical assessment. Do not delay a requested treatment referral until the person meets a particular motivational milestone.

Practical application: Ask about preferred care setting, cost, insurance, transportation, pharmacies, telehealth, privacy and childcare. Obtain consent before sharing information. A qualified prescriber handles medication selection, dosing, contraindications and withdrawal management.

7. Alcohol, sedatives and withdrawal safety

Alcohol and sedative withdrawal can be medically dangerous; abruptly stopping after sustained heavy use can require monitored care. Combining sedating substances can increase overdose and respiratory-depression risk. Screen for concerning symptoms and arrange urgent medical assessment where indicated.

Practical application: Do not provide unsupervised detoxification protocols. Have a clear escalation route for seizures, severe confusion, unconsciousness, breathing problems or other emergencies. Use supportive, non-punitive follow-up.

8. Pain, mental health and trauma

Pain, anxiety, depression, psychosis, trauma exposure, housing insecurity and substance use can interact. Offer integrated assessment without assuming a single cause. Avoid using substance use alone as a reason to withhold all mental health support.

Practical application: Consider trauma-informed care, collaborative safety planning, culturally responsive communication and referrals for pain management or psychiatric evaluation. Use professional supervision for high-risk or diagnostically complex cases.

9. Housing, food, transportation and benefits

Practical needs are not peripheral: stable housing, food, identification, phone access, insurance, transportation and safe storage of medications can affect risk and engagement.

Practical application: Case-manager steps: ask the person to prioritize two needs; offer warm referrals; confirm eligibility rather than promising services; track consent, referral dates, responsible contacts and whether the connection succeeded.

10. Documentation, privacy and consent

Document the person's stated priorities, material risks assessed, options explained, actions taken, referrals accepted or declined, and follow-up plan. Separate direct observations from interpretations. Avoid stigmatizing phrases and unsupported predictions.

Practical application: Use only an authorized health record for identifying or sensitive content. Follow applicable confidentiality requirements, including substance-use records where relevant. Do not enter protected information into public CPL tools or forms.

11. Case-manager workflow: a 15-minute check-in

1. Ask permission and identify a priority. 2. Check immediate safety and urgent medical concerns. 3. Explore available supports and barriers. 4. Offer two or three options without coercion. 5. Agree on one concrete next step. 6. Arrange a realistic follow-up.

Practical application: Example nonidentifying note structure: 'Person identified [goal]. Discussed [options and limitations]. Offered [referral/resource]; response [accepted/declined/considering]. Next step [action, responsible role, review time].'

12. Intern supervision and escalation

Review scope of practice, differential diagnosis, indications for immediate medical evaluation, suicide risk when relevant, motivational interviewing, referral decisions, and state or organization rules with a qualified supervisor.

Practical application: Bring de-identified cases to supervision. Ask: 'What am I assuming? What makes this medically urgent? Did I offer a choice? What is outside my competency? How will I know the referral actually connected?'

13. Family and supporter conversations

With appropriate permission, teach overdose response, nonjudgmental support, emergency plans and the difference between offering support and monitoring or punishment. Consent and confidentiality come first.

Practical application: Do not promise families access to confidential information. Help supporters identify their own boundaries, emergency resources and realistic ways to assist.

14. Implementation and quality improvement

Track access rather than only abstinence outcomes: naloxone access, completed referrals, vaccination/testing linkage, continuity of medications, client-defined quality-of-life goals and reduction of avoidable harm.

Practical application: Use aggregated, nonidentifying data only with proper authorization. Review whether language, hours, transport, stigma or restrictive program rules create avoidable barriers.
