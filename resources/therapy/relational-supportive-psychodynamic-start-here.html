A Working Relational Formulation

Move beyond a list of symptoms. Ask what the patient longs for from other people, what they expect will happen if they need or trust someone, how they protect themselves from that anticipated outcome, and what the protection costs them. Then watch for the same sequence in current relationships and, when clinically useful, in the treatment relationship.

Protective strategy
Withdrawal, control, pleasing, attack, intellectualization, self-sufficiency, testing, idealization, devaluation, or another adaptation may once have been intelligent protection.
Current cost
The same strategy may now narrow intimacy, agency, affect tolerance, flexibility, or the ability to receive help.

Practice Frame

  • Clarify what kind of therapy you are doing. “Supportive” should not mean aimless conversation.
  • Track the patient's goals, current functioning, recurrent relationship patterns, and the therapeutic relationship itself when relevant.
  • Match interpretation depth to ego strength, affect tolerance, reflective capacity, and the treatment frame.
  • Use clarification before confrontation and confrontation before deep interpretation when more direct approaches are clinically appropriate.
  • Avoid turning every interaction into transference analysis.
  • Support reality testing, agency, adaptive defenses, relationships, work, and daily functioning.
  • Attend to countertransference as clinical information, not as proof about the patient.
  • Maintain boundaries that are predictable, explainable, and proportionate to the setting.

Use the Relationship Without Making Everything About the Relationship

Notice shifts in closeness, trust, compliance, irritation, idealization, withdrawal, fear of disappointing the therapist, and expectations of rejection or control. Explore these when they illuminate the patient’s broader pattern. Do not treat every disagreement, silence, or practical problem as transference.
