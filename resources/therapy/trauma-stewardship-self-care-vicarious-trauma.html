Trauma Stewardship: Caring for Yourself While Caring for Others

Original learning resource · Sophia Korb Cohon, PhD

For therapists, interns, case managers, peer supporters, supervisors and others who regularly encounter distress and trauma. Caring for yourself is part of sustainable, ethical work—not a test of toughness.

Understanding the impact

Vicarious trauma can involve changes in beliefs, expectations or sense of safety after repeated empathic exposure to others' trauma. Secondary traumatic stress describes trauma-like reactions linked to indirect exposure. Burnout is associated with chronic workplace stress and may include exhaustion, cynicism and diminished effectiveness. These concepts overlap but are not interchangeable; none is a diagnosis made by this worksheet.

Recognizing early signs

  • Difficulty switching off after work, intrusive images, disrupted sleep or feeling unusually on edge.
  • Cynicism, withdrawal, irritability, hopelessness or a shrinking sense of compassion.
  • Difficulty concentrating, increased errors or trouble maintaining professional boundaries.
  • Physical tension, fatigue, headaches or missed basic needs.

Notice patterns rather than treating a single difficult day as a diagnosis.

A short transition practice

  1. Notice: Ask yourself, “What am I carrying from this contact?” Name the feeling without sharing confidential details.
  2. Orient: Feel your feet on the floor, look around, release your shoulders and take a comfortable breath.
  3. Separate: Identify what is yours to do professionally and what is outside your control.
  4. Connect: Arrange appropriate consultation, a brief peer check-in, food, water or a real break.
  5. Close: End with one achievable action for the next shift or day.

Grounding is optional; if breathing or body-focused strategies are uncomfortable, choose another sensory or movement-based reset.

Trauma stewardship in daily practice

Trauma stewardship means relating intentionally to the impact of bearing witness to suffering. It includes boundaries, reflection, social connection, restorative activities, thoughtful workload choices and attention to the systems in which care happens. Self-care is not merely an after-hours checklist and does not make unsafe working conditions acceptable.

  • Protect realistic breaks, meals, time off and a manageable caseload when possible.
  • Seek supervision for ethically complex cases; use de-identified examples and approved consultation channels.
  • Set boundaries around after-hours communication and crisis coverage consistent with professional responsibilities.
  • Notice what remains meaningful or energizing; connection and purpose can coexist with distress.
  • Use culturally relevant, accessible forms of rest and community support rather than a single prescribed wellness routine.

What supervisors and organizations must do

Wellbeing is a shared responsibility. Teams should provide safe staffing, predictable consultation, psychologically safer reporting, training, access to confidential support, and reasonable accommodations and leave processes. Do not substitute resilience training for reducing preventable workload hazards.

Supervision prompts: What exposure or workload pattern is accumulating? What resource or staffing issue needs escalation? What can be redistributed? What is one boundary we can actually protect?

A personal stewardship check-in

  • One early signal I notice: ____________________
  • One boundary worth protecting: ____________________
  • A colleague, supervisor or support I can contact: ____________________
  • One restorative activity that works for me: ____________________
  • One workplace condition needing a team response: ____________________
  • Review this plan on: ____________________

Use this as a private, non-identifying reflection. Do not place client information in notes, screenshots or public forms.

When additional support is needed

Persistent intrusive reactions, impaired functioning, escalating substance use or severe distress deserve a confidential professional assessment rather than relying on self-care alone. Use employee assistance benefits when appropriate, an independent clinician, or other trusted support. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, seek emergency services; in the U.S., call or text 988 for suicide or crisis support.
