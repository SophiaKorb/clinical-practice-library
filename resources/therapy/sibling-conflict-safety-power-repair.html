Sibling Conflict – Safety, Power & Repair

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Assessment and intervention companion

Use this companion when repeated sibling conflict is a treatment target. The first clinical task is to distinguish ordinary reciprocal conflict from intimidation, coercion, abuse, or a meaningful power imbalance.

1. First Decision: Mutual Conflict or Safety/Power Problem?

Assess injury, threats, fear, coercion, sexual behavior, weapon use, strangulation/choking, stalking within the home, property destruction, developmental/cognitive differences, and whether one sibling changes behavior primarily to avoid the other.

Do not use mutual-conflict language when one child is unsafe or afraid.

Safety planning, caregiver protection, separate assessment, mandated-reporting analysis, or a higher level of care may take precedence over family problem-solving.

2. Assess the Pattern, Not Just the Latest Incident

Clarify antecedents, high-risk times and spaces, access to valued items, transitions, caregiver availability, reinforcement after conflict, sibling alliances, differential rules, and recovery patterns.

Identify exceptions. When does the same pair manage the same demand more successfully?

Screen for ADHD/executive dysfunction, autism, learning or language differences, trauma, mood symptoms, sleep problems, pain, and family stress when clinically relevant.

3. Formulation Questions

What function does the conflict serve for each child?

Which skills are overloaded: inhibition, flexibility, communication, frustration tolerance, perspective-taking, repair, or help-seeking?

Which environmental conditions repeatedly set the conflict up?

Are caregiver interpretations increasing escalation?

Are adults inadvertently rewarding dramatic escalation, triangulation, or retaliation?

4. Parent/Caregiver Coaching

Coach neutral behavioral descriptions rather than fixed sibling roles.

Use predictable boundaries around private space, possessions, devices, and physical contact.

Increase adult proximity during predictable high-risk periods when needed.

Teach caregivers to separate first, regulate second, and problem-solve later.

Avoid demanding immediate apologies or equal consequences when responsibility and support needs differ.

5. Repair Work

Repair can include acknowledgment of impact, restitution, boundary restoration, changed behavior, and a prevention plan.

An apology can be part of repair but should not substitute for it.

Use separate reflection before joint work when arousal, blame, or developmental differences would make a shared conversation unproductive.

6. When Joint Work Is Contraindicated

Do not require joint mediation when there is coercive control, credible fear, repeated serious aggression, sexual behavior, significant exploitation, or when one sibling cannot speak freely in the other’s presence.

Individual work, caregiver-only work, safety planning, or external services may be more appropriate.

7. Documentation Focus

Document concrete behavior, safety findings, power differences, caregiver response, functional hypotheses as hypotheses, interventions used, and the rationale for joint versus separate work.

Avoid documenting moralized labels as if they were clinical findings.
