Trauma & PTSD

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Original shareable tools for treatment selection, engagement, and trauma-focused psychotherapy support.

Clinician Tool: Trauma Treatment Decision Conversation

What is the treatment target? Clarify whether the primary problem is PTSD, grief, depression, panic, substance use, dissociation, current danger, relationship violence, sleep, or another condition. Trauma history alone does not determine a protocol.

What does the patient want to change? Ask about nightmares, avoidance, guilt, fear, intrusive memories, disconnection, functioning, relationships, work, parenting, or other goals.

What treatment options fit? Discuss evidence-based trauma-focused options appropriate to the setting and clinician competence. Explain differences in structure, writing, talking, exposure, cognitive work, homework, duration, and patient preference.

What might interfere? Consider acute safety, current violence, severe instability, substance withdrawal, psychosis, cognitive limitations, medical issues, dissociation that disrupts participation, transportation, privacy, and schedule.

What is the plan if distress rises? Define coping, contact expectations, crisis pathways, and what counts as a need for reassessment rather than assuming all distress means treatment should stop.

Clinician Tool: Stabilization Without Getting Stuck There

Ask what skill or condition is actually required before trauma-focused work can proceed. Teach that skill specifically and reassess. Examples might include enough safety to attend sessions, ability to remain oriented, a workable crisis plan, or reduction of severe intoxication during treatment.

Avoid using “not ready” as an indefinite label when the actual barrier can be named and treated.

Clinician Tool: Avoidance Check

When treatment stalls, ask whether the patient is avoiding trauma reminders, whether the clinician is avoiding trauma work because it feels uncomfortable, or whether the current treatment is appropriately addressing a different priority.

Patient Handout: Trauma Treatment Is Not “Tell Everything All at Once”

Good trauma treatment is structured. You should understand what the treatment is trying to do, what will happen in sessions, what practice may happen between sessions, and how you and your clinician will handle difficult reactions.

Trauma-focused treatment often asks you to approach memories, meanings, feelings, or situations that you have been avoiding. That can be hard, but it should not be a surprise attack or a test of toughness.

You can ask questions about the treatment, discuss preferences, and say when something is confusing. Feeling distress during trauma treatment does not automatically mean the treatment is harmful, but safety and clinical judgment still matter.

Clinical Boundary

Current danger, suicidal risk, severe substance withdrawal, delirium, mania, psychosis, or medical instability may change treatment priorities. Trauma-informed care and supportive stabilization are not the same as evidence-based PTSD treatment.
