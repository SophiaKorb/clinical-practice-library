Original shareable tools for deciding where to intervene and for making parent work concrete.

Clinician Tool: Where Does the Problem Live?

Before choosing an intervention, ask whether the main leverage point is in the child, the caregiver-child interaction, the family system, the school/environment, or several places at once.

A child may need direct skill building. A caregiver may need coaching. The environment may need to change. A family may need clearer roles and repair. A school barrier may require educational intervention rather than more office-based insight.

Clinician Tool: One Behavior at a Time

Describe one behavior precisely.

  • What happens before it?
  • What does the child do?
  • What happens immediately afterward?
  • What does the behavior help the child obtain, avoid, delay, communicate, regulate, or control?
  • What adult response may accidentally strengthen the pattern?
  • What skill does the child still need?
  • What environmental change would make success easier?

Choose a replacement behavior the child can realistically perform. Reinforce the replacement more clearly and consistently than the problem behavior.

Clinician Tool: Parent Coaching Script

Start with observation: “Here is what I noticed.”

Add function: “This may be helping your child escape, get reassurance, gain control, or communicate overload.”

Choose one adult change: “This week, let's change what happens immediately before or after the behavior.”

Practice the wording in session. Do not send parents home with a concept they cannot enact.

Clinician Tool: Child and Teen Voice

Ask the young person privately when appropriate: What do adults misunderstand? What helps when things are hard? What makes help worse? What would make therapy more useful? What do you wish adults would stop doing?

Include the answer in treatment planning rather than treating participation as a reward for compliance.

Family Handout: Make the Next 10 Minutes Easier

When a child is overwhelmed, angry, shut down, or refusing, adults often try to solve the entire problem immediately. That can add more language and pressure at the moment the child has the least capacity.

First make the next 10 minutes easier. Reduce unnecessary words. State the next step clearly. Offer limited choices when choices are possible. Keep boundaries calm and predictable. Save long explanations for later.

After the situation is calmer, ask what made the task hard, what helped, and what skill or support is needed next time.

The goal is not to remove every demand. The goal is to make the demand understandable, doable, and connected to a skill the child is learning.

Clinical Boundary

Parent coaching should be adapted for developmental level, disability, communication, trauma, caregiver capacity, and actual environmental demands. Do not interpret inability, overload, language difficulty, or executive-function failure as willful defiance without assessment.
