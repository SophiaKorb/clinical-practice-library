Common Misconceptions About Therapy

Therapy terms are often reduced to slogans. These twelve clarifications explain what different approaches actually mean—and what they don't.

Misconception 1

CBT is just positive thinking

More accurate: CBT examines how thoughts, behavior, emotions and context interact. It does not require someone to deny real difficulties.

Misconception 2

ACT means accepting mistreatment

More accurate: Acceptance refers to making room for internal experiences. It does not mean approving injustice or remaining in unsafe situations.

Misconception 3

DBT is only for one diagnosis

More accurate: DBT was developed for particular high-risk problems, but DBT-informed skills are used in several settings. A few skills do not constitute comprehensive DBT.

Misconception 4

Exposure means forcing people to face their worst fear

More accurate: Effective exposure is collaborative, purposefully designed and attentive to safety. Flooding or surprise exposure is not the default.

Misconception 5

ERP works only if anxiety goes down immediately

More accurate: Learning may occur without rapid distress reduction. The target includes reducing compulsions and changing responses to uncertainty.

Misconception 6

Motivational interviewing convinces people to comply

More accurate: MI aims to explore a person's values and ambivalence while protecting autonomy, not manipulate agreement.

Misconception 7

Psychodynamic therapy just blames childhood

More accurate: It examines patterns, emotions and relationships in the present as well as relevant history.

Misconception 8

Supportive psychotherapy is just friendly conversation

More accurate: It can involve purposeful formulation, stabilization, coping support and clinical monitoring.

Misconception 9

Family therapy means parents caused the problem

More accurate: A systems perspective examines interaction and context; it should not assign blame or ignore coercion and safety.

Misconception 10

Trauma therapy always requires retelling every detail

More accurate: Trauma-focused interventions differ. Assessment, choice, pacing and the specific protocol matter.

Misconception 11

All therapies are basically interchangeable

More accurate: They overlap, but have different aims, evidence and training requirements. Fit depends on the concern and the person.

Misconception 12

A case manager or intern can use any technique after reading about it

More accurate: Knowing a method is not the same as being competent or authorized to deliver it; scope and supervision matter.

Three questions to keep asking

What is this approach designed to change? What evidence supports it for this person's concern? What training, consent and supervision are needed?
