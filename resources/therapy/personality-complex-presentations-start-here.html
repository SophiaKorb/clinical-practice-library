Formulate the Repeating Sequence

Track the interpersonal trigger, the meaning the patient gives it, the affect that follows, the protective strategy, the other person’s response, and the consequence that confirms the original expectation. This turns labels such as “manipulative,” “splitting,” or “attention seeking” into behavior that can actually be understood and treated.

Clinical Frame

  • Establish an explicit treatment frame: goals, contact expectations, crisis pathways, attendance, consultation, and boundaries.
  • Avoid making every intense affective state into an emergency while still assessing actual risk carefully.
  • Validate emotion and history without reinforcing destructive behavior, splitting, or unworkable interpersonal strategies.
  • Keep agency with the patient. Treatment should increase functioning, relationships, self-direction, and a life outside treatment.
  • Use coherent case formulation rather than cycling among labels based on the crisis of the week.
  • Revisit diagnosis when mood episodes, psychosis, trauma, neurodevelopmental differences, substance use, or medical factors better explain part of the presentation.

Specialist-Model Boundary

DBT, MBT, TFP, Schema Therapy, and other structured personality treatments require model-specific training and materials. A generalist guideline or GPM orientation is not a substitute for specialist competence when the case requires a specialist model or higher level of care.
