Clinical Practice · Practical Toolkit

Psychosis, Serious Mental Illness & Rehabilitation

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Original shareable tools for collaborative coping, functional recovery, and relapse planning.

Clinician Tool: Experience–Meaning–Response Map

Experience: What did the patient hear, see, sense, notice, or believe happened?

Meaning: What explanation makes sense to the patient? How certain do they feel? What alternative explanations, if any, can they hold alongside the main one?

Emotional effect: What fear, shame, anger, confusion, excitement, or urgency follows?

Response: What does the patient do next?

Functional effect: What happens to sleep, relationships, work, school, self-care, safety, and daily routine?

The treatment target may be distress, sleep, coping, functioning, interpretation, or behavior even when the belief itself does not change quickly.

Clinician Tool: Collaborative Reality Testing

Do not begin with “That is not real.” Start with curiosity. Ask what evidence the patient notices, what evidence does not fit, whether certainty changes across situations, and what else might explain part of the experience.

The goal is not to win an argument. The goal is to reduce distress, expand options, and improve safe functioning.

Clinician Tool: Relapse Signature

Identify early warning signs before a major episode. These may include reduced sleep, increased suspiciousness, social withdrawal, unusual meaning-making, increased substance use, missed medication, agitation, reduced eating, or decline in self-care.

For each sign, define an action: who to contact, what routine to restore, what substances to avoid, whether medication review is needed, and what would trigger urgent evaluation.

Clinician Tool: Rehabilitation Goal

Choose a concrete life role such as school, work, friendships, family participation, transportation, cooking, budgeting, exercise, or community involvement. Break the role into skills, supports, and environmental barriers. Symptom improvement and functional recovery should not be treated as the same outcome.

Patient Handout: When Something Feels Completely True and Frightening

You do not have to settle every question immediately. Slow down before acting. Notice what you experienced, what meaning your mind gave it, how certain you feel, and what action you are about to take.

Ask whether there is another explanation you can hold as a possibility, even if it is not your main explanation. Choose the next action based on safety and your goals rather than only on the strongest feeling of the moment.

Clinical Boundary

New or rapidly worsening psychosis, command hallucinations, severe disorganization, catatonia, delirium, mania, intoxication or withdrawal, inability to care for basic needs, or imminent risk may require urgent medical or psychiatric evaluation.
