Anxiety, Exposure & OCD

Curated by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A formulation-first guide for deciding whether the problem is fear, avoidance, compulsive certainty-seeking, or some combination, and what treatment needs to target next.

Use This Shelf For

Avoidance, phobic anxiety, panic, social/performance anxiety, intolerance of uncertainty, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and exposure-based treatment planning.

Start With the Maintenance Loop

Do not begin with an exposure hierarchy. First identify what keeps the problem useful to the nervous system: a feared prediction, an urge to escape or neutralize, short-term relief, and the longer-term cost of teaching the person that the trigger or uncertainty required protection.

Choose the Target

  • Fear with avoidance: increase voluntary contact with the feared situation while reducing escape and unnecessary safety behavior.
  • OCD: identify the obsession or uncertainty and the full ritual system, including reassurance, checking, researching, confession, mental review, neutralizing, and avoidance.
  • Panic: clarify feared sensations and feared consequences; consider interoceptive work when clinically appropriate.
  • Social anxiety: look for self-monitoring, impression management, rehearsal, post-event review, and subtle safety behaviors, not only avoided situations.
  • Real-world danger or instability: address the actual problem. Exposure is not a method for teaching someone to tolerate preventable danger.

Core Principle

Exposure is not “make the patient anxious until it goes away.” Define the feared outcome, the behavior that prevents new learning, and what the patient is practicing instead. Anxiety may rise, fall, or remain present. The treatment target is greater behavioral freedom and more accurate learning, not a particular distress curve.

Before You Design an Exposure

  • What exactly does the patient predict?
  • What do they do before, during, or after the trigger to obtain safety or certainty?
  • Which of those behaviors are clinically unnecessary, and which are reasonable accommodations or actual safety practices?
  • What would the patient choose to do differently if fear or doubt had less authority?
  • What would count as useful learning even if anxiety does not decrease during the exercise?

Clinical ERP Checkpoints

  • Identify obsessions, triggers, compulsions, reassurance, avoidance, and covert rituals.
  • Separate valued accommodation from behavior that maintains the fear/ritual cycle.
  • Build exposures around inhibitory/new learning and meaningful approach behavior.
  • Specify response prevention clearly, including mental rituals and reassurance-seeking when relevant.
  • Avoid flooding, humiliation, surprise exposure, or “prove you can do it” contests.
  • Coordinate family/partner involvement when accommodation is part of the maintenance cycle.
  • Use OCD-specific assessment and specialty consultation when presentation is severe, atypical, highly impairing, or diagnostically uncertain.
