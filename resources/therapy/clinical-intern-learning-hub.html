Simple access. Deep formulation.

A barrier-to-support chart is a useful front door, not an explanation of the whole clinical presentation. Maintain differential hypotheses, relationship and cultural context, setting conditions, assessment boundaries, and patient voice even when the intervention itself is simple.

For supervisees and supervisors · fictional cases only

Six assignments to complete and review

Each assignment has a concrete product and a supervisor review standard. Complete one at a time with the fictional case or role-play, marking missing information rather than supplying plausible details. A supervisor should review the product against the stated criteria, identify what meets standard, and request a specific revision only when needed. These exercises do not authorize independent assessment, treatment, crisis disposition, or use of identifiable client material.

Privacy and scope: Do not upload or enter protected health information, real case details, or identifying quotations. Use invented scenarios only. For real safety concerns, follow the organization's immediate response and supervision procedures rather than using these exercises.

1. Write an observation without guessing motives

Scenario: A 12-year-old hears three instructions in a noisy classroom, pushes a worksheet aside, and says “no.”

  1. Write a short factual account using only the stated setting, exact words, and observable actions. If support offered or timing is unknown, mark it “not provided” instead of inventing it.
  2. List three statements that would be interpretations rather than observations.
  3. Identify two pieces of missing context.

Submit: A 35–75-word factual observation, three inference corrections, two follow-up questions, and a list of unavailable facts.

Supervisor checks: No invented support, response, timing, motive, or diagnosis; clear distinction between observed events and unanswered questions.

Use the ABC Observation Guide

2. Compare competing explanations

Scenario: The same fictional student begins work after directions are modeled, but still needs help writing responses.

  1. Develop at least three hypotheses involving comprehension, output demands, initiation, or environment.
  2. For each, record one supporting observation, one contrary possibility, and one information gap.
  3. Propose an accessible, non-diagnostic observation or support trial and state what it cannot establish.

Submit: A three-row formulation matrix and a 150-word justification of your next step.

Supervisor checks: Genuine alternatives, no diagnosis from one response, developmental and cultural context, appropriate referrals and limits.

Review assessment access and validity

3. Demonstrate collaborative interviewing

Scenario: In a role-play, a fictional adult says they do not want to discuss a difficult topic.

  1. Write a five-turn dialogue including an open question, reflection, permission check, and two genuine choices.
  2. Rehearse aloud with a supervisor or peer, allowing a refusal or pause.
  3. Identify one leading question and rewrite it as a neutral one.

Submit: Five-turn script, brief reflection on the role-play, and your corrected question.

Supervisor checks: Consent, autonomy, accurate reflection, conversational pacing, privacy explanation, and no pressure to disclose.

Review communication supports

4. Rehearse a safety handoff

Scenario: A fictional client tells a support worker “I cannot do this anymore,” with no further context. The designated clinician is elsewhere in the building.

  1. Write the next two direct, nonjudgmental clarification questions consistent with your trained role.
  2. Role-play promptly notifying the designated clinician and activating the local urgent pathway if indicated.
  3. Draft a handoff using only the quoted statement and known role/context. Put “pending confirmation” beside the client’s response, recipient, time, instructions, and receipt; in a real handoff these must be established immediately, not assumed.

Submit: A brief role-play script and a 60–110-word handoff template clearly marking unknowns and confirmation requirements.

Supervisor checks: Prompt escalation, no secrecy promise, no unsupported independent risk category or disposition, clear ownership and closed-loop handoff.

Review safety and continuity guidance

5. Draft a measurable collaborative care plan

Scenario: A fictional adult wants to resume one valued weekly activity but reports inconsistent energy and distress.

  1. State one client-defined functional goal and a baseline using only supplied fictional information; if absent, mark baseline “to establish.”
  2. Offer two feasible supports with access and preference considerations, and choose only provisionally.
  3. Define one measurable indicator, review date or interval, and a condition requiring reconsideration.

Submit: One-page goal, baseline, options, measure, review plan, and consent/scope note.

Supervisor checks: Goal is meaningful to client, measurable without invented data, realistic, evidence-informed and within supervised role.

Review the treatment-planning menu

6. Revise a clinical note using supervisor feedback

Scenario: A fictional note says “Client was manipulative, refused therapy, and improved after intervention,” with no observations or outcome measure.

  1. Create a brief, evidence-limited SOAP-style or role-appropriate note skeleton. Retain the fact that a statement was supplied in the faulty fictional note, but do not present its claims as independently verified. Use explicit “not documented” placeholders for observations, interventions, responses and outcome.
  2. Identify four missing facts, what source could verify them, and whether the existing record permits a factual correction. Do not invent quotations or attest to events you did not observe.
  3. Mark each sentence as observed, reported, inferred, or planned.

Submit: A short clearly labeled note skeleton (no prescribed word count), four missing facts with verification sources, and an observation/report/inference/plan key.

Supervisor checks: Correct attribution of the faulty note’s claims, no unverified improvement or invented intervention, and clearly flagged documentation gaps requiring supervisor or original-author review.

Use the documentation lab

Supervisor completion standard: Record “meets standard,” “revise and resubmit,” or “outside assigned scope” for each product. Discuss one specific strength and one revision; completion means demonstrated reasoning, not autonomous clinical authorization.

From observation to responsible care

Five steps for clinical reasoning

Each stop includes something to practice in observation, role play, case discussion, or approved supervised clinical work.

1

Notice

Describe what actually happened, without assigning motive or diagnosis.

2

Formulate

Consider multiple explanations, developmental context, and what information is missing.

3

Choose

Match an evidence-informed approach to client goals, access needs, and training.

4

Practice

Deliver with permission, collaboration, supervision, and appropriate fidelity.

5

Review

Evaluate outcomes, safety, implementation, functioning, and patient experience.

Observe

Notice and ask

Practice neutral descriptions, active listening, professional boundaries, and supervised case discussions. Do not perform independent diagnosis or treatment without the required credentials and oversight.

Reflect

Reason and rehearse

Compare differential hypotheses, role-play informed consent and risk conversations, practice measurements, and deliver techniques only within authorized supervision and competence.

Integrate

Connect and refine

Connect guidelines to individualized formulation, therapeutic relationship, fidelity, response to treatment, referral thresholds, and documentation quality.

Worked example

Same behavior, several plausible explanations

Observable: A student sits through the instructions but does not start written work. “They won't try” is an interpretation, not an observation.

Language or comprehension

Did the directions make sense? Compare spoken directions, written steps, and a modeled first example.

Skill or output demand

Is the student able to generate the answer orally but not in writing? Examine writing, motor, and academic skill demands.

Initiation, anxiety, or overload

Is the first step unclear, error risk distressing, or the environment overloading? Compare conditions instead of inferring intent.

Clinical reasoning is the product. An access trial tells us whether changing a demand improves performance in that context. It does not, by itself, establish a diagnosis, disprove a learning disorder, or remove the need for explicit instruction.

What a good case discussion should preserve

Clinical quality

  • What do we observe, and in which settings?
  • What are at least two competing explanations?
  • What do the patient and family think is happening?
  • What are the risk, medical, cultural, and developmental considerations?
  • Which guideline, training, or mechanism supports our plan?

Implementation quality

  • What can we change now to improve access?
  • What needs direct instruction or formal assessment?
  • Which action is within my role and level of supervision?
  • What response would cause us to change course?
  • How will we measure benefit, burden, and functioning?
Training boundary

External links are educational resources, not authorization to practice independently. Suicide screening does not replace a clinical risk assessment or local response protocol. Structured diagnostic instruments, exposure treatments, trauma-focused protocols, and other specialized practices may require additional training, licensing, supervision, and fidelity monitoring.
