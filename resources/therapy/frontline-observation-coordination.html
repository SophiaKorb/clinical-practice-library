Three fictional cases for team discussion

Case 1: the client declines a worksheet

A client says “No” and pushes a worksheet away. Staff describe this as defiance. The worksheet contains several dense paragraphs and asks for an emotional narrative.

Try within the plan: ask whether the person wants a different format, a pause, an explanation, or to decline. Offer one concrete choice without coercion. Record what was offered and what the person chose.

Bring to the clinician: whether this happens with other tasks; comprehension versus output demands; fear, trauma cues, preference, and rapport. A different format helping does not prove the cause.

Review: participation, accurate communication, prompting, distress, and whether refusal remained possible.

Case 2: the referral exists, but care has not begun

A client received a specialty referral two months ago. The receiving office has no authorized records and the client does not know whom to call.

Coordinate: with consent, identify the receiving contact, required documents, payer/access barriers, and the next action owner. Confirm receipt and scheduling; a sent fax is not a completed referral.

Escalate: new risk, medical deterioration, or a time-sensitive need to the clinical team. Do not promise availability or interpret medical findings outside your role.

Review: appointment completed, recommendation returned, and the client understands the next step in an accessible format.

Case 3: a caregiver wants you to make the child comply

The child becomes overwhelmed when instructions arrive rapidly. The caregiver asks staff to remove a preferred activity until the child obeys.

Observe: task demands, regulation, safety, caregiver stress, and what happens before and after escalation. Do not independently introduce a coercive behavior plan.

Support: follow the authorized plan, model a calm single instruction and accessible first step when appropriate, and involve the clinician in revising expectations and supports.

Safety: concerns about abuse, intimidation, or retaliation need safeguarding assessment and applicable procedures; do not treat them as ordinary mutual conflict.

Closed-loop handoff sheet

Print a blank copy. Use approved documentation systems for identifiable care information.

Question and authorization

What decision or service is needed? Who authorized communication, with whom, and what may be shared?

Observed barrier

What facts, direct reports, dates, and access needs matter?

Next action owner

Who does what, by when? How will receipt be confirmed?

Loop closure

Was service received? What did the client understand? What remains unresolved and who follows up?

Advanced supervision layer: separate roles from educational level

A degree alone does not define permitted practice. Verify job duties, credentials, local policy, training, supervision, and delegated tasks. Staff may collect observations and coordinate supports without independently diagnosing, interpreting restricted tests, or delivering specialized protocols.

Discuss an observation using: fact → context → competing hypotheses → scope-appropriate action → outcome → uncertainty. Ask the supervisor what change requires escalation and how quickly.

NCTSN workforce resources provide trauma-informed training context. ASHA communication guidance provides access context. The handoff structure and fictional cases here are original CPL teaching tools.
