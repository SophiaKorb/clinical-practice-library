Clinical Practice · Practical Toolkit

Relational, Supportive & Psychodynamic Practice

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Original shareable tools for purposeful supportive and relational psychotherapy.

Clinician Tool: Give Supportive Therapy a Job

At the start of treatment, define what supportive work is meant to improve. Possible targets include reality testing, affect tolerance, self-esteem, adaptive coping, relationship functioning, work, daily structure, treatment adherence, grief, identity, or the ability to use support without becoming overwhelmed.

“Supportive” should describe the treatment stance and goals, not the absence of a formulation.

Clinician Tool: Repeating Relationship Pattern

Choose one recurring interpersonal problem. Ask what the patient expects from other people, what they fear will happen, how they protect themselves, and how that protective strategy affects the other person.

The clinical task is not to blame the patient for the pattern. It is to make the sequence visible enough that new choices become possible.

Clinician Tool: Clarify Before You Interpret

When meaning is uncertain, begin by clarifying what happened and what the patient experienced. Then identify the tension or contradiction that may be worth examining. Interpret more deeply only when the patient has enough reflective capacity and affect tolerance to use the interpretation rather than simply feel exposed, corrected, or shamed.

Clinician Tool: Use the Therapy Relationship Carefully

When something important happens between patient and clinician, describe it tentatively and connect it to the treatment goal. Avoid turning every disagreement, silence, late arrival, or emotion into transference material.

Clinician Tool: Countertransference Check

Notice the clinician's pull to rescue, punish, withdraw, argue, overexplain, prove usefulness, bend boundaries, or become unusually passive. Treat that reaction as information to examine, not as evidence that the patient objectively “is” whatever the clinician feels.

Patient Handout: Notice the Pattern Without Turning It Into Blame

Sometimes the same relationship problem happens with different people. That does not mean everything is your fault. It may mean you learned a way of protecting yourself that once made sense and now has costs.

The goal is not to erase your history. It is to give you more choices in the present.

Clinical Boundary

Supportive and relational psychotherapy should not become a reason to avoid evidence-based treatment when a specific disorder has a better-supported intervention. Use formulation and treatment goals to decide what this approach is doing.
