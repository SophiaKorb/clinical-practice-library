Clear tools for hard moments.

Plain language. Fewer steps. Use what works.

________________

USE THE WHOLE PICTURE

Use this when you need to decide what to do and emotion is strong enough to narrow your attention.

WHY IT HELPS

Big feelings zoom in. Thinking can zoom out. Use both. You do not need to calm down completely before making a good decision.

DO THIS

1. Facts: What do I actually know?

2. Emotion: What am I feeling, and what does it make me want to do?

3. Signal: What might this emotion be detecting or protecting?

4. Goal: What matters beyond the next few minutes?

5. Uncertainty: What do I not know yet?

6. Choice: What action best fits the facts, risks, values, and goal together?

WATCH OUT

Calm is not the same as correct. Strong emotion can be accurate. A rational-sounding explanation can also be avoidance.

FACTS, STORY, GUESS

Use this when what happened and what it means have started to feel like the same thing.

WHY IT HELPS

What happened, what you think it means, and what you think will happen next are not the same thing. Separate them before you act.

WORK THROUGH IT

OBSERVATION

What could a camera, recording, or neutral witness verify?

INTERPRETATION

What meaning am I assigning to it?

PREDICTION

What do I think happens next?

CONFIDENCE

How sure am I, from 0 to 100 percent?

ALTERNATIVES

What else could explain the same observations?

CONSEQUENCE

What happens if I act as though my interpretation is certainly true?

ACTION

What response makes sense given both the evidence and the uncertainty?

WATCH OUT

The goal is not to replace a negative story with a cheerful one. “I do not know yet” is a legitimate conclusion.

CHANGE WHAT YOU DO NEXT

Use this when an emotion is pushing you toward a behavior that may make the situation worse.

WHY IT HELPS

A feeling can be real and still push you toward a bad move. You do not have to change the feeling first. Change what you do next.

USE IT WHEN

  • The urge is based on a likely misread of the situation, OR
  • The feeling makes sense, but the default action conflicts with your goal.

DO THIS

1. Name the urge.

2. What is the short-term payoff if you follow it?

3. What is the delayed cost?

4. Choose the smallest behavior that moves toward the outcome you actually want.

5. Do enough of it to create new information.

6. Reassess.

WATCH OUT

This is not “do the opposite of whatever you feel.” Protective emotions sometimes deserve to be followed. Do not reverse a safety behavior merely to prove you can tolerate discomfort.

________________

THIS IS HAPPENING. NOW WHAT?

Use this for something that has already happened, or a constraint that is currently real, when repeatedly thinking “this cannot be happening” is consuming energy without changing it.

WHY IT HELPS

Something can be awful and still be true. Fighting the fact that it happened can drain energy you need for the next move.

DO THIS

1. State the fact without adding a judgment.

2. State what you hate, oppose, or wish were different.

3. Separate:

“This exists.”

“This is acceptable.”

These are not the same statement.

4. Ask what is still changeable.

5. Put effort there.

WATCH OUT

Acceptance does not mean approval, forgiveness, reconciliation, passivity, or staying in danger. Nobody gets to use this concept to make you easier to mistreat.

________________

GET YOUR BRAIN BACK ONLINE

Use this when arousal is so high that useful thinking is becoming difficult.

WHY IT HELPS

When you're flooded, thinking gets harder. Bring the intensity down first. Then deal with the actual problem.

TRY ONE

  • Lengthen the exhale.
  • Brief vigorous movement, if medically safe.
  • Cool the face or hands.
  • Reduce sensory input.
  • Orient to what you can see and hear around you.
  • Change posture or location.
  • Use a grounding sequence you already know.

THEN ASK

Can I think usefully now?

If yes, move to problem-solving.

If no, try another state-change method or reduce demands further.

WATCH OUT

This is not the solution to the relationship, work problem, trauma, discrimination, housing problem, or unpaid bill. It is a way to buy cognitive bandwidth before dealing with the actual problem.

________________

GET THROUGH THE NEXT BIT

Use this when acting immediately is likely to make things worse and the problem cannot be fully solved right now.

THE JOB

Get through a defined interval without adding avoidable damage.

DO THIS

1. Define the interval.

Ten minutes? One hour? Tonight?

2. Name the behavior you are trying not to make worse.

3. Reduce access to the most dangerous or irreversible option.

4. Lower arousal enough to regain some choice.

5. Use distraction or soothing deliberately.

6. Decide when problem-solving resumes.

A USEFUL SENTENCE

“I do not have to solve this right now. I do have to get to the next point where I can think and act more effectively.”

WATCH OUT

Crisis survival is temporary. If “tolerate it” keeps replacing problem-solving, advocacy, protection, or change, the tool is being misused.

ASK CLEARLY. KNOW YOUR LINE.

Use this for requests, limits, negotiation, or conflict.

BEFORE YOU SPEAK

OBJECTIVE

What specifically am I asking for?

RELATIONSHIP

How much does preserving this relationship matter here?

SELF-RESPECT

What would I regret agreeing to?

POWER

What can each person actually control?

ALTERNATIVES

What happens if the answer is no?

SAFETY

Is directness safe here?

THEN

1. Describe only the facts needed for the request.

2. Make one concrete ask or boundary.

3. Give the relevant reason, not your entire legal brief.

4. Listen for the actual answer.

5. Negotiate only what is negotiable.

6. If the answer is no, move to your alternative instead of repeating the same argument louder.

WATCH OUT

Communication skill cannot erase a power imbalance. Sometimes the problem is not your phrasing.

HOW DID WE GET HERE?

Use this after a behavior you want to understand well enough to interrupt next time.

RULE

We are reconstructing causation, not building a prosecution.

MAP THE SEQUENCE

VULNERABILITIES

PROMPT

MEANING

What did the event seem to mean?

URGE

LINKS

BEHAVIOR

IMMEDIATE CONSEQUENCE

DELAYED CONSEQUENCE

REINFORCEMENT

WATCH OUT

PATIENT

THERAPIST

AIM YOUR ATTENTION

Use this when attention is being captured by rumination, threat monitoring, dissociation, sensory overload, or automatic behavior.

WHY IT HELPS

You can move your attention on purpose. Looking around the room, moving, doing a task, or noticing your body all count. Meditation is only one option.

CHOOSE A MODE

EXTERNAL

Notice visual, auditory, or tactile details in the environment.

MOVEMENT

Walk, stretch, pace, or do a repetitive task while tracking movement and sensation.

TASK-BASED

Do one concrete activity with full attention.

INTERNAL

Notice internal sensations, only if this is tolerable and useful.

OPEN MONITORING

Notice thoughts and feelings without immediately acting on them.

WHICH MODE WORKS BEST FOR ME?

WATCH OUT

If inward attention increases dissociation, panic, traumatic intrusion, or destabilization, change the attentional target. Mindfulness is a method, not a moral achievement.

EMOTION IS DATA, NOT AN ORDER

Use this when you want to understand an emotion without either obeying it automatically or dismissing it.

ASK

1. What event or meaning triggered this emotion?

2. What does the emotion predict, signal, or protect?

3. What action does it prepare me to take?

4. Is that action useful here?

5. Is the intensity proportionate to the evidence, history, and current stakes?

6. What information should I keep even if I change the action?

A USEFUL DISTINCTION

An emotion can make sense even when an inference is wrong.

An inference can be right even when the action it produces is ineffective.

THE GOAL

Keep the information. Choose the behavior.

TRACK WHAT MATTERS

Use tracking only when the information could change what you do.

VARIABLE

What are we measuring?

DECISION

What treatment decision could this change?

BURDEN

How annoying or difficult is it to record?

FREQUENCY

How often is enough to detect the pattern?

STOP RULE

When will we stop tracking it?

EXAMPLES

Instead of rating seven emotions every day because a form has seven rows, you might track:

  • Self-harm urges before and after conflict.
  • Sleep hours when irritability is the clinical question.
  • Shutdown duration while testing sensory accommodations.
  • Use of two competing strategies when deciding which actually helps.

RULE

If nobody would make a different decision based on the answer, reconsider why you are collecting the data.

ONE THING AT A TIME

Use this when your attention is split enough that the task becomes harder, slower, or more error-prone.

WHY IT HELPS

Attention is limited. Switching repeatedly between tasks creates a cost, especially when you are stressed, tired, or overloaded. Deliberate single-task focus can reduce that cost.

DO THIS

1. Name the one thing you are doing for the next few minutes.

2. Put the next step where you can see it.

3. Reduce one unnecessary competing input.

4. Start the task.

5. When attention leaves, notice it and return without turning the return into a referendum on your character.

6. Stop when the planned interval ends or the task changes.

WATCH OUT

This is not a rule that multitasking is always bad. Some people focus better with movement, music, fidgets, or parallel low-demand input. Keep supports that improve performance.

NOTICE WITHOUT OBEYING

Use this when a thought, feeling, image, memory, or urge is present so strongly that it starts to feel like an instruction.

WHY IT HELPS

A thought, feeling, memory, or urge can be loud without being a command. Even a short pause gives you more choice.

DO THIS

1. Name what showed up: thought, emotion, memory, image, urge, body alarm.

2. Describe it briefly without arguing with it.

3. Ask: Does this require action right now?

4. If not, choose a delay: 30 seconds, 5 minutes, or longer.

5. Put attention on the next useful action during that interval.

6. Reassess when the interval ends.

A USEFUL SENTENCE

“This is present. I do not have to obey it immediately.”

WATCH OUT

Do not use this to ignore a genuine safety signal or practical problem. The point is choice, not automatic disbelief of your own mind.

________________

PAUSE, PROTECT, DECIDE

Use this when the next few minutes could create consequences that are hard to reverse.

THE JOB

Do not solve everything. Prevent the next avoidable piece of damage, then make a better decision.

DO THIS

PAUSE

Stop long enough to interrupt automatic action.

PROTECT

Reduce access to the most dangerous, irreversible, or costly option. Leave the room, put down the phone, hand over the keys, move away from the object, or get another person involved if needed.

DECIDE

Ask:

1. What happens if I act on the urge right now?

2. What happens in an hour?

3. What happens tomorrow?

4. What option keeps the most choices open?

5. What is the smallest safe next action?

WATCH OUT

A pause is useful only if something happens during it. Rumination with better branding is still rumination.

MAKE ROOM, THEN CHOOSE

Use this when part of you is spending enormous effort fighting the fact that you are having an emotion, sensation, urge, or unwanted situation.

WHY IT HELPS

Sometimes the fight with the feeling becomes a second problem. Make a little room for it, then decide what you want to do.

DO THIS

1. Notice what you are resisting.

2. Ask whether the resistance is warning you about something important. If yes, keep the information.

3. If the struggle itself is no longer helping, loosen one part of it: jaw, shoulders, fists, breath, posture, argument in your head.

4. Allow the experience to be present for the next short interval.

5. Choose the next action based on your goal, not on whether the feeling disappeared.

WATCH OUT

Making room is not submitting. You can allow an emotion to exist while opposing what caused it.

CHANGE WHAT IS CHANGEABLE

Use this when the main problem is outside you and something can actually be done about it.

DO THIS

1. DEFINE THE PROBLEM

Describe the situation in behavioral terms.

2. SORT THE VARIABLES

Control: what can I directly do?

Influence: what can I ask, negotiate, or affect?

Outside control: what cannot be changed by my action right now?

3. GENERATE OPTIONS

List at least three possible actions, including small ones.

4. CHOOSE

Which option has the best balance of likely benefit, cost, safety, and effort?

5. ACT

Make the next step specific enough to put on a calendar or do now.

6. REVIEW

Did the action change the problem? If not, revise.

WATCH OUT

Do not use coping skills to get beautifully calm about a problem that needs a phone call, application, boundary, repair, appointment, complaint, or exit.

BUILD UP. PLAN AHEAD.

Use this to make hard moments less dominant by building more reward, competence, direction, and preparation into ordinary life.

FOUR DIFFERENT LEVERS

REWARD

Do something that is pleasant, meaningful, interesting, connecting, or restorative.

CAPACITY

Do something that gives you contact with competence or progress. It should be challenging enough to matter and small enough to finish.

DIRECTION

Choose something that moves life toward a value or priority you care about. It does not have to feel pleasant in the moment to count.

REHEARSAL

For a predictable difficult situation:

1. Picture the actual setting.

2. Predict the likely trigger.

3. Choose the first useful response.

4. Imagine the likely obstacle.

5. Rehearse recovering when it does not go perfectly.

WHAT DO I WANT FROM THIS CONVERSATION?

Use this before a difficult conversation so you know what you are trying to protect.

RATE EACH 0–10

OBJECTIVE

How important is the concrete outcome I want?

RELATIONSHIP

How important is preserving closeness, trust, or cooperation with this person?

SELF-RESPECT

How important is it that I act consistently with my values and limits?

SAFETY

What are the realistic risks of directness here?

POWER

Who controls what? What can happen if the other person says no?

THEN DECIDE

What matters most?

What am I willing to trade?

What am I not willing to trade?

What is the smallest useful goal for this conversation?

WATCH OUT

You are allowed to want all of these things. The skill is noticing when they conflict so the tradeoff happens on purpose instead of by accident.

STAY CONNECTED. KEEP YOURSELF.

Use this when the relationship matters and you want to remain connected without abandoning your own position.

DO THIS

1. LISTEN FOR THE ACTUAL POINT

What is the other person trying to communicate?

2. SHOW THAT YOU HEARD IT

Reflect the part you genuinely understand.

3. VALIDATE WHAT MAKES SENSE

Name why their response is understandable given their information, history, or priorities.

4. KEEP YOUR OWN POSITION

Understanding is not agreement. State your view without erasing theirs.

5. STAY HUMAN

Use warmth, curiosity, humor, or softness only when it fits the relationship and the situation.

A USEFUL FORM

“I can see why ___ makes sense to you. I still need/want/think ___.”

WATCH OUT

Relationship effectiveness is not compulsory niceness. Do not use warmth to make yourself stay in an unsafe interaction.

________________

KEEP YOUR SELF-RESPECT

Use this when getting agreement would cost too much in self-respect.

CHECK FOUR THINGS

FAIRNESS

Am I applying a standard I would consider fair if the roles were reversed?

APOLOGY

Did I actually violate something? If yes, repair it. If no, do not use apology as payment for having a need.

VALUES

What principle do I want my own behavior to reflect here?

ACCURACY

Am I exaggerating, minimizing, bluffing, or claiming certainty I do not have?

THEN ASK

If I look back at this conversation tomorrow, what behavior would I respect?

WATCH OUT

Self-respect is not winning, dominating, or proving the other person wrong. It is leaving the interaction able to stand behind your own conduct.

POWER, RISK, OPTIONS

Use this when “just be assertive” is not enough information.

MAP THE SITUATION

MY CONTROL

What can I directly do?

THEIR CONTROL

What can the other person decide or withhold?

DEPENDENCE

What do I need from them, and what do they need from me?

ALTERNATIVES

What can I do if the answer is no?

COSTS

What could directness realistically cost?

SAFETY

Could confrontation create retaliation, danger, housing/work consequences, or other serious risk?

LEVERAGE

What information, support, documentation, policy, timing, ally, or alternative changes the balance?

NEXT MOVE

What is the smallest action that improves my position without creating unnecessary risk?

WATCH OUT

Communication cannot solve every power problem. Sometimes the effective move is documentation, advocacy, support, distance, escalation through a system, or leaving.

REQUEST, BOUNDARY, OR DECISION?

Use this when you are trying to say something clearly but the sentence keeps becoming an argument about what the other person must do.

REQUEST

You are asking the other person to choose an action.

“I’m asking you to text me if you’ll be more than 20 minutes late.”

They can say yes or no.

BOUNDARY

You are describing what you will do under specific conditions.

“If yelling starts, I’m going to end the conversation and come back later.”

It does not require permission.

DECISION

You are choosing an action for yourself.

“I’m not attending this event.”

It does not require the other person to agree.

TRY IT

What do I want to communicate?

Is it mainly a request, boundary, or decision?

What is the cleanest one-sentence version?

WATCH OUT

Calling a demand a “boundary” does not magically give you control over another person. Boundaries describe your behavior.

BUILD THE RELATIONSHIPS YOU WANT

Use this when you want more connection, a stronger friendship, or more people in your life and waiting for it to happen is not enough.

WHY IT HELPS

Relationships usually grow through repeated small contact, not one perfect conversation. The goal is to create more chances for connection and notice where the effort is returned.

DO THIS

1. GO WHERE REPEATED CONTACT IS POSSIBLE

Look for places, activities, communities, classes, work settings, groups, or routines where you can see some of the same people more than once.

2. MAKE A SMALL BID

Say hello. Ask a real question. Respond to something they said. Offer a small invitation. Follow up once.

3. LOOK FOR RECIPROCITY

Do they answer, ask back, initiate sometimes, make room, remember things, or seem glad to see you?

4. BUILD THROUGH REPETITION

Small reliable contact usually builds more than one huge disclosure or one perfect hangout.

5. LET CLOSENESS GROW AT ITS OWN SPEED

Different relationships move at different rates. Do not force intimacy to prove the connection is real.

6. PUT EFFORT WHERE THERE IS FIT

Look for respect, interest, basic reliability, enough safety, and room for you to be a person rather than a performance.

TRY IT

One place where repeated contact is possible:

One small bid I could make:

What reciprocity would look like:

WATCH OUT

Effort is not the same as chasing. If contact stays consistently one-sided, dismissive, unsafe, or exhausting, the answer may be to move your effort somewhere else.

REPAIR, RENEGOTIATE, OR LEAVE IT ALONE

Use this after an interaction did not go as planned.

FIRST ASK

What actually went wrong?

REPAIR

Use when your behavior caused avoidable harm.

Name what you did, acknowledge the effect, correct what you can, and change the future behavior.

RENEGOTIATE

Use when both people still want an arrangement but the original plan did not work.

Clarify what failed and make a more realistic agreement.

TRY AGAIN DIFFERENTLY

Use when the goal still matters but your first approach was poor.

ACCEPT OR LEAVE IT ALONE

Use when further discussion is unlikely to improve anything.

DISTANCE OR ACT ELSEWHERE

Use when the interaction itself is unsafe, coercive, repetitive, or not the place where the problem can be solved.

WATCH OUT

Repeating the same conversation with increasing eloquence is still repeating the same conversation.
