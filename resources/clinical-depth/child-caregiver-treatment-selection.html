Phase 8B · Child and family treatment · Clinician guide

When a child struggles, what—and whose—needs should treatment change?

Begin with development, access, safety, the caregiver–child relationship, and the environment before choosing a child-only intervention. An observed behavior can have more than one explanation and function. This guide supports treatment selection and supervised practice, not a PCIT, CPP, TF-CBT or play-therapy protocol.

Safety decision first. Suspected abuse, sexualized or coercive behavior, current violence, serious injury, unsafe contact, severe neglect, or substantial fear/power imbalance needs safeguarding and qualified individual assessment, not a forced joint discussion or reconciliation. Assess local reporting duties and protect children without making the child responsible for adult conduct.

First, separate four explanations that look like “noncompliance”

A child cannot or does not do an expected activity
↙↘

Task and regulation access

Can the child understand the direction, communicate, tolerate the demand, coordinate their body, transition, or remember the sequence under today's conditions?

Possible responses: language/AAC, visual cues, task-size changes, co-regulation, medical or developmental assessment.

Relationship and context

What preceded the event? Who holds power? Is there danger, unpredictability, reinforcement, caregiver overwhelm, school exclusion, or trauma-related fear?

Possible responses: safety, caregiver skills, predictable routines, dyadic help, changes in school/home environment.

Concrete caregiver-coaching sequence in a routine session

  1. Start with the caregiver's and child's priorities. Define one meaningful activity: getting ready, putting away materials, transitions, shared play, or bedtime. Ask what currently works and what each person wants to be easier. Confirm safety, opportunity for supported choice and privacy.
  2. Observe before interpreting. Watch or carefully reconstruct one attempt. Record exact adult direction and timing, child response, environment, what happens immediately afterward and where access breaks. Distinguish child skill from motivation.
  3. Choose only one adult adjustment. Examples: change the language load, shorten a task, offer a visual cue, connect before directing, increase predictability, reinforce an accessible replacement skill, or support a calm recovery break. Avoid adding five simultaneous demands.
  4. Demonstrate and rehearse. The clinician models brief language and access; caregiver practices while feedback is concrete and respectful. If child participation is safe and appropriate, rehearse directly, noticing assent and signs of overload.
  5. Plan for the real context. Identify when/where, necessary materials, who will provide support, a backup plan, and what happens if the child cannot comply despite help. Practice caregiver repair: “I asked too much at once. I'll make it clearer.”
  6. Review both outcomes. Track participation, conflict/recovery, perceived safety, caregiver effort, child autonomy, and adverse effects. A reduction in outward behavior alone is insufficient if fear, withdrawal or restraint increased.

Two fictional cases with different treatment implications

Case A: a three-year-old who bites at daycare

Rae, 3, bites during group transitions. Rae joined an adoptive family after early unstable caregiving and has ongoing scheduled contact with a biological relative. Caregivers report warm, safe routines at home; staff note that biting follows crowded, loud transitions and adult verbal redirection. Language comprehension, pain/medical status, current safety of contact and the child's understanding of transitions have not yet been clarified.

Is this evidence of trauma reenactment?

No. Early adversity is relevant history but the cause cannot be inferred from biting or play themes. Observe transition, communication and sensory demands; ask about pain/sleep and safety without leading allegations. Consider caregiver support, daycare environmental adjustment and developmentally appropriate assessment; evaluate safeguarding and mandated-reporting obligations when indicated.

When would dyadic trauma care be considered?

When qualified assessment supports trauma-related relational or developmental treatment needs and a safe participating caregiver is available. CPP/other specialty models require trained providers. A behavior-specific intervention and dyadic care may be complementary, not alternatives chosen purely by the child's history.

Case B: an 11-year-old who “refuses” complex schoolwork

Noah, 11, stops working when multi-step written directions are given. The caregiver believes more consequences are needed; however, brief spoken directions paired with a simple visual example enable participation. Math gaps, reading comprehension, possible language disorder, anxiety and task difficulty remain under evaluation.

Which response would change the assessment?

Compare performance with accessible instructions versus unsupported tasks, without changing the academic construct being assessed. Seek school/SLP/psychoeducational evaluation when indicated. Treat the observed access change as evidence that demand characteristics matter, not proof of a specific disability category or intent.

How might caregiver coaching look?

Coach one concise instruction, a worked example, a supported first step, choice over sequence and praise for use of effective strategies; practice a respectful repair when misunderstanding occurs. Track independent learning separately from completing work with substantial adult help.

Monitoring the right outcomes

Child and relationship outcomes

Safety, child assent/choice, successful transitions, actual skill acquisition, access to activities, emotional recovery, play connection, school participation and distress. Record the conditions in which improvement occurred.

Caregiver and service outcomes

Feasibility of the adult change, burden, confidence without coercion, consistency across providers, therapist fidelity/competence, service access and need for protective intervention. Consider any unintended worsening or escalation.

Review weekly or at a clinically appropriate interval: continue if the support enables participation and relationship repair; adapt if it is inaccessible or burdensome; reassess if the outcome is unrelated to your predicted mechanism; escalate for protection or acute needs.

Primary guidance, limits, and review needs

NCTSN: Parent–Child Interaction Therapy (fact sheet 2019): specifies PCIT as live-coached, evidence-based caregiver–child work for approximately ages 2–7, not generic parent advice.

NCTSN: Early Childhood Trauma Interventions: describes the evidence base and distinct indications for Child–Parent Psychotherapy (ages 0–5) and other young-child interventions.

AAP ADHD Clinical Practice Guideline (2019): parent training in behavior management and/or classroom behavioral intervention first-line for preschool-aged children with ADHD when available.
