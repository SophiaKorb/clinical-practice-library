Phase 8B · Psychosis · Bipolar spectrum · Recovery

What changed, when—and what helps this person live?

Distinguish unusual experiences, episodic mood/energy change, baseline development, sleep and medical/substance effects before making a single diagnosis explain everything. Coordinate specialist treatment while preserving the person's values, consent, dignity and functional goals.

Do not turn an acute change into routine worksheet work. New severe confusion, altered consciousness, possible delirium, catatonia, serious medical symptoms, severe mania, rapidly worsening functioning, immediate self-harm concerns or threatening/unsafe commands require appropriate urgent qualified assessment. Unusual beliefs or voice-hearing alone do not imply dangerousness or justify coercion.

Evidence that actually changes the differential

Possible explanationQuestions and sourcesInterpretation boundary
Bipolar mania/hypomania or mixed featuresDistinct change in activity/energy and mood with sustained duration, decreased need for sleep rather than just inability to sleep, impaired judgment and function, past episodes, family history and between-episode baseline.Emotional reactivity, ordinary sleep deprivation or a high screening score alone does not establish bipolar disorder. Qualified urgent assessment is indicated when mania is suspected and severity is high.
Psychosis-spectrum experienceDetailed phenomenology, insight and interpretation, distress, disorganization, symptom timing relative to mood episodes, baseline functioning, family/medical history and culture.Voice-hearing, spiritual or culturally shared beliefs, trauma intrusions or unusual experiences are not individually diagnostic. Don't automatically affirm or dispute an explanation.
Substance/medication or medical statePrescribed and nonprescribed agents, intoxication and withdrawal, seizures, neurological condition, metabolic/medical illness, acute confusion and onset relative to exposure.A reported history of mental illness should never prevent medical evaluation for an acute change. A substance contribution does not rule out concurrent psychiatric illness.
PTSD, dissociation and sleep-related phenomenaTrauma reminders, depersonalization, time/memory differences, nightmares, hypnagogic/hypnopompic phenomena, duration/context and distress.Don't infer trauma histories or DID from an auditory experience. Report subjective experience without suggesting a cause.
Autism/ADHD, learning/language or cultural contextLongstanding neurodevelopmental patterns, figurative communication, sensory regulation, preferred language, community meaning and ability to express uncertainty.A communication difference or socially unusual statement is not proof of delusion or loss of decision-making capacity.

Prompt to the clinician: “What new information would lead me to refer urgently, revise the leading explanation, or choose a different treatment?” Note the contrast between baseline, onset, duration, opportunities and daily-life impact—not simply the most striking symptom.

Choose coordinated care and protect competence boundaries

QuestionApproach and serviceBoundary
First or worsening psychosisTimely qualified medical/psychiatric assessment, early-psychosis/coordinated specialty care where appropriate; collaborative psychological, family, education/occupation and physical-health services.Psychological support alone should not displace indicated specialty evaluation and medication discussion with prescribers. Monitor side effects and physical health through responsible clinicians.
Acute mania or severe bipolar depressionSpecialist assessment and crisis care according to severity; clear shared care plan and medication review through authorized prescribers, with patient preferences and safety needs considered.A therapy worksheet does not manage acute mania, select medication or authorize involuntary measures. Avoid unilateral changes to prescribed medicines.
Distressing voices or firmly held beliefs without immediate emergencyCollaborative work on meaning, distress, sleep, coping, safety, attention, and actual functioning. CBT for psychosis and family intervention are specialist, evidence-informed options delivered by trained teams.Do not shame, argue, reinforce a frightening interpretation as certain, or require a belief to disappear before functional support begins.
Long-term bipolar disorder with residual symptomsStructured bipolar-specific psychological relapse-prevention interventions, agreed early warning signs, stress/sleep rhythm support, family participation if wanted, and specialist medication/physical health coordination.“Mood charting” is not a substitute for appropriately trained bipolar treatment; new activation, deteriorating depression or medication concerns prompt specialist review.
Work, education and daily-life accessSupported employment/education, social inclusion, family supports when chosen, practical organization, transportation, safe housing and skills matched to actual needs.Symptom reduction does not itself prove recovery in relationships, cognition, work, self-care or autonomy. Supports may remain needed.

NICE CG178 supports multidisciplinary assessment, individual CBT and family intervention for psychosis, physical health monitoring and recovery-oriented services. NICE CG185 addresses bipolar episode history, specialist care, structured psychological intervention and relapse planning. These UK guidelines require local implementation review; psychotherapy and prescribing roles differ by profession.

Session-level work without making the therapist a prescriber

  1. Clarify acute safety, medical state and participation. Determine whether current needs require urgent medical/specialist evaluation, ensure accessible communication and avoid assuming automatic risk from diagnosis.
  2. Invite the person's account. “What are you noticing, what meaning does it have for you, and what happens after it?” Ask about distress and coping rather than debating whether their experience is real.
  3. Locate the episode in context. Mark sleep, mood/energy, perceptions, medications/substances, stress, function and support independently on the timeline. Note collateral and missing information.
  4. Choose a patient-valued target. Examples: reduce fear during voices, make a work shift accessible, improve sleep reliability or prepare for a prescriber review. Agree on a practical, safe step within clinician competence.
  5. Coordinate named roles. Identify prescriber, therapist, case manager, early-psychosis service, supporter (if agreed), medical follow-up, who contacts whom and contingency if the plan fails.
  6. Reassess function and harms. At the next contact, review safety, perceived control, functioning, adverse effects, service accessibility and whether new evidence warrants re-formulation.

Fictional cases with competing, not predetermined, answers

Case A: is this an episode, sleep loss, or stimulant-related change?

Samira, 23, has slept two to three hours for four nights while reporting abundant energy, rapid speech, new spending, and a frightening sense that familiar songs contain messages. Her roommate describes a rapid change from baseline. She also recently began a stimulant medication and has used nonprescribed substances at parties. The therapist planned an exposure homework assignment for “anxiety about signs.”

What should be assessed before routine exposure homework?

Possible mania, acute psychosis, medication/substance effects and immediate safety/medical considerations require timely qualified assessment and prescriber coordination. Clarify actual sleep need, duration, functioning, prior episodes, health and use timeline. Avoid assigning a definite cause from these facts alone.

What can a therapist do while specialist evaluation proceeds?

Address immediate safety and access, use calm nonconfrontational engagement, obtain consented collateral where appropriate, coordinate care and minimize overstimulating demands. Avoid medication directives and do not use unstructured persuasion about the content of beliefs.

Case B: quieter voices, persistent disability

Jordan, 39, reports that voices are now less distressing after treatment. Family assumes Jordan can immediately return to independent medication management and full-time work. However, Jordan remains slow to initiate tasks, misses appointments when bus routes change, and wants to start with a supported part-time role. Possible adverse medication effects, depression, negative symptoms, cognitive changes and environmental access have not been differentiated.

Does reduced positive-symptom distress imply full functional recovery?

No. Work participation, self-care, executive demands, negative/depressive symptoms, medication effects and environmental barriers need their own assessment. Support chosen employment goals and clarify responsibility for medical review.

How can progress be measured without taking away autonomy?

Collaboratively track meaningful tasks, help required, burden, satisfaction, transportation reliability and harms along with symptom/distress outcomes. Change the plan when an apparently “successful” symptom intervention leaves serious functional barriers unaddressed.

Two outcomes, two distinct timelines

Clinical experience and episodesSleep, mood/energy, voices/beliefs, distress, immediate safety, treatment burden, physical health and side effects.
Function and recoveryHousing, personal goals, self-care, employment, relationships, agency, social inclusion, access and supports needed.
Improvement in one domain cannot be assumed to establish improvement in the other. Review both, with the person's priorities.

Official guidance and review boundaries

NICE CG178: Psychosis and schizophrenia in adults (2014; reviewed 2025) — specialist/physical-health assessment, coordinated early care, CBT/family interventions and functional recovery.

NICE CG185: Bipolar disorder, assessment and management (2014, amended later) — episode history, urgent specialist review for suspected mania/severe depression, structured psychotherapy and recovery planning.

Limits: This original timeline and worked cases are not validated diagnostic criteria, medication guidance or a substitute for qualified psychiatric/clinical supervision.
