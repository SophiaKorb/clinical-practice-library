Phase 8B · Substance use · Co-occurring mental health

Treat the whole picture, not two disorders in separate queues

Clarify the person's goals, immediate medical risk, patterns of use, mental-health concerns, function, housing and access to care. The practical problem is often deciding what must happen in parallel rather than waiting for “sobriety” before addressing another disorder.

Urgent medical and safeguarding conditions supersede worksheets. Possible overdose, respiratory suppression, dangerous alcohol or sedative withdrawal, seizures, severe confusion, acute suicidal concern, major medical illness, severe agitation or psychosis requires immediate qualified medical/psychiatric assessment and locally indicated emergency response. Do not advise a patient to stop a physically dependent substance abruptly without appropriate medical input.

Assessment with genuine competing explanations

QuestionInformation to seekWhy it changes the decision
What substance and pattern?Substances, routes, amount/frequency over time, co-use, intoxication and withdrawal experiences, prior severe withdrawal, overdose, prescribed/nonprescribed medications, changing supply and exposure, and social context.Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal can be medically dangerous; opioid exposure carries respiratory overdose concerns. A generic “use severity” label cannot replace assessment of immediate physiology.
What is the person's purpose or function?Stress relief, sleep, pain, anxiety, social belonging, energy, coping with trauma, avoiding withdrawal, habit and other person-named meanings.A function hypothesis informs collaborative alternatives; it does not excuse harm, diagnose motivation or imply the substance is the only mechanism.
What else is occurring?Psychosis, mood episodes, PTSD, anxiety, neurodevelopmental issues, chronic pain, sleep, cognitive problems, injury, grief, medication effects and suicidal concerns.Build a timeline of before/during/after intoxication/withdrawal and baseline; mental health conditions and substance effects can coexist. Do not force one explanation.
What does recovery mean to this person?Reduced overdose risk, change in use, safer relationships, housing, work, parenting, medical stabilization, abstinence if chosen, treatment access and quality of life.Engagement is not a binary “motivated/unmotivated” attribute. Set goals that the person regards as meaningful and monitor safety alongside use.
What care can they actually receive?Housing, money, transport, stigma, privacy, insurance, language, disability, work/family responsibilities, social support, prior experiences of treatment.An otherwise good plan fails if the person cannot reach the service or if continuity depends on a nonfunctional referral.

Separate observation and interpretation: changes in thoughts or sleep after substance use may reflect intoxication, withdrawal, independent psychiatric illness or multiple processes. Appropriate medical assessment, longitudinal collateral when consented, and continued reassessment matter.

Three streams often need to move together

Level-of-care decision: consider urgent medical risk, co-occurring psychiatric and biomedical needs, environmental safety, prior response, accessible service intensity and patient preferences with a qualified addiction assessor. Follow applicable organizational standards and consult official ASAM Criteria materials when licensed/trained; CPL does not reproduce its proprietary decision rules or assign levels.

Select treatment according to condition, preference and provider capacity

Clinical considerationEvidence-informed possibilitiesLimit or care handoff
Opioid use disorder or recurrent overdose concernOffer/coordinate access to evidence-based medication treatment with authorized prescribers and recovery support; provide overdose prevention and naloxone education/access as locally appropriate.Counseling is not a substitute for evidence-based OUD medication where indicated. Prescribing, interactions and withdrawal management require medical expertise.
Alcohol use with withdrawal concernQualified medical assessment of withdrawal danger and indicated setting; after stabilization, integrated psychosocial and medication options with the relevant medical professional.Do not use a worksheet to manage potentially severe alcohol withdrawal. Withdrawal care alone is not ongoing AUD treatment.
Stimulant use with behavioral treatment goalsExplore evidence-based contingency management where available and organizationally authorized, CBT/skills approaches, recovery goals and integrated psychiatric care.Contingency management is a structured program, not an improvised punishment/reward contract. Acute agitation, psychosis or cardiovascular symptoms may require medical care.
Ambivalence about any changeMotivational interviewing spirit and skills: curiosity, reflective listening, person-led values, concrete choice and collaborative experiments.MI is not covert persuasion or a prerequisite for offering medical harm-reduction options. Do not call disagreement “resistance.”
Co-occurring PTSD, depression, psychosis, bipolar or painCoordinate mental-health and substance treatment instead of automatically delaying one until the other is “fixed.” Adapt pace and scope to safety, capacity, preferences and available care.Integrated care does not imply every therapy modality is safe during intoxication, withdrawal or severe instability; qualified providers coordinate timing.

Practical psychotherapy/case-management encounter

  1. Open with consent and immediate needs. Ask whether the person can participate, whether medical/overdose/withdrawal concerns need action, and what would make this visit worthwhile. Verify confidentiality and its limits.
  2. Specify one change target. “What do you want to be different about the next week?” Allow safety, use, relationships, pain and treatment access as legitimate priorities.
  3. Reconstruct one recent episode nonjudgmentally. Note sleep, physical state, cue, access, substance/route, expectation, immediate effect and next-day consequence. Identify sources and uncertainties, not an inferred personality motive.
  4. Ask for a choice among realistic supports. Possible options include a medical appointment, overdose prevention resource, recontacting a prescriber, alternative coping or transportation arrangement. Avoid suggesting that a single exercise treats dependence.
  5. Make the handoff concrete. Name the service, person responsible, contact route, time, consented information, what could go wrong, and what to do if the connection fails.
  6. Review safety and function. Check overdose/withdrawal events, patient goals, use without moral judgment, housing/work/relationships, symptoms, support burden and missed contacts. Reassess earlier assumptions.

Two fictional cases with different first priorities

Case A: alcohol, poor sleep and tremor

Devon, 44, says anxiety is unbearable and has recently reduced heavy daily alcohol consumption without clinical guidance. They now report shaking, insomnia and feeling confused at night, with a remote history of withdrawal-related seizures. A therapist planned breathing exercises and an alcohol-reduction homework sheet.

Which task cannot wait for routine psychotherapy?

Potentially dangerous withdrawal warrants timely qualified medical assessment and appropriate urgent care. Do not rely on relaxation, a behavior sheet, or a home taper devised by a therapist. Explore other immediate safety needs and coordinate ongoing treatment after medical stabilization.

What changes after stabilization?

Build a longer-term, person-centered plan addressing alcohol use disorder treatment, mental health symptoms, supports, safe care transitions, sleep, relationships and access to medical/behavioral services. Withdrawal management alone is not continuing AUD care.

Case B: opioid exposure, PTSD and unstable housing

Alex, 27, seeks help with nightmares and anxiety. They have used nonprescribed opioids, survived a prior overdose, and live in temporary housing where calls from clinics often fail. Alex does not want a coercive abstinence contract but is willing to discuss medication treatment and a trusted support contact.

Which streams should start together?

Offer appropriate medical/addiction assessment and access to medication treatment, overdose prevention/naloxone information, psychosocial engagement, and housing/communication supports. Assess current mental-health and safety needs; do not make PTSD care conditional on an arbitrary abstinence period.

What makes a referral meaningful?

With Alex's permission, identify a specific receiving clinic/contact, feasible travel and phone route, an appointment/backup plan and whether the handoff succeeded. Follow-up measures include safety and functioning, not simply urine results or frequency of use.

Review actual recovery and coordinated functioning

Risk, symptoms and access

New intoxication/withdrawal danger, overdose, suicidal concerns, return-to-use pattern, physical condition, emerging mood/psychotic symptoms, appointment access and treatment harms.

Quality of life and change

Patient-defined goals, housing security, family/work roles, safer relationships, pain/sleep functioning, use of choices, trust in care and effort required to maintain supports.

Continue, adapt or escalate according to actual conditions; revise the formulation when a psychiatric symptom persists outside substance effects, medical risk changes, the person cannot access the proposed treatment, or support itself causes harm.

Authoritative sources and limits

SAMHSA: Screening and Treatment of Co-Occurring Disorders — recommends coordinated/co-located/integrated mental-health and substance care.

SAMHSA TIP 63: Medications for Opioid Use Disorder (resource update 2024): covers authorized medications and recovery supports, not dose instructions here.

ASAM: Alcohol Withdrawal Management guideline — notes withdrawal management is only one component of AUD treatment. This page reproduces no ASAM proprietary criteria, instrument items or treatment orders.

ASAM Criteria 4th Edition overview — appropriate level of care requires qualified multidimensional assessment, not a CPL-generated risk category. Consult publisher terms before using any ASAM materials.

CDC: fentanyl overdose prevention — overdose education, naloxone and treatment access.
