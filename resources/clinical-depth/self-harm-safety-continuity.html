Clinical reasoning · Safety · Care continuity

After a self-harm or suicide-related concern: what changes the next decision?

A clinician-facing workflow for integrating an immediate response, individualized psychosocial assessment and actual follow-through. It is not a prediction score, self-administered risk assessment, local emergency protocol, or substitute for a qualified clinician.

Urgent needs are not postponed for this worksheet. Significant injury or poisoning, inability to maintain immediate safety, suspected medical instability, severe intoxication/withdrawal, confusion, rapidly deteriorating mental state, or acute safeguarding concerns require an immediate response under local emergency and supervisory procedures. Physical and psychosocial assessment may be concurrent when feasible.

Separate constructs that are often collapsed: self-harm is not synonymous with suicide attempt; non-suicidal intent in one event cannot settle current or future safety; emotional function does not determine medical lethality; a risk scale does not authorize or preclude care. Record uncertainty explicitly.

From formulation to an implementable plan

1 · Formulate the near-term concerns collaboratively

Use the person's words for the feared event and immediate needs. Integrate recent events, chronic vulnerability, current change, access to means, reasons for living, values and connections, and foreseeable circumstances. Document the clinical reasoning and its limits, not a globally predictive label.

2 · Decide what care and specialty support is needed

Qualified staff determine setting, immediate clinical/medical evaluation and required supervision using current evidence and local law/policy. Involve medical, crisis, psychiatry, safeguarding or specialist services as indicated. Explain what is proposed, seek consent where appropriate, use least-restrictive safe care and recognize confidentiality exceptions that require consultation.

3 · Make a usable safety and support plan

Build or review a collaborative, accessible plan that the person can actually use: individual warning signs, feasible coping steps, chosen contacts and safer settings, professional and out-of-hours contact details, environmental/means safety, and specific barriers (transport, phone access, language, isolation). Test how the plan will be found during distress. Do not replace it with a no-suicide contract.

4 · Close the handoff

Name the receiving clinician/team, contact route, requested action, timing, confirmation method and contingency if contact fails. Communicate the assessment's uncertainty and physical needs. Follow required disclosure/consent procedures. If a transition is planned, do not rely on “patient will call” when actual access or continuity remains unknown.

What belongs in the contemporaneous note?

Presenting concern and sources; physical/psychiatric urgency and action taken; the person's account and functional context; specific suicidal/self-harm phenomena assessed, limits/uncertainties, protective resources; capacity, communication and consent issues when relevant; individualized clinical formulation; rationale for level-of-care decision; collaborative safety actions; consultations and receiving team's name; follow-up date, contact method and failure contingency. Adapt to your practice's legal/organizational requirements.

Fictional case: one statement, several reasonable concerns

Morgan, 29, reports superficial self-injury after conflict and says, “It stops the spinning.” They deny wanting to die during this episode. They recently stopped sleeping well, missed outpatient appointments after a phone disconnection, and will lose temporary housing on Monday. At intake, they are alert, distressed and willing to speak, with no medical evaluation yet of the injury. The clinician has no reliable information about earlier episodes, future intent, or whether Morgan can receive follow-up calls.

Question 1: What do you do before assigning a risk category?

Address current injury and immediate safety, complete or arrange appropriate qualified clinical assessment, and ask about this episode's intent, past behavior, present suicidal thoughts, access, rapidly changing context and protective supports. Denial of intent now is data—not a guarantee. Do not infer “manipulation” or “low risk” from a proposed affect-regulation function.

Question 2: Which apparently good plan could fail?

“Call your therapist tomorrow” fails because the phone is disconnected and appointments were missed. Work collaboratively on reachable contacts, where the plan can be kept, access to housing support, an identified care-team owner, a backup contact route and an agreed follow-up timeline.

Question 3: When is the decision revisited?

Reassess when new information emerges, physical or psychiatric state changes, planned transitions occur, the person cannot use supports, or the plan is not working. Record who will re-evaluate and how. NICE NG225 recommends prompt aftercare when ongoing safety concerns persist after self-harm; local setting/policy determines implementation and more urgent needs take precedence.

Review actual safety and continuity, not a single risk score

Ask at the next contact

  • Did the person receive the agreed medical/clinical follow-up?
  • Did the plan work when distress increased, and what made it inaccessible?
  • Have self-harm episodes, suicidal thinking, intent, means, agitation, medication, housing or support access changed?
  • Was the person treated respectfully and included in decisions?
  • Did staff/teams complete and confirm the handoff?

Possible decisions

  • Continue if supports are usable, needs addressed and clinical reassessment supports the approach.
  • Adapt when contact, access, confidence or support burden obstructs implementation.
  • Escalate when new immediate concerns or unmet needs require urgent action.
  • Reformulate when the person's account, function, diagnosis or context does not fit the earlier explanation.

Evidence, scope and review limits

NICE NG225, Self-harm: assessment, management and preventing recurrence (2022) — official clinical guideline; specifically supports psychosocial assessment, collaborative care planning, concurrent physical/psychosocial care, avoiding predictive risk stratification, and aftercare. UK service recommendations require adaptation to other jurisdictions and settings.

Joint Commission suicide prevention resource hub — curated implementation resources including the Zero Suicide Toolkit and care-transition guidance; a resource collection, not a single outcome trial.

Not provided: a diagnostic assessment, individualized suicide-risk prediction, emergency response orders, medication advice, involuntary-treatment legal criteria, or a validated safety-plan instrument. Professional and organizational review pending.
