Complex relationships: from recurring crisis to a working treatment plan

For clinicians assessing persistent relational instability, strong fears of abandonment or engulfment, self-harm, rapid shifts in attachment expectations, or severe emotion dysregulation. Formulate the person and the environment; do not diagnose a personality disorder from one crisis or treat a label as a motive.

When immediate danger is present, use an appropriate safety, psychosocial assessment and continuity pathway. Respond proportionately, preserve access to care, and do not withdraw routine support as a behavior-control technique.

Formulate one interpersonal sequence without assigning motives

Choose a concrete event and distinguish the person's internal experience, what others observed, and what no one yet knows. Patterns may involve attempts to protect closeness, autonomy, safety, dignity or self-worth. “Manipulative,” “attention-seeking” and “splitting” often obscure the target behavior, circumstances and actual need.

Engulfment and abandonment are experiences, not labels

The same person can fear being left and fear loss of autonomy when someone comes close. Ask what closeness meant, when a boundary felt threatening, and what response felt available. Do not use the pattern to explain away an actual controlling relationship or real neglect.

Check the other person's and service's contribution

Availability, staff turnover, inconsistent promises, family power, disability access, actual discrimination, coercion, communication style, boundary changes and provider anxiety may create or intensify the sequence. The patient is not responsible for all components of a reciprocal pattern.

Select treatment by needs, evidence, training and preference

Guidelines support a structured psychotherapy and a collaborative person-centered treatment plan. The clinical question is not “Which branded model wins?” but what structure, resources, expertise and patient preferences fit the current pattern and level of impairment.

Service structure is itself an intervention condition: clarify frequency, availability, crisis contacts, communication boundaries, supervision, provider roles, review points and ending/transition plans. The frame should be predictable, not punitive. Explain it in an accessible language/format and revisit it with the person.

One session devoted to a rupture, without abandoning the treatment

  1. Begin with safety and repair of access. Assess new safety concerns when indicated. Ask whether the person can discuss the interaction and what they need to feel able to participate.
  2. Agree on what actually happened. “Last week I said I could not respond that evening, and you experienced that as being left alone. What did you understand me to mean?” Distinguish provider intention, observable words and impact.
  3. Test multiple hypotheses together. Fear of abandonment, expectation of intrusion, a real broken promise, poor explanation of policies, trauma cue, sensory/language demands, or several explanations may fit.
  4. Validate and keep responsibility appropriate. “That was frightening and the plan wasn't clear enough.” Avoid blame, premature interpretation, reassurance without limits, or withdrawal to punish distress. Acknowledge real service errors when present.
  5. Practice a small new option. Rehearse asking for clarification, expressing a boundary or checking service contact procedures. Clinician practices a clearer response, too; this is not only patient skills work.
  6. Close with plan and review. What will each person do next time? How will the team respond consistently? What will demonstrate better function—not merely fewer messages or fewer reports of distress?

Supervision prompt: What did the therapist/team feel pulled to do—rescue, explain repeatedly, retreat, punish, bend the rules, confront, or give up? Those reactions are hypotheses about the interaction, not evidence that a patient intended a particular effect.

Fictional case across three visits: several defensible priorities

Leah, 31, attends therapy for distress in relationships and unstable work participation. After a scheduling error the therapist cannot respond the same afternoon. Leah sends several messages, then says in session that the clinician does not care and that she may stop attending. She has previously used self-injury to cope, but no one has asked about recent self-harm or current suicidal thoughts. In another relationship she says, “When someone gets close, I can't breathe.” Her case manager has offered different crisis contact rules than the therapist.

Visit 1: is the immediate task interpretation, boundary-setting or safety?

First ask about current safety and changes in self-harm/suicidal phenomena as clinically appropriate, using the existing care plan and urgent response if needed. Clarify the actual scheduling error and the person's experience, avoid treating messages as proof of a motive, and establish a reliable, shared contact plan. This can be collaborative without erasing appropriate boundaries.

Visit 2: what evidence would differentiate abandonment and engulfment?

Explore episodes of feared separation and feared loss of autonomy, timing and safety in actual relationships, developmental pattern, trauma/sensory context and what help means to Leah. Don't impose one label or assume either concern describes all relationships. Consider what the therapist and service did, not only how Leah responded.

Visit 3: what counts as progress if emotions remain intense?

Better work and social participation, repair conversations, predictable clinical contact, use of agreed coping supports, reduced harm, less treatment burden and more autonomous choices may matter even if distress remains high. Review the plan when crises recur or service access fails. A period without messaging is not automatically recovery.

Longitudinal tracking: two timelines, one collaborative review

Episode and service timeline

Dates: interpersonal event, symptoms, safety, actual provider response, appointment/medication changes, stressor and contact availability. Mark unknowns rather than reconstructing a false story.

Participation and treatment timeline

Work, relationships, daily living, closeness/autonomy, goals, agency, unwanted effects, amount of support and ability to access care. Separate burden and context from observable change.

Source and implementation boundaries

APA Practice Guideline for the Treatment of Patients with Borderline Personality Disorder (2024) — supports structured psychotherapy, collaborative person-centered plans and clinical assessment; full implementation requires appropriate training and professional review.

NICE CG78 (2009), borderline personality disorder — emphasizes treatment continuity, supervision, comprehensive functional planning, non-exclusion from care and careful transitions. Service/legal provisions are UK-specific and may be dated.

Gunderson Personality Disorders Institute: scientific basis of GPM — describes the structured generalist model; it is an organizational/model source, not independent verification that different specialized treatments are interchangeable.
