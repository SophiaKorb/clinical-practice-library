Acquired aphasia: supported communication and participation — Visual support map

Clinician formulation guideInteractive case practiceVisual support mapClient workbookCare-partner guideProgress review tool

Adults with acquired aphasia · Presentation → Understanding → Assessment → Intervention → Tools → Monitoring

After acquired brain injury, an adult may have difficulty understanding or expressing language through speech, reading or writing. Communication difficulty must not be mistaken for low intelligence, lack of interest or automatic inability to make decisions.

How the parts relate

  1. MessageOne topic, accessible form
  2. Response modeSpeech, gesture, writing, pointing
  3. Time + accessFatigue, hearing, vision, language
  4. VerificationCheck meaning without guessing
  5. ParticipationPreference, next step, SLP handoff

Consider message, response mode and access conditions together. Then verify the person's meaning and agree on a participation step; never infer capacity from speech alone.

This map organizes a conversation; it does not establish causation or calculate risk. Follow the sequence for one event, then return to context and competing explanations. The arrows show a proposed relationship to examine, not proof.

Clinician layer: what the map could miss

Language access

Determine receptive/expressive strengths across modes with qualified SLP assessment.

Other access factors

Hearing, vision, motor control, fatigue and multilingual context can change what works.

Decision-specific understanding

Support communication before evaluating understanding; capacity is decision-specific and governed by qualified procedures.

Work with speech-language pathology and the person's preferred communication modes. Hearing, vision, fatigue, motor speech, attention, language history and context can affect access. Offer short messages, key words, pictures, gesture or choices as appropriate, and verify meaning rather than assuming yes/no reliability. Adapted standardized testing may no longer support standard score interpretation. This is an access-support aid for acquired aphasia, not a child language-development or dementia protocol.

Worked example

Fictional case: Jordan, 61, has aphasia after a stroke. During a visit they say "yes" to several questions, but point away when the worker moves to proceed. Their partner answers every question quickly. Jordan reads key words and can point to options when given time. The worker needs to confirm a preference about an appointment, not make a capacity determination. Choose a communication-access step.

Best supported: support the adult's communication and document uncertainty; consult SLP/qualified team as needed.

Safety and scope

New or sudden language difficulty, weakness, facial changes or other acute neurological signs require emergency medical response. Do not use this tool to assess stroke, consent validity or decisional capacity independently.

Staff and care partners can support communication, observe and coordinate within their authorized role. Diagnosis, treatment selection and specialized interventions require appropriate training and supervision. This page is not a crisis service.
