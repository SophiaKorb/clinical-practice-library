Chronic pain: pacing, function and access — Visual support map

Clinician formulation guideInteractive case practiceVisual support mapClient workbookCare-partner guideProgress review tool

Adults · Presentation → Understanding → Assessment → Intervention → Tools → Monitoring

Pain can vary across tasks and days, with effects on sleep, mood, movement, work and relationships. A person may alternate overactivity with prolonged recovery, or avoid valued activity because they expect worsening. Pain must not be dismissed as merely psychological.

How the parts relate

  1. Valued activityWhy it matters; task demand
  2. Available capacityPain, sleep, health, context
  3. Activity + restFeasible pattern, not endurance test
  4. Delayed effectRecovery, flare, participation cost
  5. Adapt with teamAccess, care, next choice

This map organizes a conversation; it does not establish causation or calculate risk. Follow the sequence for one event, then return to context and competing explanations. The arrows show a proposed relationship to examine, not proof.

Clinician layer: what the map could miss

Task/recovery pattern

A boom-and-bust pattern may relate to pressure, limited opportunity or fear of letting others down.

Access and environment

Position, tools, transport, workload and flexibility may change the cost of participation.

Medical/co-occurring needs

New symptoms, condition changes, sleep and mood merit assessment; a pacing response is not diagnostic proof.

Differentiate chronic primary and secondary pain through qualified assessment; they can coexist and require different medical approaches. Psychological and rehabilitation supports target living with pain and participation, not proving the pain unreal. A pacing plan balances meaningful activity, rest, capacity and flare management. It should not demand fixed increases regardless of symptoms or use reduced pain as the only success criterion.

Worked example

Fictional case: Elena cleans the whole home on a lower-pain day, then cannot join a valued family activity for two days. They fear others will call them lazy if they stop early. The clinician has not assessed whether new symptoms require medical attention. Elena wants to keep the family activity and would accept task sharing. Choose a collaborative next step.

Best supported: tailor pacing and environment, then review function and delayed costs.

Safety and scope

New neurological changes, injury, systemic symptoms or other concerning pain changes need appropriate medical assessment. This page does not establish that a flare is benign or recommend medication changes.

Staff and care partners can support communication, observe and coordinate within their authorized role. Diagnosis, treatment selection and specialized interventions require appropriate training and supervision. This page is not a crisis service.
