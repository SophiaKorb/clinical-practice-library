Bipolar: episodes, baseline and support — Clinician formulation guide

Clinician formulation guideInteractive case practiceVisual support mapClient workbookCare-partner guideProgress review tool

Adults · Presentation → Understanding → Assessment → Intervention → Tools → Monitoring

A change in sleep, speech, activity, irritability or spending becomes clinically meaningful through its time course, relationship to baseline and effect on functioning. A lively day, chronic distractibility or a stressful argument does not establish a mood episode.

Understanding the pattern

Build a longitudinal formulation before treating isolated features as a diagnosis. Reduced need for sleep means markedly less sleep with unexpectedly sustained energy; insomnia often leaves the person tired. These can coexist. Ask about depressive episodes, mixed features, psychotic experiences, medications, substances, health changes and the person's own account of what changed. Obtain collateral with permission and preserve disagreements rather than choosing the loudest account.

Keep competing explanations open

Episodic mood change

A bounded cluster of altered mood/energy, sleep and behavior differs from the person's baseline. Establish duration and consequences; this sheet does not supply diagnostic thresholds.

Longstanding traits or context

ADHD traits, shift work, stress and excitement may explain some features. Reconstruct earlier functioning and settings rather than assigning a new label from one visit.

Substance, medication or medical contribution

Recent changes in stimulants, steroids, antidepressants, substance use, thyroid or other health problems require qualified evaluation. Do not instruct medication changes.

Ask the person or caregiver

  • When did people first notice a change, and what was different from your usual self?
  • After sleeping less, did you feel exhausted, or surprisingly able to keep going?
  • What happened to work, money, relationships and decisions during that period?
  • Who would you like involved, and what would they say helped previously?
Instructions for the interviewer

Ask one question at a time, use the person's preferred language/response mode, and distinguish direct observations, attributed reports and hypotheses. Do not read these instructions as interview questions. Ask permission before collateral contact and explain confidentiality limits. Record contradictions and missing information.

Intervention selection and coordination

Clarify the episode timeline

Place sleep, energy, mood, decisions and treatment changes on the same dates. Mark uncertain intervals rather than reconstructing false precision.

Coordinate assessment and a shared response plan

A qualified clinician evaluates the episode and risk; involve psychiatric/medical care as indicated. Staff can observe and relay changes, not diagnose mania.

Protect routines and valued roles

Agree realistic sleep/routine supports, spending safeguards chosen by the person, and early contact routes. These complement treatment rather than replacing it.

Safety and scope

Rapidly escalating activation, unsafe decisions, inability to care for basic needs, suicidal thinking, psychosis or substantial sleep loss with rising energy needs prompt clinical assessment; imminent danger or medical instability uses the local urgent response pathway.

Staff and care partners can support communication, observe and coordinate within their authorized role. Diagnosis, treatment selection and specialized interventions require appropriate training and supervision. This page is not a crisis service.
