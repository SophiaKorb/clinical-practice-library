Fictional case: Sam, 22, has begun hearing a critical voice in crowded places. They skip meals in the shared kitchen because the voice says others are laughing. Sam denies wanting to hurt anyone and says talking with a cousin sometimes helps. They are unsure whether to disclose this to the early-psychosis service. Their case manager wants to help them eat and remain connected. The voice has not been assessed for instructions, perceived power, substance exposure, sleep loss or health changes. Worked formulation and supervision prompts

Separate what the person experiences from the meaning they make of it and the response that follows. Culture, grief, trauma, sleep, hearing changes, mood, substances and medical conditions can matter. Engagement is strengthened by acknowledging distress without confirming a contested explanation. Collaborative coping targets practical safety, valued activity and distress; it does not require winning an argument about what is real.

What still needs assessment?

  • How would you describe the experience in your own words?
  • When is it more or less distressing, and what helps you stay in control?
  • Does it tell you to do anything? How likely do you feel you are to act, and what keeps you safe?
  • What part of your day would you most like to regain?

Explain what evidence would change the plan, what your role permits, and who owns the next handoff. Review your reasoning with a supervisor.

Safety and scope

Command content needs assessment of intention, perceived control, means, distress and protections; content alone is not a risk classification. Imminent harm, inability to meet basic needs, acute confusion or major deterioration requires urgent local response.

Use these tools for communication, observation and coordination within your role. Follow your training, supervision and agency requirements for diagnosis and treatment. For a crisis, use local emergency services.
