Psychosis: voices, distress and collaborative coping — Clinician formulation guide

Clinician formulation guideInteractive case practiceVisual support mapClient workbookCare-partner guideProgress review tool

Adults · Presentation → Understanding → Assessment → Intervention → Tools → Monitoring

A person may hear voices, feel watched or struggle to organize thoughts. First understand the experience, distress, safety, communication and functioning. Hearing a voice alone does not establish schizophrenia, dangerousness or lack of decision-making ability.

Understanding the pattern

Separate what the person experiences from the meaning they make of it and the response that follows. Culture, grief, trauma, sleep, hearing changes, mood, substances and medical conditions can matter. Engagement is strengthened by acknowledging distress without confirming a contested explanation. Collaborative coping targets practical safety, valued activity and distress; it does not require winning an argument about what is real.

Keep competing explanations open

Experience and appraisal

A voice may be less disruptive in one context than another. Ask about content, perceived power, control, distress and what the person has tried.

Episode and contributing conditions

Clarify onset, mood, substances, medications, sleep, cognition and health. Sudden confusion or altered consciousness is not simply a coping problem.

Role and environment

Isolation, stigma, housing insecurity and overwhelming demands can worsen functioning. Supports should address these directly alongside clinical treatment.

Ask the person or caregiver

  • How would you describe the experience in your own words?
  • When is it more or less distressing, and what helps you stay in control?
  • Does it tell you to do anything? How likely do you feel you are to act, and what keeps you safe?
  • What part of your day would you most like to regain?
Instructions for the interviewer

Ask one question at a time, use the person's preferred language/response mode, and distinguish direct observations, attributed reports and hypotheses. Do not read these instructions as interview questions. Ask permission before collateral contact and explain confidentiality limits. Record contradictions and missing information.

Intervention selection and coordination

Establish a shared target

Agree one concrete goal such as staying through lunch, taking a chosen walk or contacting support. Keep symptom relief and role participation separate.

Offer a choice of coping experiments

Explore a previously helpful safe activity, reduced overload, social contact or attention-shifting strategy. Check whether it actually helps; some sensory strategies increase distress.

Coordinate early or ongoing care

New or worsening psychosis warrants qualified assessment and appropriate early-intervention services. Psychological and rehabilitation supports complement individualized treatment.

Safety and scope

Command content needs assessment of intention, perceived control, means, distress and protections; content alone is not a risk classification. Imminent harm, inability to meet basic needs, acute confusion or major deterioration requires urgent local response.

Staff and care partners can support communication, observe and coordinate within their authorized role. Diagnosis, treatment selection and specialized interventions require appropriate training and supervision. This page is not a crisis service.
