Substance use disorder: what counts?

How do clinicians tell the difference between using a substance and having a substance use disorder? This interactive lesson teaches you what to look for, why it matters, and what questions to ask next.

A criterion is one thing a clinician looks for when deciding whether someone might have a disorder. Criteria means more than one. For example, regularly drinking more than you meant to is a possible criterion. Simply drinking alcohol is not.

Clinicians look at what happened, how it affected the person's life, and whether the problems occurred within the same 12 months. They check several possible signs before deciding what the evidence means. A checklist by itself cannot give someone a diagnosis.

Teaching aid, not a diagnostic instrument. Use fictional cases, not identifiable patient information. The checkboxes do not verify diagnostic criteria, clinical significance, exclusions, remission, or substance-specific DSM-5-TR rules. This worksheet cannot establish a diagnosis, even with all boxes answered. Never use this count alone to assign a diagnosis or level of care.
First, a useful word

What is a criterion?

A criterion is one specific sign or pattern that clinicians look for when deciding whether a diagnosis fits. Think of it as one requirement on a diagnostic checklist—not a diagnosis by itself.

Example: craving

If someone reports a strong urge to use alcohol, that may meet the craving criterion. It tells us one thing about their experience. It does not, on its own, mean they have alcohol use disorder.

For many substance use disorders, clinicians consider 11 possible criteria occurring within the same 12-month period. Usually at least two must be met, along with the applicable diagnostic requirements. Each criterion needs adequate evidence, and some substance classes have exceptions.

Remember: A criterion = one feature to check. Evidence = the information that helps decide if it is present. A diagnosis = a clinical conclusion based on the full pattern, not just a box checked.

Define the assessment

The supervised-treatment exception is considered for prescribed opioids, stimulants, and sedatives/anxiolytics. Select “Yes” only when both tolerance and withdrawal, if present, arise solely from appropriate medical supervision; otherwise choose “Uncertain” and evaluate those criteria separately. The dropdown is disabled for other classes; clinical exceptions still require individual verification.

Use “Not supported” only when the available information actually weighs against the criterion; absence of an assessment is “Unclear.” A criterion should be marked “Supported” only when the full relevant requirement is established, not merely suggested. “Unclear” keeps diagnostic uncertainty visible.

Medical cannabis: what changes?

Using cannabis for a medical reason does not by itself mean someone has cannabis use disorder. A medical authorization also does not rule it out. Ask about control, impairment, harms, the 12-month pattern, and treatment context. Tolerance and withdrawal in medically supervised situations require individual, substance-specific evaluation; they are not automatically removed from the count here.

Clinical reasoning before a conclusion

Check clinically significant impairment or distress; substance type and diagnostic exceptions; whether evidence genuinely occurred in one 12-month interval; medical and psychiatric explanations; intoxication/withdrawal syndromes; co-occurrence; and any acute safety concerns.

Important: Tolerance or withdrawal arising solely from appropriate medically supervised use may be excluded as applicable; verify the specific substance and the circumstances of each criterion. Do not exclude both automatically based only on the existence of a prescription. Withdrawal criteria are not defined for some substance classes (including hallucinogens, inhalants, and PCP); verify the substance-specific DSM-5-TR criteria before using a tally. Medical withdrawal risk needs separate assessment; alcohol or sedative withdrawal can be dangerous.

Severity by supported criteria, when all diagnostic conditions are independently verified: 2–3 mild, 4–5 moderate, 6+ severe. A 0–1 count is below the usual SUD threshold, but does not establish safety or rule out other problems.

After the assessment · Treatment and specifiers

MAT / MOUD and remission or maintenance therapy

MAT means medication-assisted treatment. MOUD means medications for opioid use disorder. Methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone are used for opioid use disorder; medication status alone does not prove current misuse.

Medication treatments also exist for alcohol use disorder, including naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram. A clinician considers current criteria, remission, and whether someone is on maintenance therapy when recording diagnostic specifiers. Treatment is not an automatic diagnosis or a severity score.
