Before opening the manual

Why classify anything?

People classify things for many reasons. Sometimes a category helps with a decision. Other times it helps us remember something, share an experience, or name a connection that matters to us.

We might group groceries by allergens, clothes by washing instructions, or songs by the people we associate with them. Someone might group together foods from Mexico because they bring back memories of family or a place they miss. That is a perfectly good category. Categories can be practical, personal, emotional, cultural, or simply interesting.

Practical

Group groceries by storage needs or ingredients someone cannot eat.

Shared

Use familiar words to explain a pattern to someone else.

Personal

Group foods, places, or songs because of a memory, identity, or feeling.

Example: the same things, different meaningsYou can sort laundry by washing instructions or by who owns it. You can also keep a group of clothes that remind you of someone. The first two systems are practical. The last one is personal. None is the only correct way to group those items.

Personal categories help people describe experiences and identities in ways that matter to them. Formal diagnostic categories serve a different purpose. They follow shared criteria and help guide assessment, research, and care. Neither captures everything about a person. Both can become reductive if we ignore individual differences, memories, culture, or context.

Before asking “What diagnosis fits?” find out what the person means. Are they naming a memory, a sense of belonging, a problem they need help with, or a possible symptom? Sometimes listening or reassurance comes first. When a formal diagnosis is needed, the assessment follows its own criteria.

Think a little deeper (expand when useful)

People create categories for different reasons+

The same groceries could be grouped by ingredients, where they were grown, favorite recipes, or foods that remind someone of childhood in Mexico. One grouping helps with allergies, another with cooking, and another with memory. They do not have to compete.

Some categories are shared and standardized. Others are private, playful, or idiosyncratic. When someone offers a category, ask what it means to them before deciding whether it serves your purpose.

Classification is not the same as explanation+

Calling something a “reading difficulty” describes a problem, but not its cause. The difficulty might involve decoding, comprehension, attention, inaccessible instructions, or several factors. A category may tell us what is happening while leaving why unanswered.

A label names a pattern. It does not explain everything behind it.

What if categories make people feel reduced to a label?+

That concern is reasonable. Classification can carry stigma or make a rich experience sound like a single problem. Use categories with humility; describe strengths, context, functional needs, and the person's preferred language. Recognizing someone's identity and lived experience does not require declaring that every diagnostic claim has been clinically established.

We can use a label for a specific task and still recognize a person as much more than that label.

2

Orientation

What is the DSM?

The DSM is a manual that provides shared descriptions and criteria for mental disorders.

DSM stands for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. In the United States, the current text-revised fifth edition is called DSM-5-TR.

What it gives you

Shared names and descriptions of disorders.

What it does not give you

A complete explanation of someone's experiences, circumstances, or needs.

Who uses it

Therapists, psychologists, other qualified assessors, supervised trainees, and researchers.

Think of it as a map of diagnostic categories, not a complete map of someone’s life. A map of Tucson can show you where Sabino Canyon is; it cannot explain why someone scheduled a hike there for noon in July.

Go deeper: diagnostic label versus clinical formulation+

A diagnosis identifies a recognizable symptom pattern. A formulation asks how this particular person's difficulties developed or are maintained, considering their strengths, history, environment, relationships, developmental and medical context. Two people can share a diagnosis and need quite different supports.

The DSM helps with shared classification. It does not do the formulation for you.

3

From observation to pattern

Symptom ≠ diagnosis

A symptom is an experience or problem. A diagnosis is a carefully evaluated pattern—not one symptom on its own.

Example: losing track of a sentenceSomeone says, “I've read the same message four times and still can't tell you what it said.” This describes a concentration difficulty. It does not tell us what caused it. Sleep, stress, physical health, medication, substances, and mental health conditions could all warrant exploration. That single report is not enough to establish a diagnosis.

Therapists and assessors also consider distress (how much the person is suffering) and functioning (what has become harder in daily life). Neither is a judgment about someone's character.

4

Language and diagnostic precision

Everyday words versus clinical terms

Many diagnostic terms are also everyday words. Their meanings depend on the context.

People may use words such as depressed, panic, trauma, and OCD to describe their experiences, sometimes informally. In a clinical setting, those same words may refer to specific patterns, criteria, and important distinctions. Everyday and diagnostic meanings serve different purposes. Start by asking what the person means.

Everyday language

“I'm totally manic today.” What does this person mean?

Clinical questions

What changed in mood, energy, sleep, behavior, and functioning? For how long?

Careful documentation

Record the person's exact words and observed facts. Do not turn a phrase into a diagnosis.

The essential distinction: A person's words tell us where to begin asking questions. They do not establish a diagnosis on their own. A client may use a clinical term accurately, casually, or in a personal way. Clarify rather than correct or dismiss them.

Compare the meanings (select a topic to expand)

Mood and energy: “depressed,” “manic,” “bipolar”+

Everyday speech

“Depressed” sometimes means sad, disappointed, or discouraged. “Manic” may mean exceptionally busy or enthusiastic. “Bipolar” is sometimes (inaccurately) used for abrupt mood changes.

Clinical meaning

Depressive disorders involve defined patterns, not any sad day. Manic and hypomanic episodes involve characteristic changes in mood and energy/activity with associated symptoms and duration requirements. Bipolar disorders are diagnosed from the specific episode history and differential. Emotional variability alone is not enough.

Clinical translation: “You mentioned feeling manic. When you say that, what was different about your mood, sleep, activity, or behavior?”

Finishing an entire chore list before breakfast does not, by itself, establish a manic episode.

Fear and stress: “anxiety,” “panic attack,” “trauma”+

Everyday speech

“Anxiety” can mean ordinary anticipatory worry. “Panic attack” may describe any moment of intense upset. “Trauma” may describe a wide range of painful experiences.

Clinical meaning

Anxiety disorders require their respective symptom patterns, duration, and relevant distress or impairment criteria. A panic attack is a particular abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort with associated symptoms; it does not by itself mean panic disorder. PTSD has a specific qualifying exposure requirement and other criteria, although experiences outside that definition can still be harmful and deserving of support.

Clinical translation: “When you say ‘panic attack,’ what did you feel in your body, how quickly did it begin, and how long did it last?”

Behavior and labels: “OCD,” “antisocial,” “narcissistic”+

Everyday speech

“OCD” may mean orderly or particular. “Antisocial” often means preferring a quiet evening. “Narcissist” is sometimes used for someone who behaves selfishly.

Clinical meaning

OCD involves obsessions and/or compulsions with specific clinical features and impact. Antisocial personality disorder concerns a pervasive pattern of disregard for others' rights and has specific history and age-related criteria. It does not mean introversion. Narcissistic personality disorder requires a persistent pattern assessed in context, not evidence from one unpleasant disagreement.

Clinical translation: “What actions have you noticed? When do they happen, and how do they affect you?”

Preferring a quiet evening at home does not establish antisocial personality disorder.

Perception and attention: “psychotic,” “dissociating,” “ADHD”+

Everyday speech

“Psychotic” is sometimes used unfairly as an insult or synonym for unusual. “Dissociating” can refer to zoning out, absorption, or experiences of disconnection. “ADHD” may be used loosely to mean being distracted.

Clinical meaning

Psychosis is a clinical term referring to features such as delusions, hallucinations, or disorganization and requires careful differential assessment. Dissociation can include ordinary absorption or zoning out, as well as more disruptive changes in awareness, memory, identity, or perception. Zoning out can be a dissociative experience; it does not by itself establish a dissociative disorder. Clarify the quality, frequency, context, distress, and effects on functioning. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition with diagnostic requirements for symptoms, history, settings, functional impact, and alternative explanations.

Clinical translation: “When you say you zoned out, were you absorbed in something, did time seem to pass differently, or did you feel disconnected in some other way?”

Personality disorders are more complicated than personality traits

Words like selfish, dramatic, controlling, avoidant, or narcissistic are everyday judgments or descriptions. None is a diagnosis by itself. Personality disorders involve longstanding patterns in thinking, emotions, relationships, or behavior that occur across situations and meet specific diagnostic criteria. The pattern needs to be understood in its developmental and cultural context. The assessor considers the individual's cultural and developmental context, history, and alternative explanations.

Pattern over time

Is it persistent across situations and stages of life, rather than only during a crisis?

Context and alternatives

What about trauma, attachment, mood episodes, neurodevelopment, substances, health, or an unsafe environment?

Impact and adaptation

What distress, functional limitations, strengths, coping strategies, and changes do we actually observe?

Why one difficult interaction proves almost nothing+

A client might disagree with a therapist, decline a recommendation, or express fear of abandonment. Each reaction could have several explanations, including immediate stress, past experiences, communication barriers, or a broader relationship pattern. A personality disorder cannot be established from one conflict, an observer's complaint, or a checklist taken out of context.

Example: “My sister disagreed with me about holiday plans” is not the same as “my sister has narcissistic personality disorder.” The first statement describes a disagreement. The second makes a diagnostic claim that cannot be supported by that disagreement alone.

Longstanding does not mean hopeless or unchangeable+

Patterns can be deeply established and still change. With appropriate care and support, people with personality disorders can make meaningful progress in relationships, emotional regulation, and functioning. Psychotherapy can be effective. Dialectical behavior therapy is one approach used to treat borderline personality disorder. A diagnosis is not a moral verdict or a prediction that a person will remain the same.

Why diagnosis requires special care with development and culture+

Personality continues to develop through adolescence and early adulthood. Age, developmental history, and diagnosis-specific criteria therefore matter. DSM rules include special limitations for some personality diagnoses. What looks “unusual” in one cultural context may be understood differently in another. Persistent survival responses to danger may also look like stable traits until the environmental context is understood.

Different classification systems and models can describe personality pathology differently, including dimensional approaches that consider the severity of personality-functioning difficulties and maladaptive traits. The DSM-5-TR retains categorical personality diagnoses in its main diagnostic section alongside an alternative model for further study; do not substitute a trait score for a diagnosis.

For a grounded overview, see the American Psychiatric Association's personality disorder explanation, NIMH's definition, and NIMH's guide to borderline personality disorder and treatment.

Practice: translate the quote into a defensible note+

Casual: “He's bipolar because his mood changes every ten minutes.”

Better: “Caregiver reports rapid mood shifts during disagreements. Onset, duration, associated symptoms, sleep, context, and alternative explanations have not yet been evaluated.”

Casual: “She's OCD about her workspace.”

Better: “Person reports a strong preference for keeping work materials arranged in a specific order; intrusive thoughts, repetitive behavior, distress, and interference have not yet been assessed.”

Client's own words: “I had a panic attack.”

Better: “Client describes the episode as a ‘panic attack.’ Further assessment will clarify onset, physical and cognitive symptoms, duration, context, and impact.”

These are fictional teaching examples, not templates to paste as if an assessment occurred. Preserve attribution and the person's preferred language.

5

Before choosing a label

Six questions that change the picture

The goal is to understand the pattern before searching for a diagnosis.

  1. What? What is happening? Use the person's own words and observable facts.
  2. When? When did it begin? How often? How long does it last?
  3. Where? In which situations does it happen? When does it not happen?
  4. How much? What distress or changes in daily life are present?
  5. What else? Could sleep, health, medication, substances, development, environment, trauma, or culture help explain it?
  6. Safety? Are there urgent concerns that require immediate action or consultation?
A useful first sentence“Tell me what has been happening, and when you first noticed it.”

These are learning prompts, not complete diagnostic criteria, a screening instrument, or a replacement for a trained safety assessment. Before choosing a diagnosis, find out what is happening and what may be contributing to it.

6

Understanding the landscape

Where does the pattern belong?

The DSM groups related conditions into chapters. Learn how the categories relate, then consult the actual criteria rather than trying to memorize the entire manual.

Mood

Examples: depressive and bipolar-related conditions. They are different categories.

Fear and worry

Examples: anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive-related disorders. They also have distinct chapters.

Development and attention

Examples: autism, ADHD, and learning-related disorders.

Trauma and stress

Conditions associated with certain kinds of stressors; experiences vary.

Perception and thinking

Examples: schizophrenia-spectrum and other psychotic disorders.

Substance use

Patterns involving alcohol, drugs, or other substances that require careful assessment.

Important: These are informal teaching labels, not official DSM chapter names or a complete list. Similar-looking symptoms can appear in several categories. A diagnosis needs the specific criteria and differential assessment. The table of contents is a starting point, not a verdict.

Learning through cases

Two case studies

Read a story. Separate what you know from what you still need to ask.

Case A: Maya

“I have stopped seeing friends. I feel worn out most days.”

What do we know?

Maya reports reduced social contact and low energy. No diagnosis can be established from this sentence.

What would you ask?

For how long? What changed? Sleep? Mood? Health? Stress? Safety? Impact on activities? What does Maya think is happening?

Case B: Eli

“He gets upset when the teacher gives several spoken instructions and hands out a crowded worksheet.”

What do we know?

Someone reports a situation-specific pattern; we do not yet know the child's perspective, communication needs, or contributing conditions.

What would you ask?

What happens before and after? Is the direction understood? What works in other settings? What about learning, language, sensory load, worry, and access?

8

From hypotheses to decisions

How the DSM supports assessment and treatment

The DSM helps organize diagnostic questions and compare possible explanations. Assessment still requires interviewing, context, judgment, and supervision when indicated.

1 · Listen

Understand the person and their goals.

2 · Compare

Review plausible diagnoses and full current criteria, including exclusions.

3 · Check again

Consider health, culture, age, function, safety, and missing information; consult as needed.

You may hear the term differential diagnosis. It simply means considering which of several explanations best fits the evidence and what would distinguish them.

What about diagnostic codes? In U.S. practice, DSM-5-TR diagnoses use ICD-10-CM codes. A code is for classification and reporting; it is not the entire clinical explanation. A code supports accurate reporting. A formulation helps the treatment team understand the person's needs and plan care. Verify current codes and updates before use.
Three words to recognize

Criterion: one requirement within a diagnostic definition. Specifier: extra detail that applies to certain diagnoses. Comorbidity: more than one condition may be present. Learn what the terms mean. Look up the exact criteria and coding guidance when you need them.

Continue the training: Use the clinical intern learning hub or workforce learning center for structured interviewing, formulation, and supervised practice.

Go deeper: what belongs in the differential?+

A useful differential is a short, reasoned comparison of plausible explanations. For each possibility, identify supporting features, contradictory or missing features, and what would change your assessment. Consider nonpsychiatric explanations and co-occurring conditions rather than treating every case as a contest with one winner.

Personal meaning and clinical purpose

Diagnosis, identity & self-knowledge

A diagnosis is a clinical description. Identity is how someone understands themselves. They can be connected, but one is not a substitute for the other.

Diagnosis

“Which clinical pattern, if any, fits?” Requires evidence, criteria, context, and diagnostic judgment.

Identity

“What does this experience mean to me?” Includes self-understanding, community, preferred language, and values.

For some people, autism, ADHD, or DID is an important part of their identity or the language that helps them understand their experiences. Other people relate differently to those diagnoses. A diagnosis does not decide how someone must describe their identity.

Identity and clinical knowledge (select a topic to expand)

Autism, ADHD, DID: diagnoses can matter to identity+

For some people, autism is not merely a clinical category: being autistic shapes their understanding of communication, sensory experience, community, relationships, strengths, and needs. For some, ADHD helps explain patterns of attention, motivation, time, creativity, and the effort of everyday tasks. These may be important parts of identity; they are not everybody's experience, and they do not define a person completely.

For some people with dissociative identity disorder (DID), their experience of identity, dissociative parts, internal relationships, and continuity is central to how they understand themselves. People may use terms such as parts, alters, or system; ask what words they use rather than assigning an identity description to them. The clinical diagnosis of DID is a distinct question involving specific dissociative symptoms and careful assessment. Not everyone who uses plurality-related language meets criteria for DID, and not everyone with DID has the same relationship to these terms.

Notice the two questions: “What does this experience mean to you?” and “What clinical diagnosis, if any, is established?” Respecting a personal answer to the first question does not require pretending the second has already been settled.

Self-knowledge does not require medical validation+

You do not need a therapist's permission to notice your own patterns. People can recognize that crowded rooms exhaust them, that written instructions make tasks easier, that memory gaps are frightening, that they need more recovery time, or that certain words and communities help them understand their lives. Those experiences, needs, and preferences matter whether or not a diagnosis is on record.

A formal evaluation can provide additional information, reveal alternative or co-occurring explanations, guide treatment, or supply documentation a particular institution requires. An evaluation can add useful information without displacing the person's existing self-knowledge.

A useful distinction: “This language helps me understand myself” is a personal statement. “I meet DSM-5-TR criteria for this disorder” is a clinical claim that requires assessment. A therapist can respect the former without automatically certifying the latter.

Example: “I know I struggle with rapid spoken directions and benefit from written steps.” That is useful, legitimate self-knowledge. An evaluation may help explain that difficulty, but the person understood their need for written steps before receiving any diagnosis.
Identity-first, person-first, and personally chosen words+

Identity-first: “autistic person,” “disabled person.” Person-first: “person with autism,” “person with a disability.” Some people care deeply about this distinction; others do not. Neither choice should be imposed as the only respectful possibility.

People may welcome a diagnostic label, reject it, find parts of it useful, or hold several descriptions at once. A person can value a neurodivergent identity and seek help with symptoms, impairment, or distress. A person can dislike a diagnostic label and still benefit from support. Ask rather than presume.

Therapist language: “What words do you prefer? What do they mean to you? Is there anything you want me to avoid?”
Good documentation keeps three kinds of information separate+

Self-description: “Client describes themself as neurodivergent and states that written directions have always improved access to tasks.”

Reported or verified diagnosis: “Client reports prior ADHD diagnosis” is not interchangeable with “ADHD established by evaluation.” Document the source and confidence of the information rather than silently upgrading a self-report into a confirmed diagnosis.

Functional impact: “Client requires written directions to complete multistep work accurately; rapid verbal instructions are associated with missed steps.” Record observed and reported limitations, environmental barriers, and supports.

The three can be related, but one does not automatically prove another. Respectful listening and clinically accurate documentation belong together.

Diagnosis is useful. It is also imperfect.

Clinical diagnosis can be helpful without being the final word on a person's life. It organizes information into recognizable patterns, but may flatten individual differences, miss important context, or become a label people mistakenly use as an explanation for everything. Diagnoses and criteria can change as research develops. Stigma and stereotypes are real concerns, and culture and environment shape how difficulties are experienced and understood.

None of this makes diagnosis disposable. Used carefully, it gives professionals a common language, helps distinguish possible explanations, connects people to relevant research and treatments, and supports communication, continuity of care, referrals, and clinically defensible documentation.

Example: the very efficient referral“Anxiety” might be enough for a casual conversation. For a referral or clinical record, the assessor may need to establish whether the concerns fit a particular anxiety disorder, another condition, or do not yet support a definitive diagnosis. The referral may need a diagnostic impression, but the therapist still has to understand the person's experience. The diagnosis and code must be supported by assessment, not used in place of it.
Where diagnosis can get things wrong+

A category can obscure differences between people who meet the same criteria, encourage premature certainty, or be mistaken for a cause rather than a description of a pattern. Experiences shaped by culture, neurodevelopment, trauma, poverty, physical illness, and inaccessible environments can be misunderstood when the assessor stops asking questions after finding a matching label.

An assessor should treat a diagnosis as an evidence-informed conclusion that may need to change when new information emerges. Criticism should make the use of diagnosis more thoughtful, not dismiss the real distress or needs people describe.

Why diagnosis is necessary in much clinical work+

Clinical communication: Shared diagnostic criteria help professionals explain which presentation they are treating, select relevant evidence-based options, and clarify when a specialist referral is appropriate.

Documentation and care coordination: Clinical records often need a defensible diagnostic impression that matches the history and findings. Treatment plans and monitoring also need the person's actual goals and functional needs; a diagnosis alone is not a treatment plan.

Billing and administration: In U.S. covered health-care transactions, the ICD-10-CM system is used for diagnosis coding, including insurance claims. The DSM-5-TR supplies clinical descriptions and criteria; the codes used for covered claims are ICD-10-CM codes. Some encounters warrant symptom-based coding when a definitive disorder is not established; which services are reimbursable and how they must be documented depends on the payer and setting.

Professional responsibility: A qualified professional should diagnose only within their role and competence, show the evidence behind the conclusion, consider differential diagnoses, and revisit the assessment when new information appears. A diagnostic code is administratively useful, but it is not a substitute for reasoning.

Primary sources: American Psychiatric Association: DSM and ICD · CMS: ICD-10-CM coding.

Identity can be personally meaningful without clinical certification+

“This way of understanding my experience matters to me” is not the same claim as “a clinical evaluation establishes a particular diagnosis.” Both conversations deserve respect and clarity. A therapist should not invalidate someone's observations or chosen words merely because the chart has no code; likewise, respect for self-understanding does not require presenting an unassessed condition as formally diagnosed.

Clinical principle: Listen to the person's meaning, assess diagnostic claims carefully, and use formal classifications as tools for care, not definitions of the person.

Functioning in context

Disability, functioning & access

Disability is not a synonym for a diagnosis. It concerns the interaction between health-related impairments, daily activities, participation, and the environments in which people live and work.

Functioning

What can the person do? What is difficult? In which domains, and with how much effort?

Environment

What barriers make participation more difficult? What settings are more accessible?

Access & support

Which adaptations, accommodations, assistance, and conditions change what is possible?

Example: good grades, limited accessRobin earns strong grades in social studies but misses key information when teachers give multiple spoken instructions in a noisy classroom. With written steps and a quiet space, Robin succeeds. A teacher points to Robin's strong grades as evidence that the supports are unnecessary. But the grades may reflect how much those supports help. Good grades are not a complete assessment of functioning.

Two people can share a diagnosis but have different support needs. Someone may have a substantial limitation without a DSM mental health diagnosis. And effective adaptations can improve functioning without making the underlying limitation disappear.

Disability and practical participation (select a topic to expand)

Diagnosis does not equal disability severity+

Clinical criteria describe disorders. Disability assessment asks what a person can do, what is difficult, what it takes, and what support changes the situation. Memory, concentration, communication, learning, self-care, social participation, independent living, stamina, and work can be affected differently in different people.

Someone may appear capable during a short appointment but require extensive help outside it. Someone else may perform very well at school or work only through extraordinary effort and accommodations. A successful task does not by itself show that an impairment is absent.

For example, a person with DID may develop greater internal cooperation yet continue to have major difficulties with memory or daily functioning. Document each domain independently: progress in one area does not erase limitations in another.

Environment, access, and accommodations+

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) considers health-related functioning in context, including activities, participation, and environmental factors. Accessibility changes what is possible; it need not change someone's diagnosis.

In the United States, disability rights and accommodations involve applicable legal standards, not merely a DSM code. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) recognizes impairments affecting major life activities, and its rules address episodic impairments and mitigating measures. Benefits programs, school services, and workplace accommodations may have different documentation requirements.

Practical question: Instead of stopping at “Can the person do this?”, ask “Under which conditions, at what effort or cost, and with which supports?”

This lesson does not decide disability benefits or legal eligibility. Seek program-specific guidance for those decisions.

What changes in work, school, and independent living?+

Describe specific domains: memory, concentration, communication, learning, self-care, completing tasks, social interaction, stamina, independence, and consistent attendance or performance. Ask what happens with and without support, how reliable functioning is over time, and what it costs the person to perform a task.

For example, a person with DID may have greater internal cooperation while continuing to experience substantial gaps in daily memory. Progress in one domain should not be mistaken for full restoration of functioning in another. Record what was reported, observed, or formally assessed.

Workplace accommodations: documentation is not always a DSM label+

Employment accommodation decisions under the U.S. ADA focus on whether someone has a covered disability, what limitations relate to work, and which effective accommodations are reasonable. When the disability or need is not obvious, an employer may ask for appropriate, limited documentation. But a particular DSM diagnosis is not automatically required for every request, and employers may choose to provide accommodations on the person's description without requesting documentation.

Similarly, school supports, disability benefits, insurance reimbursement, and employment accommodations are distinct systems with different rules. A DSM diagnosis does not automatically establish eligibility for every service, nor is it required for every accommodation.

Primary sources: EEOC: ADA guidance on accommodations · EEOC: limits on requested medical documentation.

A disability assessment is not a list of diagnoses+

Diagnosis: which disorder, if any, is clinically supported? Functioning: which activities are limited, with what frequency and intensity? Participation: what is possible in home, school, work, and community life? Supports: what helps, what is still difficult, and what barriers persist?

Document strengths alongside limitations. Documenting strengths does not cancel limitations, and describing limitations does not erase strengths. A short appointment may not demonstrate the effort, recovery time, or help required in everyday life.

Supervision reflection: what do you know, and whose knowledge is it?+
  1. What has the person told you about who they are and what helps them?
  2. Which diagnostic claims are formally established, reported, or still hypotheses?
  3. What functional difficulties, abilities, adaptations, and barriers can you describe without relying on assumptions from the diagnosis?

Both uncritical acceptance of every diagnostic claim and dismissal of self-knowledge can lead to poor care. Ask good questions, respect the person's agency, and document honestly.

Practical question: “Under which conditions can this person participate, how consistently, at what effort, and with what supports?” This tells us more than a yes-or-no question about whether the person can do the task.
