Orientation

What is the DSM?

The DSM is a book that helps trained professionals describe and classify patterns of mental health difficulties.

DSM stands for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. In the United States, the current text-revised fifth edition is called DSM-5-TR.

What it gives you

Shared names and descriptions of disorders.

What it does not give you

A mind-reading machine or an explanation of an entire person.

Who uses it

Qualified clinicians, trainees under supervision, and researchers.

Think of it as a map of diagnostic categories, not a complete map of someone’s life. A map of London can help you find King’s Cross; it cannot tell you why the person standing there looks furious at the train schedule.

2

From observation to pattern

Symptom ≠ diagnosis

A symptom is an experience or problem. A diagnosis is a carefully evaluated pattern—not one symptom on its own.

Example: the mystery browser tabSomeone says, “I’m exhausted, I can’t focus, and somewhere among my 27 browser tabs a song is playing. I cannot locate it.” Fatigue and trouble concentrating are clues. The tabs are a relatable detail, not a diagnostic criterion. Sleep, stress, physical health, medication, substances, and mental health conditions could all warrant exploration. No diagnosis follows from that sentence.

Clinicians also think about distress (how much the person is suffering) and functioning (what has become harder in daily life). Neither means the person is flawed.

3

Before choosing a label

Six questions that change the picture

The goal is to understand the pattern before searching for a diagnosis.

  1. What? What is happening? Use the person's own words and observable facts.
  2. When? When did it begin? How often? How long does it last?
  3. Where? In which situations does it happen—or not happen?
  4. How much? What distress or changes in daily life are present?
  5. What else? Could sleep, health, medication, substances, development, environment, trauma, or culture help explain it?
  6. Safety? Are there urgent concerns that require immediate action or consultation?
A useful first sentence“Tell me what has been happening, and when you first noticed it.”

These are learning prompts, not complete diagnostic criteria, a screening instrument, or a replacement for a trained safety assessment. Asking which disorder someone has before asking what happened is rather like diagnosing the printer before noticing it is unplugged.

4

Understanding the landscape

Where does the pattern belong?

The DSM groups related conditions into chapters. Learn how the categories relate, then consult the actual criteria rather than trying to memorize the entire manual.

Mood

Examples: depressive and bipolar-related conditions. They are different categories.

Fear and worry

Examples: anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive-related disorders. They also have distinct chapters.

Development and attention

Examples: autism, ADHD, and learning-related disorders.

Trauma and stress

Conditions associated with certain kinds of stressors; experiences vary.

Perception and thinking

Examples: schizophrenia-spectrum and other psychotic disorders.

Substance use

Patterns involving alcohol, drugs, or other substances that require careful assessment.

Important: These are informal teaching labels, not official DSM chapter names or a complete list. Similar-looking symptoms can appear in several categories. A diagnosis needs the specific criteria and differential assessment. The table of contents is a starting point, not a verdict.

5

Clinical reasoning in practice

Two case studies

Read a story. Separate what you know from what you still need to ask.

Case A: Maya

“I have stopped seeing friends. I feel worn out most days.”

What do we know?

Maya reports reduced social contact and low energy. No diagnosis can be established from this sentence.

What would you ask?

For how long? What changed? Sleep? Mood? Health? Stress? Safety? Impact on activities? What does Maya think is happening?

Case B: Eli

“He gets upset when the teacher gives five spoken instructions and a worksheet that appears to have a sequel.”

What do we know?

Someone reports a situation-specific pattern; we do not yet know the child's perspective, communication needs, or contributing conditions.

What would you ask?

What happens before and after? Is the direction understood? What works in other settings? What about learning, language, sensory load, worry, and access?

From hypotheses to decisions

How clinicians actually use the DSM

The DSM helps clinicians check possibilities. It does not replace interviewing, context, judgment, or supervision.

1 · Listen

Understand the person and their goals.

2 · Compare

Review plausible diagnoses and full current criteria, including exclusions.

3 · Check again

Consider health, culture, age, function, safety, and missing information; consult as needed.

You may hear the term differential diagnosis. It simply means considering which of several explanations best fits the evidence—and what would help tell them apart.

What about diagnostic codes? In U.S. practice, DSM-5-TR diagnoses use ICD-10-CM codes. A code is for classification and reporting; it is not the entire clinical explanation. The billing system may appreciate a perfectly formatted code. The person in front of you generally appreciates being understood. Verify current codes and updates before use.
Three words to recognize

Criterion: one requirement within a diagnostic definition. Specifier: extra detail that applies to certain diagnoses. Comorbidity: more than one condition may be present. Learn the terms first; look up the specifics when relevant.

Review

Key takeaways

A symptom is a clue. A diagnosis is a carefully evaluated pattern. A person is always more than a diagnosis.

In five words: Listen → Describe → Explore → Compare → Consult.

To practice, ask a supervisor to walk through a fictional vignette and name at least two possible explanations and one missing piece of information.
