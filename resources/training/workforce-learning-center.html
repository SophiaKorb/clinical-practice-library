Four learning paths

All path descriptions · available without JavaScript

High-school-diploma-level staff

Observation → communication → trauma-informed engagement → risk recognition → handoff

Practice: Describe one event without a label; rehearse offering two response routes and contacting the designated supervisor.

Boundary: Use assigned support plans and local response procedures. Do not independently diagnose, interpret tests, or design psychotherapy.

Bachelor’s-level workers & case managers

Foundations → family engagement → observation patterns → documentation → coordination

Practice: Map one access barrier, identify the responsible service, obtain required permission, and verify follow-through.

Boundary: Degree level alone does not authorize clinical tasks. Use screenings or structured interventions only when role, training, policy, and supervision permit.

Graduate clinical trainees

Foundations → interviewing → functional reasoning → supervised assessment/treatment → review

Practice: Bring two explanations, missing information, a supported intervention rationale, and a progress measure to supervision.

Boundary: Clinical assessment and treatment require the supervisor’s authorization and adequate training. Consult before interpreting specialized instruments or starting protocols.

Licensed clinicians

Formulation → evidence appraisal → adapted delivery → measurement → consultation

Practice: Audit one treatment decision for guideline fit, access, consent, response, adverse effects, and referral thresholds.

Boundary: Licensure does not confer competence in every method. Seek consultation and training for unfamiliar populations, instruments, or procedures.

Core skills: learn, rehearse, review

Observation

Replace “manipulative” with actions, exact words, time, setting, and response. Separate observation, client report, and inference.

Rehearse: Describe a fictional two-minute interaction. Ask a partner which statements could be directly observed.

Supervisor review

Does the note imply motive or a diagnosis unsupported by the facts?

Interviewing & listening

Ask one question at a time, reflect meaning, allow silence, and check understanding. Use interpreters and communication aids appropriately.

Rehearse: Role-play an opening question and a reflection. Offer pointing or writing before adding more questions.

Supervisor review

Which prompt was a client question and which was a reminder for the interviewer?

Trauma-informed engagement

Offer predictable steps, feasible choices, privacy explanations, and collaboration. Do not demand trauma disclosure to provide support.

Rehearse: Practice explaining what will happen next and how the person can request a pause.

Supervisor review

Did our procedure create unnecessary pressure, surprise, or loss of control?

Behavioral concepts

Track before/action/after, distinguish consequences from punishment, and treat function as a hypothesis. Distress is not proof of danger.

Rehearse: Compare one episode with a successful context using the functional map.

Supervisor review

What evidence would change our explanation? Who is qualified to assess function?

Family engagement

Listen to each perspective. Clarify consent, confidentiality, access, culture, and practical constraints. Avoid recruiting one person against another.

Rehearse: Rehearse a shared goal and a real choice about participation.

Supervisor review

Whose voice or needs did we miss? Are safety/reporting concerns present?

Risk recognition & escalation

Recognize concerning words or changes, ask plainly within trained procedure, and connect promptly to the designated responder.

Rehearse: Rehearse a handoff with exact words, observations, actions, recipient, and confirmed receipt.

Supervisor review

What if the clinician is unavailable? Who activates emergency response?

Documentation

Record what occurred, what you actually did, the response, and the agreed next action. Attribute reports. Avoid invented findings or copied interventions.

Rehearse: Rewrite an interpretive note in neutral language. Compare with the worked example.

Supervisor review

Can the next worker tell what happened and who is responsible?

Supervision & coordination

Bring uncertainty early, define the question, protect privacy, and verify referrals and follow-up. A referral sent is not a referral completed.

Rehearse: Use a de-identified role-play to report concern and obtain a clear next action.

Supervisor review

Who owns the next step, by when, and how will receipt and completion be checked?

No start after instructions

A young person watches the instructor, then pushes a worksheet away and says “no.” The room is loud. No immediate danger is observed.

Ask the client: “What part feels hard? You can tell me, point, or show me.”

Interviewer instruction: Offer a preferred response mode. Do not assume motive from refusal. Notice direction length, writing demand, sensory load, and what happens next.

Document “defiant and unmotivated.” That assigns motive. Record observable actions and context; the explanation remains open.

Offer one demonstrated step and ask what would help. A useful access trial. Record participation and support needed, then consider language, skill, worry, and overload without diagnosing from the result.

Remove all demands indefinitely. A pause may help; a permanent change needs collaborative review. Preserve access while assessing learning needs and goals.

Worked note: Observed: after three spoken instructions in a loud room, the student pushed the worksheet away and said “no.” Offered one demonstrated step and a quieter seat. Student attempted the first item with a model. Shared observations with the assigned clinician; cause remains uncertain.

Supervision: What skill, access, and health information would distinguish competing explanations? Which supports are appropriate now?

“I cannot do this anymore”

An adult says, “I cannot do this anymore.” Their meaning is unclear. You are a support worker, and the clinician is elsewhere in the building.

Ask the client: “When you say that, are you thinking about suicide or hurting yourself?”

Interviewer instruction: Ask plainly using your organization’s trained response procedure. Notify the designated clinician promptly. If immediate danger or urgent medical need is present, activate the emergency pathway; do not wait for a routine appointment.

Promise secrecy so they keep talking. Do not promise secrecy. Explain relevant safety limits and connect to the designated response team.

Clarify directly and promptly involve the designated clinician. Appropriate role-based recognition and handoff. A “no” answer does not settle safety. Follow local procedure and convey exact words, observations, and the response.

Calculate a risk level independently and send them home. A support worker should not independently assign clinical risk or disposition. Structured screening requires approved training and cannot replace clinical assessment.

Worked note: Client stated, “I cannot do this anymore.” Clarified meaning using the trained safety procedure and promptly notified the designated clinician. Document the actual reply, who received the handoff, time, instructions, and actions taken; do not invent responses or findings.

Supervision: Who receives a safety handoff, who confirms receipt, and what happens if that person is unavailable?

Caregiver and client disagree

A caregiver asks you to “make him cooperate.” The young person looks away and says they do not want to discuss the conflict in front of the caregiver.

Ask the client: “What would help you take part? We can talk about how to make this conversation feel safer.”

Interviewer instruction: Clarify confidentiality, consent/assent, and caregiver roles within policy. Do not take sides or promise private conversations that cannot be offered. Check for safety concerns and consult when needed.

Agree with the caregiver that compliance is the goal. That can bypass the young person’s goals and access needs. Clarify the shared concern and seek an appropriate collaborative plan.

Acknowledge both concerns, offer feasible choices, and consult about participation. Preserves engagement and scope. A choice must be real; legal consent, privacy, and safety duties still apply.

Conclude the caregiver caused trauma. The observed disagreement cannot establish trauma or causation. Gather context, consult, and follow reporting duties when facts require it.

Worked note: Caregiver requested help with cooperation. Young person looked away and requested a different setting for discussion. Acknowledged both perspectives; reviewed available participation options with the assigned clinician. Record the agreed plan and permissions, without inferring diagnosis or family motive.

Supervision: How do we preserve voice, required caregiver involvement, privacy limits, and safety in this setting?

Documentation lab

Instruction for the worker: Rewrite “Client was attention-seeking and refused to cooperate.” Use fictional facts, distinguish reports from observations, and name the action and follow-up. Do not paste a real client record.

Worked revision

During the group introduction, the participant interrupted twice and said, “I want a turn.” Offered a visible turn order and asked whether they wanted to speak or pass. Participant chose to wait and spoke when invited. Reviewed the support with the group lead. The purpose of the interruptions remains uncertain.

Only document these actions if they actually occurred. A polished note cannot supply missing facts.

Authoritative training & evidence boundaries

  • SAMHSA trauma-informed approaches · Systems and engagement principles; a framework, not one validated therapy protocol.
  • PBIS comprehensive functional assessment guide · Function-linked school support; formal assessment needs qualified teams.
  • NCTSN / VA Psychological First Aid · Evidence-informed disaster support, not a replacement for psychotherapy or routine clinical risk assessment.
  • APA, CCI, VA, Columbia, Zero Suicide & SAMHSA training references · Check course requirements and local procedures.
