ADHD–AUTISM–TRAUMA DIFFERENTIAL MATRIX

Hypothesis guide, not a forced either/or tool

PURPOSE

Use this matrix when attention problems, social difficulty, rigidity, sensory differences or dysregulation could plausibly reflect ADHD, autism, trauma, or more than one. The matrix organizes hypotheses. It does not replace developmental history, trauma assessment, collateral, functional analysis or formal diagnosis.

DEVELOPMENTAL ONSET

ADHD more likely when: attention/impulse-regulation problems are persistent from childhood and appear across relevant demands.

Autism more likely when: social-communication and restricted/repetitive/sensory patterns are evident developmentally.

Trauma more likely when: there is a meaningful change after threatening/adverse exposure, recognizing that prior vulnerability may also exist.

Collateral needed: early school/developmental records, caregivers and a timeline.

Caution: adult retrospective recall alone is weak evidence.

SOCIAL DIFFICULTY

ADHD more likely when: cues are missed because of inattention/impulsivity and performance varies substantially with engagement or task demands.

Autism more likely when: reciprocity/pragmatic differences are persistent and occur alongside developmental restricted interests, repetitive patterns or sensory features.

Trauma more likely when: withdrawal, mistrust, threat interpretation or avoidance increases after trauma or in threat-linked contexts.

Collateral needed: concrete examples across safe versus stressful settings.

Caution: social difficulty is not autism-specific.

REPETITIVE BEHAVIOR / RIGIDITY

ADHD more likely when: behavior reflects stimulation seeking, impulsive repetition or difficulty shifting.

Autism more likely when: insistence on sameness, repetitive patterns or circumscribed interests are longstanding and developmentally patterned.

Trauma more likely when: routines, checking or avoidance function primarily to increase safety or reduce threat.

Useful next step: ask what the behavior accomplishes and when it began.

Caution: topography alone does not identify function; include OCD in the differential when appropriate.

ATTENTION PROBLEMS

ADHD more likely when: attention regulation is broadly persistent, cross-situational and often sensitive to boredom, delay or executive demand.

Autism more likely when: apparent attention difficulty clusters around overload, transitions, nonpreferred demands or intense focus.

Trauma more likely when: inattention reflects hypervigilance, intrusive memories, dissociation, poor sleep or threat monitoring.

Collateral needed: setting, trigger, onset, sleep and task type.

Caution: rating scales document symptoms; they do not determine etiology.

SENSORY SENSITIVITY

ADHD more likely when: sensory seeking/sensitivity coexists with broader regulation difficulty.

Autism more likely when: sensory patterns are developmental and stable across time.

Trauma more likely when: sensitivity emerges or worsens with hyperarousal or trauma reminders.

Collateral needed: developmental course and trigger specificity.

Caution: sensory symptoms are not diagnostic by themselves.

BEST DISCRIMINATORS

ADHD: cross-situational executive/attention-regulation pattern with developmental onset and impairment.

Autism: developmental social-communication differences plus restricted/repetitive/sensory pattern.

Trauma: temporal and functional relationship between symptoms and trauma/threat systems.

All three can include impairment, dysregulation, sleep problems, social difficulty, attention problems and sensory complaints.

COLLATERAL QUESTIONS

What was present before the suspected trauma?

What was present in early childhood?

Where does the problem disappear or become much smaller?

What demands make it worse?

What happens in safe, highly structured or high-interest environments?

Is the behavior serving stimulation, predictability, escape, threat reduction, social communication, emotion regulation, or more than one function?

Are there school records, prior evaluations, caregiver observations or work samples that clarify the timeline?

ASSESSMENT RULE

Use the smallest targeted assessment that answers the remaining question. Do not administer one ADHD scale, one autism scale and one trauma scale and then let the highest number “win.”

COMORBIDITY RULE

ADHD, autism and trauma can co-occur. The goal is not to force the patient into one explanatory box. The goal is to identify which processes are present, which are impairing, and which distinctions actually change treatment or services.

ESCALATE WHEN

Formal autism/ADHD/PTSD diagnosis is high-stakes or remains uncertain after appropriate multimethod assessment.

Developmental, cognitive, language, learning or adaptive questions require formal testing.

Acute safety, psychosis, mania, medical/neurologic change or substance effects complicate the presentation.
