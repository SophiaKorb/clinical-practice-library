Clinical reasoning · Access · Learning

Assessment casebook: development, language, and learning

Three layered worked cases connect observations, competing explanations, assessment decisions, access supports, tools, and functional review.

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Cases teach reasoning, not diagnostic guessing.

There is no single winning diagnosis. Compare onset, context, response access, impairment, and alternative explanations. These fictional cases intentionally leave information missing.

Longstanding differences; a recent escalation

A fictional 12-year-old has longstanding sensory and social-communication differences, increasing distractibility, and recent avoidance after peer mistreatment. The referral asks whether autism, ADHD, trauma, or anxiety explains the difficulties.

What am I seeing? Put the evidence on a timeline

Early developmentGather concrete examples of communication, play, sensory preferences, flexibility, and attention; do not fill gaps with assumptions.
Changing demandsCompare different classrooms, language load, transitions, task interest, and the supports already in place.
Recent changeLocate bullying, sleep changes, distress, avoidance, and safety concerns on the timeline.

What might explain it? Keep competing and co-occurring explanations

Autism-related developmental pattern

Look for developmental social-communication differences alongside restricted/repetitive patterns. Sensory complaints alone are insufficient.

Next evidence / limitation: Collateral and direct observation may clarify history and present functioning; apparent competence in one setting does not settle the question.

ADHD-related attention / executive pattern

Clarify childhood onset, functional impairment, and examples across relevant settings.

Next evidence / limitation: Do not diagnose from a rating-scale elevation alone. Compare attention when language and sensory demands are accessible.

Trauma or anxiety added to earlier vulnerabilities

Ask what changed, which situations feel unsafe, and what the person avoids or anticipates.

Next evidence / limitation: Safety and trauma inquiry should be paced and private where appropriate. Recent adversity does not erase prior developmental differences.

Language, learning, sleep, or medical contributors

Explore understanding of directions, reading/math demands, fatigue, hearing, pain, and medication effects.

Next evidence / limitation: Use targeted referrals and assessment rather than assuming all difficulty belongs to the first diagnosis.

What should I assess?

State the decision, list what remains uncertain, and choose evidence that can distinguish the explanations above. Integrate history, authorized collateral, direct observation, functional information, and qualified testing as appropriate. Consider cultural and language context and access before interpreting results.

What can I do now?

While clarification proceeds, provide a predictable session map, written single-step instructions, a safe way to request a break, and a coordinated response to peer safety. Preserve chosen participation rather than requiring typical-looking behavior.

Which tool helps?

Communication access boards · Competing-hypothesis lab · Learning and access plan

How do I evaluate progress?

Track access to one chosen learning or peer activity, support level, distress, safety, and the youth’s view. Record which barriers remain even if behavior appears calmer.

Advanced supervision and uncertainty

What evidence would support multiple co-occurring explanations? What could we safely do now without deciding the diagnosis? Which uncertainty changes referral or treatment?

This case cannot be diagnosed from the vignette. There are no test scores, diagnostic verdict, or implied probability ranking.

Limited spoken output; uncertain access to testing

A fictional adult with a documented developmental autism history and established acquired aphasia is referred for cognitive and functional clarification. Short spoken answers are being mistaken for poor understanding.

What am I seeing? Put the evidence on a timeline

Before the injuryEstablish developmental communication, adaptive functioning, education, and the person’s reliable response modes.
After the injuryReview authorized neurological and rehabilitation records; distinguish acquired changes from longstanding differences.
Current decisionSpecify the real question: support needs, treatment access, cognition, or another decision requiring separate criteria.

What might explain it? Keep competing and co-occurring explanations

Expressive-language limitation

Compare opportunities to understand with the demand to produce spoken language.

Next evidence / limitation: Collaborate with speech-language pathology; use established communication supports and check response reliability.

Receptive-language limitation

A person may have difficulty understanding as well as expressing language.

Next evidence / limitation: Simplify and verify understanding without coaching a preferred answer. Do not assume writing or pictures automatically solve access.

Cognitive or executive limitations

Consider attention, memory, learning, initiation, and fatigue with appropriate methods.

Next evidence / limitation: A task called nonverbal may still depend on comprehension, vision, motor output, or familiarity. Check those demands.

Threat, fatigue, and context

New people, time pressure, sensory load, and fear of being judged may alter performance.

Next evidence / limitation: Document access conditions, breaks, support, and limits on interpreting altered administration.

What should I assess?

State the decision, list what remains uncertain, and choose evidence that can distinguish the explanations above. Integrate history, authorized collateral, direct observation, functional information, and qualified testing as appropriate. Consider cultural and language context and access before interpreting results.

What can I do now?

Use the person’s established response mode, a low-demand opportunity to confirm or correct meaning, processing time, and a visible option to pause or decline. Familiar partners can inform history without supplying the answer.

Which tool helps?

Communication access boards · Competing-hypothesis lab · Learning and access plan

How do I evaluate progress?

Track accurate self-expression, opportunities to correct misunderstandings, decision participation, fatigue, and everyday support needs. More speech does not by itself establish improved cognition or adequate consent.

Advanced supervision and uncertainty

Which findings can be interpreted using norms, and which are qualitative observations? What access modification changes the construct? How can we preserve autonomy without leading the response?

New or sudden speech change, focal neurological signs, or acute confusion requires urgent medical assessment. This case assumes the acquired condition has already been medically assessed.

Worksheet failure: skill gap, access barrier, or both?

A fictional fifth-grade learner produces little on multi-step math worksheets, gives some accurate answers orally, and struggles when asked to explain procedures. Adults repeatedly redo the work.

What am I seeing? Put the evidence on a timeline

Instruction historyCheck opportunity to learn, attendance, curriculum, explicit teaching, practice, language, and prior support.
Task demandsCompare a clean single-step item, a modeled example, written output, and the actual crowded worksheet.
Review learningLook at independent work samples, error patterns, and progress under documented instruction and accessible conditions.

What might explain it? Keep competing and co-occurring explanations

Math concept or procedure not yet learned

Analyze errors in quantity, relationships, strategy, and procedure rather than only a total score.

Next evidence / limitation: Provide explicit teaching and guided practice matched to the missing skill; seek qualified educational assessment when indicated.

Working-memory / attention load

Multi-step oral directions and crowded pages may obscure a known first step.

Next evidence / limitation: Externalize steps and reduce competing visual material while preserving the learning goal.

Language or output mismatch

Word problems and explanations may require skills different from the underlying calculation.

Next evidence / limitation: Compare comprehension, reasoning, and output modes carefully; collaborate with qualified language/learning professionals.

Fear, fatigue, and feedback history

Repeated correction can obscure the child’s own knowledge and increase shutdown.

Next evidence / limitation: Record the child’s original response before help, ask what feels confusing, and use error-specific feedback without humiliation.

What should I assess?

State the decision, list what remains uncertain, and choose evidence that can distinguish the explanations above. Integrate history, authorized collateral, direct observation, functional information, and qualified testing as appropriate. Consider cultural and language context and access before interpreting results.

What can I do now?

Teach the identified skill and provide access at the same time: one worked example, a visible first step, space to show reasoning, and a response mode suited to the task. Adults record the level of help rather than replacing the child’s answer.

Which tool helps?

Communication access boards · Competing-hypothesis lab · Learning and access plan

How do I evaluate progress?

Separate supported task completion from independent skill growth. Track accuracy, prompt level, generalization to a new example, distress, and whether the learner can explain or show the relevant concept.

Advanced supervision and uncertainty

Which task demands are part of the target skill, and which are incidental? What would an accommodation hide if used on a particular assessment? What needs school evaluation or targeted teaching?

A brief support comparison cannot establish a learning-disorder diagnosis, school eligibility, or the best educational placement. Access support and evaluation can proceed together.

Source scope and evidence

NICE CG128 provides the child autism assessment context; direct retrieval was blocked in this session, so this casebook does not reproduce or claim to audit its recommendations. NICE NG87 states that ADHD diagnosis is not based solely on rating-scale or observation data; this statement was verified in official search results.

ASHA Spoken Language Disorders describes pediatric language assessment and co-occurring difficulties; ASHA Aphasia covers acquired adult language disorders. APA assessment guidelines anchor integration and interpretation. Cases, visual sequences, and worksheets are original CPL synthesis, not evidence-graded diagnostic algorithms.
