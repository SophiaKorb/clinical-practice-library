My Child Has a Diagnosis. What Does That Mean at School?

Understanding medical diagnoses, IEPs, Section 504 plans, and the evaluation process in Arizona

Two systems, two questions

The medical question: Does my child meet criteria for a condition such as ADHD, autism, anxiety, depression, or another disorder?

The school question: How does the condition affect my child at school, and what does my child need to access and benefit from education?

What is an IEP?

An Individualized Education Program (IEP) is provided under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). In general, the school must determine that your child has a disability in an IDEA category and needs special education because of that disability.

  • Specially designed instruction and individualized goals
  • Accommodations and supplementary aids
  • Related services when needed for the student to benefit from special education
  • Behavioral or functional supports and progress monitoring, when appropriate

ADHD and OHI: ADHD is specifically listed in federal and Arizona guidance as a condition that may fit the IDEA category Other Health Impairment (OHI). A diagnosis of ADHD does not automatically qualify a child for an IEP; the school must still determine educational impact and need for special education.

What is a Section 504 plan?

Section 504 is a federal civil rights law. A student may be protected when a physical or mental impairment substantially limits a major life activity. If the student needs school-based aids, services, or changes in order to receive an appropriate education, the school determines what those supports should be.

  • Changes in testing or classroom procedures
  • Organizational or executive-function supports
  • Health, behavioral, or emotional supports
  • Related aids or services when needed

What information helps the school?

A useful clinical report explains not only the diagnosis, but also what the condition makes harder for your child to do.

  • Following multistep directions or remembering what was just taught
  • Starting work, organizing materials, or completing assignments independently
  • Attending, concentrating, or working efficiently
  • Managing transitions, frustration, sensory demands, or emotional distress
  • Attending school consistently or participating in presentations, tests, or group work
  • Communicating, navigating peer interactions, or managing daily school routines

If you want the school to evaluate your child

You can ask the school to evaluate your child. It is usually helpful to make the request in writing, describe the specific school difficulties you are seeing, and provide relevant medical or psychological reports. You do not have to decide first whether an IEP or Section 504 plan is the correct outcome. The evaluation process is meant to answer that question.

Arizona IDEA timeline, in plain language

  • After the school receives a parent’s written request for an IDEA evaluation, Arizona rules require the school, within a reasonable time not to exceed 15 school days, either to begin the evaluation process by reviewing existing data or to provide written notice refusing the evaluation.
  • If the evaluation moves forward, the 60-calendar-day evaluation period begins when the school receives the parent’s informed written consent.
  • The 60-day period generally ends with the multidisciplinary team’s eligibility determination. Arizona permits a written extension of up to 30 additional days when the parent and school agree that the extension is in the child’s best interest.
  • Section 504 does not use the same fixed federal 60-day evaluation deadline. Schools must evaluate when they know or have reason to believe a student needs disability-related aids or services, and an unreasonable delay can violate Section 504.

What if I disagree with the school’s IDEA evaluation?

If the school completes an IDEA evaluation and you disagree with it, you may request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at public expense. Under IDEA, the school must, without unnecessary delay, either provide the IEE at public expense or file for a due process hearing to show that its own evaluation was appropriate. The district may ask why you disagree, but it cannot require you to explain before acting on the request.

An IEE is a right connected to disagreement with the school’s IDEA evaluation. It is different from simply obtaining another medical or psychological opinion on your own.

Sources: Arizona Department of Education, Exceptional Student Services; U.S. Department of Education IDEA regulations; U.S. Department of Education Office for Civil Rights Section 504 guidance.

A diagnosis can help a school understand your child, but it does not automatically create an IEP or a 504 plan. The school still evaluates how the condition affects your child and what educational support is needed.

Good grades do not end the conversation. A child can have good grades and still have a disability. Grades do not show how much time, effort, tutoring, parent help, medication, informal accommodation, or recovery time it takes to achieve them.

A useful way to remember it:

Medical diagnosis: What condition does my child have?

School evaluation: How does it affect my child at school, and what support is needed?
