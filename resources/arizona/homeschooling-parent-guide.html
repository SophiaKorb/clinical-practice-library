Homeschooling in Arizona – Parent Guide

A practical start-to-finish guide for families. General educational information, not legal advice. Reviewed September 2026.

First: choose the right Arizona pathway

Arizona uses several different education pathways that can happen at home.

Traditional homeschool means a nonpublic school conducted primarily by the parent, guardian, or other person with custody, or nonpublic instruction provided in the child's home.

Arizona Online Instruction or a virtual charter school is public school at home. The child remains a public-school student, and public-school rules apply.

An Empowerment Scholarship Account, or ESA, can fund education at home, but Arizona law treats ESA education as a separate legal pathway. A parent who signs an ESA contract must not file a homeschool affidavit.

Do not use the homeschool affidavit simply because learning happens at home. Choose the legal pathway first.

Arizona homeschool checklist

1. File the Affidavit of Intent

For traditional homeschool, file an Affidavit of Intent with the school superintendent for the county where the child lives.

The affidavit is due within 30 days after homeschool instruction begins. It includes the child's name and date of birth, the homeschool address, and identifying/contact information for the people who have custody.

You do not file a new affidavit every year. If homeschooling stops, notify the county superintendent within 30 days. If homeschooling later starts again, file a new affidavit within 30 days.

2. Provide proof of identity and age

Within 30 days after home instruction begins, provide the county school superintendent a certified birth certificate or another form of reliable proof of identity and age allowed by Arizona law.

3. Know the compulsory-school ages

Arizona compulsory-school rules generally apply from age 6 through age 16.

If a child has not reached age 8 by September 1 and the parent does not want formal homeschool instruction to begin yet, Arizona law allows the parent to file the affidavit and state that instruction will be delayed until age 8.

4. Teach the required subjects

Arizona requires homeschool instruction in at least:

Reading

Grammar

Mathematics

Social studies

Science

The law does not require a particular commercial curriculum.

Testing

Arizona does not require homeschooled children to take the state testing program while they are receiving homeschool instruction.

If a homeschooled child later enrolls in public school, Arizona law allows testing to help determine appropriate grade placement.

Even when testing is not legally required, families may choose periodic academic assessment to answer practical questions such as whether reading, math, or writing skills are progressing.

Records worth keeping

Arizona law is relatively light on homeschool recordkeeping, but good records make high school, college, disability services, and reentry into school much easier.

Keep:

A copy of the Affidavit of Intent and county confirmation

Proof-of-birth documentation submitted

A yearly list of courses or subjects

A reading list or major curriculum list

Work samples

Grades or mastery notes

Any outside-course transcripts

Standardized or diagnostic test results, if used

IEP, MET, 504, therapy, or evaluation records when relevant

A high-school transcript once the student reaches high school

High school, transcripts, and diplomas

The Pima County School Superintendent does not issue diplomas to homeschool students. The homeschool is responsible for its own records.

Arizona law requires state and local government entities to recognize and treat diplomas and transcripts associated with the schooling options in A.R.S. §15-802 in the same manner. A public school may still evaluate homeschool/private-school transfer credits when a student re-enrolls.

For college-bound students, plan backward from the admission requirements of likely colleges rather than assuming the minimum Arizona homeschool subjects will be enough.

Arizona State University, for example, accepts a homeschool transcript signed by the parent or guardian who oversaw the education. ASU also offers an Affidavit of Completion of Secondary School Education when a homeschool transcript is unavailable.

Dual enrollment

Homeschooled Arizona students may participate in dual-enrollment opportunities and earn college credit. Check the community college or university for placement, age, prerequisite, and tuition rules.

For high-school planning, keep college transcripts separate from the homeschool transcript and list the college course clearly on the homeschool record.

Sports and interscholastic activities

Arizona law allows a homeschooled student who lives in a public school's attendance area to try out for interscholastic activities for that school in the same manner as enrolled students, subject to the same general eligibility, fee, insurance, conduct, and performance requirements.

The person providing the primary homeschool instruction must verify passing grades and satisfactory academic progress.

A student who leaves a public, private, or charter school for homeschool during a school year may be ineligible for interscholastic participation for the rest of that school year under the current statute.

Special education and disability support

Homeschooling does not make disability concerns disappear.

Arizona Child Find applies to homeschool students. If you suspect a disability, contact the public school district responsible for Child Find or AZ FIND.

A parentally homeschooled student does not have an individual right to receive the full IEP/FAPE package the student would receive while enrolled in public school. However, homeschool students are treated as parentally placed private-school students for IDEA equitable-services purposes and may receive some services through proportionate-share arrangements.

Ask the district:

Who handles Child Find for my address?

Can my child be evaluated?

What equitable special-education services are currently available to homeschool students?

Would the services be provided through a services plan rather than an IEP?

What would change if my child enrolled in public school?

Do not assume an existing IEP remains fully enforceable after voluntary withdrawal to homeschool.

Homeschooling and therapy or neurodevelopmental needs

Homeschooling can reduce some school-based stressors, but it can also hide skills a child still needs.

Continue to work on communication, flexibility, executive functioning, self-advocacy, independence, frustration tolerance, daily routines, and safe participation in community settings when these are areas of need.

Build regular contact with peers and other adults in settings that fit the child. This might include clubs, sports, libraries, religious communities, community classes, volunteering, co-ops, arts, recreation, or dual enrollment.

The goal is not to recreate a school building. The goal is to make sure the child's world does not become unnecessarily small.

A simple weekly planning model

Choose a few nonnegotiable academic priorities.

Use shorter blocks when attention or stamina is limited.

Separate direct teaching from independent work.

Build in movement and recovery time.

Use real-life tasks for math, writing, planning, and independence.

Keep one visible weekly plan rather than many disconnected systems.

Review what worked at the end of the week and adjust.

When to get extra help

Consider outside help when:

Reading, writing, or math progress is persistently stalled

The parent-child relationship is becoming dominated by school conflict

The child is increasingly isolated

Anxiety or avoidance is growing rather than shrinking

The family cannot tell whether the curriculum matches the child's level

A disability evaluation may be needed

High-school planning has become unclear

The child wants to return to public or private school
