IEP & 504 – Parent Guide

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

HOW TO GET YOUR CHILD

an IEP or 504 Plan

A step-by-step guide for recovery coaches to use with parents

Use this guide to:

decide whether to ask about an IEP, a 504 plan, or both;

write and send a clear request for an evaluation;

gather useful records and examples;

prepare for school meetings; and

take the next step if the school says no or the plan is not working.

This guide gives general information. It is not legal advice. School rules and timelines can vary by state. Arizona notes are marked when they apply.

First: What is the difference?

Both plans help students with disabilities. They do different jobs.

A quick way to think about it

Ask about an IEP when the child may need special teaching, therapy at school, behavior instruction, or measurable goals.

Ask about a 504 plan when the child can learn the grade-level work but needs barriers removed to take part and show what they know.

Ask for an evaluation for both if you are not sure. The school team should use data, not guesswork, to decide.

What a recovery coach can do

Help the parent turn worries into clear examples.

Help write the request, but keep the parent's voice.

Help organize records and questions.

Practice what the parent wants to say.

Attend the meeting if the parent and school allow it.

Take notes and help the parent follow up in writing.

Important words: a plain-language legend

School teams use many short forms and legal terms. Parents do not need to memorize them. Use this legend during calls and meetings.

Step 1: Write down the school problems

Start with what people can see. Avoid words such as “lazy,” “bad,” or “doesn't care.” Those words blame the child and do not tell the team what help is needed.

Use this sentence

Look at all parts of school

Learning: reading, writing, math, memory, starting work, finishing work

Communication: understanding directions, speaking, asking for help, social language

Behavior and feelings: anxiety, shutdowns, anger, elopement, unsafe acts, coping

Daily skills: eating, toileting, dressing, moving around school, using materials

Access: attendance, fatigue, pain, medication effects, hearing, vision, mobility

Participation: group work, lunch, recess, field trips, tests, changes in routine

Collect examples

Useful records may include:

report cards, test scores, work samples, and missing-work reports;

emails or messages from school;

attendance, nurse visits, behavior reports, or discipline records;

medical, therapy, or evaluation reports; and

a short parent log with dates, what happened, and how much help the child needed.

Coach questions for the parent

What is hardest for your child at school?

What can your child do only with much more help than other children the same age?

When does the problem happen most often?

What has the school tried? Did it work?

What do you want your child to be able to do more safely or independently?

Step 2: Ask for an evaluation in writing

A parent can ask for an evaluation. The request should be written, dated, and sent to the principal, school psychologist, special education director, or 504 coordinator. Email is useful because it makes a record.

After sending the request

Save a copy of the request and proof that the school received it.

If the request was first made out loud, send a follow-up email that repeats it.

Write down the date it was sent and the date the school replied.

Read the consent form. Ask questions before signing if anything is unclear.

Keep copies of every form and report.

The school should evaluate all suspected needs

The evaluation should not look at only one score or one problem. It may include academic skills, thinking and memory, speech and language, behavior, social and emotional needs, motor skills, health, and daily living skills. The tests should fit the child's language, culture, and needs.

Step 3: Prepare for the evaluation meeting

The team will review information and decide whether the child qualifies. The parent is part of the team.

Before the meeting

Ask for copies of reports early enough to read them before the meeting.

Write the child's strengths. A plan should build on what works.

Choose the top three concerns. Long lists can hide the biggest needs.

Write questions and the support the parent wants the team to consider.

Invite a support person if helpful. Tell the school who will attend.

Questions to ask about the data

What does this score mean in everyday school life?

How does my child compare with same-age students?

What are my child's strengths?

Did the team test every area we raised?

Could language, culture, attendance, teaching, trauma, or health affect these results?

What help does the data show my child needs?

If the child qualifies for an IEP

The IEP should describe the child's current needs, yearly goals, services, supports, how progress will be measured, and where the child will learn. In general, the IEP meeting must be held within 30 days after the child is found eligible.

If the child qualifies for a 504 plan

The 504 team should identify the barriers and write clear supports. The plan should say what staff will do, when they will do it, and who is responsible. “As needed” is often too vague unless the plan explains who decides and how the child gets the support.

Step 4: Build a plan people can follow

A good plan is clear enough that a new teacher can read it and know what to do. The team should connect each support to a real barrier.

For an IEP, check these parts

Present levels: Does the IEP clearly explain what the child can do now and what is hard?

Goals: Can the goal be measured? Does it address an important skill?

Services: Does the plan name the type, amount, frequency, and location of each service?

Accommodations: Are they specific and tied to the child's barriers?

Progress reports: How often will the parent get data about each goal?

Behavior: If behavior blocks learning, did the team consider positive behavior supports?

For either plan

Ask who will tell each staff member about the plan.

Ask how substitute teachers and testing staff will know what to do.

Ask how the school will track whether supports are being used.

Ask when the team will meet again if the plan is not helping.

Step 5: Check whether the plan is working

A plan is useful only when it is followed and helps the child. Check the plan soon after it starts, not only at the yearly meeting.

Make a simple follow-up plan

Choose two or three things to track, such as missing work, reading growth, class exits, panic episodes, attendance, or use of a support.

Ask the child what helps and what feels hard.

Check school data and teacher reports.

Write down when a listed support was not provided.

Ask for a team meeting if the child is not making progress or the plan is not being followed.

If the school says no

Stay calm, ask for the reason, and get it in writing. A spoken “no” is hard to review later.

Ask: What data did the team use?

Ask in writing for Prior Written Notice that explains what the school refused and why.

Ask for a copy of the parent rights or procedural safeguards.

Correct wrong facts in writing and send new information.

Ask for another meeting, mediation, a state complaint, a due process hearing, or an Office for Civil Rights complaint when appropriate.

Contact a parent training center, disability rights group, or special education advocate for help.

Common school objections and calm responses

School staff may raise real questions or concerns. Parents do not need to argue. Ask for the facts, repeat the request, and follow up in writing.

School may say: “We need to try MTSS or RTI first.”

Parent can say: I am glad the school is giving support. I am still requesting a disability evaluation in writing. Please do not delay the evaluation while MTSS or RTI is happening. If the school refuses, please give me Prior Written Notice.

School may say: “Your child is passing and has good grades.”

Parent can say: Grades are only one kind of information. My concerns also include [behavior, focus, communication, attendance, daily skills, safety, or the amount of help needed]. Please consider all areas of school functioning.

School may say: “Your child is not far enough behind.”

Parent can say: Please explain what data and rule the team is using. A child does not always need to be failing to be evaluated. I am requesting a full evaluation based on the concerns I shared.

School may say: “We do not see this problem at school.”

Parent can say: Let us compare information. Please review attendance, work completion, nurse visits, behavior, effort, breaks, and the support adults already give. I will also provide examples from home that may help explain the child's needs.

School may say: “You need a medical diagnosis first.”

Parent can say: Please show me the rule that requires a medical diagnosis for this evaluation. The school has its own duty to evaluate suspected disability-related needs. I can share medical information if it is relevant and available.

School may say: “A diagnosis means your child should get a 504, not an IEP.”

Parent can say: A diagnosis alone does not choose the plan. Please evaluate whether my child needs specially designed instruction, related services, accommodations, or other supports. The team should decide from the child's needs and data.

School may say: “Let us just give informal accommodations.”

Parent can say: I appreciate the support. I am still asking for a formal evaluation and written decision. Informal help may change when staff or classes change, and it does not replace the evaluation process.

School may say: “The teacher can handle this without a plan.”

Parent can say: The teacher's help is valuable. I would still like the team to evaluate whether my child has a disability and needs a written plan that can be followed across teachers, classes, and school years.

School may say: “Your child is too young. Let us wait and see.”

Parent can say: I understand that children develop at different rates. These concerns are affecting my child now. I am requesting an evaluation now so the team can use data rather than waiting for the problems to grow.

School may say: “The problems are caused by attendance, behavior, motivation, or parenting.”

Parent can say: Those factors can be discussed, but they do not answer whether my child has a disability-related need. Please evaluate all suspected areas and consider why the attendance, behavior, or work problems are happening.

School may say: “We do not have enough staff, time, or money for that service.”

Parent can say: I understand the school has limits. Please make the decision based on my child's needs. If the team refuses a requested evaluation, service, or support, please explain the refusal and the data used in Prior Written Notice.

School may say: “We already evaluated and your child did not qualify.”

Parent can say: Please give me the evaluation report and written eligibility decision. I would like the team to explain the data, whether every area of concern was tested, and what options I have if I disagree.

If the parent disagrees with an IEP evaluation

The parent may ask for an Independent Educational Evaluation, called an IEE, at public expense. The school must respond under IDEA rules. Because this can become a formal dispute, the parent may want help from a parent center or advocate before deciding the next step.

Recovery coach session guide

This is a short structure for a 30- to 45-minute parent session.

1. Name the goal (5 minutes)

What does the parent want the school to understand or change?

2. Choose the path (5 minutes)

Does the child seem to need special teaching, access supports, or an evaluation for both?

3. Find three examples (10 minutes)

Write one sentence for each: what happens, when it happens, and how it affects school.

4. Draft the request (10 minutes)

Use the template. Include the child's strengths and the parent's main concerns.

5. Make a written record plan (5 minutes)

Choose one email address and one folder for requests, replies, forms, reports, meeting notes, and dates.

6. Practice one sentence (5 minutes)

Help the parent say the main request in a calm, direct way.

Useful phrases for the coach

Let's turn that worry into an example the team can use.

What does your child need help doing at school?

Which concern matters most for safety, learning, or independence?

What would you like the school to measure?

Would you like to practice how you will say that in the meeting?

Things to avoid

Do not tell the parent that a diagnosis guarantees an IEP or 504 plan.

Do not argue that one exact service is the only lawful choice unless a qualified advocate or attorney has reviewed the case.

Do not write an angry email while the parent is upset. Draft it, pause, check the facts, and then send.

Do not let the meeting become only about blame. Return to the child's barriers, needs, data, and progress.

The most important rule: Make every request in writing. A conversation can help, but it may not create a clear record. Send an email or letter, include the date, and save a copy.

Start here: A parent does not need to know the right diagnosis, test, or school word. The parent only needs to explain what the child is struggling with and ask the school to evaluate.

Plan

What it does

A child may need it when...

IEP

Gives special education. It includes goals, services, supports, and a way to track progress.

The child has a disability and needs specially designed teaching to make progress at school.

504

Removes barriers and gives equal access. It may include changes to the setting, tasks, rules, or schedule.

The child has a physical or mental impairment that greatly limits a major life activity and needs supports to access school.

Important: Good grades do not always mean a child does not qualify. A child may still need support with behavior, focus, communication, attendance, daily skills, or the amount of effort it takes to keep up.

Stay in your lane: The recovery coach can support, organize, and explain. The coach should not diagnose the child, promise eligibility, or choose services for the school team.

Word

What it means

Accommodation

A change in how a child learns or shows learning. It does not change what the child is expected to learn.

Consent

A parent's informed written permission. Consent to evaluate is not the same as consent to start special education.

Eligibility

The team's decision about whether the child meets the rules for an IEP or 504 plan.

Evaluation

The full process used to learn about the child's strengths and needs. It may include tests, records, observations, and parent or teacher input.

FAPE

Free Appropriate Public Education. The school must provide an appropriate education at no cost to the family.

IDEA

The federal special education law. It sets the rules for evaluations, IEPs, services, and parent rights.

IEE

Independent Educational Evaluation. An evaluation done by a qualified person who does not work for the school.

IEP

Individualized Education Program. A written plan with goals, special education, services, and supports.

LRE

Least Restrictive Environment. A child should learn with students without disabilities as much as is appropriate for that child.

MET

Multidisciplinary Evaluation Team. In Arizona, this team reviews evaluation data and decides special education eligibility.

Modification

A change in what a child is expected to learn or do. This is different from an accommodation.

MTSS or RTI

General education systems that give extra support and check progress. They must not be used to delay a needed disability evaluation.

PWN

Prior Written Notice. A school document that explains what the school proposes or refuses, why, and what information it used.

Procedural safeguards

A written explanation of parent and student rights under special education law.

Related services

Services a child may need to benefit from special education, such as speech therapy, occupational therapy, counseling, or transportation.

Section 504

A federal civil rights law that protects people with disabilities from discrimination. A 504 plan lists supports needed for equal access at school.

Specially designed instruction

Teaching changed to fit the child's disability-related learning needs. This is a key part of an IEP.

When a new term comes up: Ask the school to explain it in plain language. Then ask, “What does that mean for my child during a normal school day?”

PARENT STARTER

My child has trouble with __________. This happens when __________. It affects school by __________. We have tried __________. The result was __________.

Do not wait for a perfect folder: A parent can request an evaluation now and send more records later. Paperwork has a natural urge to reproduce. Do not let it become the gatekeeper.

Do not rely on a verbal request: If the parent asks in a meeting, phone call, pickup line, or hallway, send an email that same day. Write: “This email confirms that I requested...” Then state the request again.

Use the word evaluate: Asking for help, a meeting, or testing may not clearly start the formal process. Ask in writing for a full evaluation for special education under IDEA and for Section 504 eligibility.

EMAIL OR LETTER TEMPLATE

Subject: Request for evaluation for [child's full name]

Dear [name],

I am the parent/guardian of [child], who is in [grade] at [school]. I am requesting a full evaluation to see whether my child qualifies for special education under IDEA and/or a Section 504 plan.

I am concerned about [reading, writing, math, attention, behavior, communication, health, attendance, daily skills, or other concern]. For example, [give 2 or 3 short examples]. These problems affect my child's ability to learn, take part in school, or show what they know.

Please evaluate all areas related to these concerns. Please send me the next steps, the consent form, and a copy of my parent rights. If the school refuses any part of this request, please give me written notice that explains the decision and the information used.

Thank you,

[Parent name]

[Phone and email]

[Date]

Arizona timeline: Arizona generally requires the evaluation and eligibility decision to be completed within 60 calendar days. The start date can depend on whether the parent or the school started the evaluation. Keep the written request, consent form, and dates.

IF THE MEETING MOVES TOO FAST

I want to take part in this decision. Please slow down and explain that in plain language. I need time to read this page before we move on.

IF A NEEDED AREA WAS NOT TESTED

I am still concerned about [area]. What information did the team use to decide that no testing was needed there? I am asking the team to evaluate that area or explain the refusal in writing.

Barrier

Weak wording

Clearer wording

Loses track of long directions

Repeat directions as needed

Give one or two steps at a time. Ask the student to say the steps back.

Freezes during timed tests

Extra time

Give time-and-a-half on classroom and district tests in a quiet setting.

Leaves class when overwhelmed

Take breaks

Allow a break card and access to the named calm space for up to 10 minutes, with an adult check-in.

Do not sign just to end a long meeting: A parent may ask for time to read documents and may ask questions. Signing an attendance page is not always the same as agreeing to the plan, but the parent should ask what each signature means.

FOLLOW-UP EMAIL

I am writing to check on [child's] plan. The plan says [support or service]. I am concerned because [short facts and dates]. Please tell me how the school is providing this support and what data show whether it is helping. I would like a team meeting to review the plan and make needed changes.

Put concerns in writing: If a support is missing or the plan is not working, send a short factual email. Include dates, the part of the plan involved, what happened, and what response you want. Save the email and reply.

MTSS is not a waiting room: A school may use MTSS, RTI, tutoring, or behavior supports, but these steps should not be used to delay an evaluation when the school knows or has reason to believe the child may have a disability.

AFTER A VERBAL CONVERSATION

Thank you for speaking with me today. This email confirms that I requested [evaluation, meeting, service, support, or change]. I heard the school say [short summary]. Please correct me if I misunderstood. Please send the school's written response and any required notice.

A useful reset: The parent does not have to answer every objection in the moment. The parent can say, “I need time to think about that. Please send it to me in writing.”

Final reminder: The goal is not to win a meeting. The goal is to build a plan that helps the child learn, take part, feel safe, and become more independent.
