Medical Diagnosis, Educational Eligibility, and School Support Letters

A practical guide for pediatric psychiatrists and other treating clinicians

When writing to a school, start with function

The most useful letters identify the diagnosis, describe what the student has difficulty doing, and explain how that difficulty may affect access, participation, or progress at school. The examples below are usually more useful than simply naming a requested plan.

Medical diagnosis and educational eligibility answer different questions

IEP eligibility under IDEA

For an IEP, the school must determine that the student meets an IDEA disability category and, because of that disability, needs special education. The educational evaluation addresses both disability and educational need.

  • Qualifying disability. Categories especially relevant to behavioral health include Autism, Emotional Disturbance/Emotional Disability, Other Health Impairment (OHI), Specific Learning Disability, Traumatic Brain Injury, and Intellectual Disability.
  • Other Health Impairment matters clinically. Federal and Arizona guidance specifically include attention deficit disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder as examples of health conditions that may fit OHI when the required educational criteria are met.
  • Educational impact. The team considers more than grades, including access, participation, progress, independence, behavior, communication, adaptive functioning, attendance, and other school-related functioning.
  • Need for special education. A diagnosis alone is not enough for an IEP. The student must need specially designed instruction because of the disability.

Section 504

Section 504 is a civil rights law. In K-12 schools, a student may be protected when a physical or mental impairment substantially limits one or more major life activities, and the school determines what related aids, services, or accommodations are needed for the student to receive an appropriate education.

Describe function, not just symptom labels

Link recommendations to the impairment

  • Working-memory difficulty → written directions, repetition, and checks for understanding.
  • Slow task initiation → explicit start cues, task chunking, and structured check-ins.
  • Anxiety under time pressure → evaluation of timing and testing-environment supports.
  • Difficulty with transitions → advance warning, predictable routines, and visual supports.
  • Executive-function impairment → external planning systems and organizational supports.
  • Emotion-regulation difficulty → a predictable regulation plan and access to appropriate school personnel.

A medical diagnosis can be important evidence in a school evaluation, but it does not automatically establish eligibility for an IEP or a Section 504 plan. The clinician documents the condition and its functional impact; the school team determines educational eligibility and services.

Less useful

“Patient has ADHD and needs an IEP.”

More useful

“Symptoms include significant difficulty with sustained attention, working memory, task initiation, and behavioral inhibition. At school, these difficulties may interfere with following multistep directions, organizing work, beginning independent tasks, and completing assignments without repeated prompting. A school-based evaluation is recommended to determine what educational supports are needed.”

Less useful

“Patient has autism and needs an Autism IEP.”

More useful

“Clinically significant differences are present in social communication, flexibility, sensory regulation, and adaptation to changes in routine. These may affect group participation, transitions, interpretation of social situations, and regulation in high-demand environments. A comprehensive school-based evaluation is recommended.”

Less useful

“Patient has anxiety and needs extended time.”

More useful

“Anxiety is associated with anticipatory worry, physiological arousal, avoidance, and difficulty concentrating under performance demands. At school, symptoms may interfere with attendance, tests, oral presentations, transitions, and initiation of unfamiliar tasks. The school should evaluate whether supports are needed to provide appropriate access.”

Less useful

“Patient has depression and should have reduced work.”

More useful

“Depressive symptoms include reduced energy, slowed task initiation, impaired concentration, and episodic difficulty attending school. These symptoms may affect participation, work completion, attendance, and sustained effort. School-based evaluation is recommended to determine appropriate supports.”

Clinical diagnosis

Educational eligibility

Does the child meet diagnostic criteria for a disorder or medical condition?

Does the student have a disability under the applicable education law, how does it affect school functioning, and what support is needed?

May result in diagnoses such as ADHD, autism, anxiety, depression, PTSD, or a sleep disorder.

IDEA uses federal disability categories. Section 504 uses a broader disability standard based on substantial limitation of a major life activity.

Treatment may include medication, psychotherapy, parent guidance, or other clinical care.

School supports may include accommodations, related aids and services, behavioral supports, or specially designed instruction.

A clinician determines the diagnosis within the clinician’s scope of practice.

Eligibility is determined through the school’s evaluation process by the appropriate team, using information from multiple sources.

Important: A medical diagnosis is not automatically sufficient for Section 504 eligibility, and a specific medical diagnosis is not always required. Good grades also do not rule out disability. Schools should consider the individual student, including the effort, time, outside support, compensatory strategies, or informal accommodations required to perform well.

Instead of only writing…

Describe what the student has difficulty doing

“Poor executive functioning”

Loses track of multistep directions; needs repeated prompts to begin; cannot independently organize assignments or materials.

“Severe anxiety”

Leaves class when physiological anxiety escalates; avoids presentations; misses instruction because of anxiety-related absence.

“Emotionally dysregulated”

Has difficulty returning to academic activity after distress; transitions can trigger prolonged disruption.

“Sensory issues”

Becomes overwhelmed in noisy or crowded settings and may be unable to remain engaged without regulation supports.

“Given the documented functional impact of these symptoms, a comprehensive school-based evaluation under IDEA and/or Section 504 is recommended to determine whether the student requires accommodations, specially designed instruction, related services, behavioral supports, or other educational interventions.”

Avoid statements such as “the student qualifies for an IEP,” “the school must provide this accommodation,” or “ADHD automatically qualifies under Other Health Impairment.” These statements go beyond the treating clinician’s role in the school eligibility process. A stronger approach is to document diagnosis, functional impairment, likely school impact, and the reason a school-based evaluation is warranted.
