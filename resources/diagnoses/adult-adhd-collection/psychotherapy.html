Clinician guide · Treatment selection

Adult ADHD: evidence-informed psychotherapy

Structured ADHD-focused CBT and supportive psychological work can be part of adult treatment; select techniques by clinical formulation, preferences and co-occurring needs.

Evidence calibration: NICE recommends structured supportive ADHD-focused psychological intervention when non-pharmacological care is indicated, potentially including CBT elements; the AADPA guideline reviews cognitive-behavioural approaches. The session sequence here is an educational adaptation, not a validated manual or mandatory dose.

Eight-session flexible treatment scaffold

This sequence translates guideline-supported intervention components into teaching examples; its exact session count and order have not been independently evaluated. The AADPA adult intervention evidence review finds benefits against usual care or waitlist but less clear superiority against active supportive treatment. Choose a trained clinician and adapt to the person.

1 · Engagement and goalsWhat has been costly? What helps? Choose one meaningful, observable target and baseline.
2 · Functional chainMap actual tasks, emotional predictions, sensory load, skill demands and environmental barriers.
3 · Memory and externalizationTrial one capture/recall system. Practice retrieving information, not merely writing it down.
4 · Priorities and timeEstimate and test time, reverse-plan transitions, clarify success and stopping points.
5 · Avoidance and beliefsExamine shame, perfectionism and all-or-nothing thinking; create a graded behavioral experiment.
6 · Regulation and impulsesIdentify activation cues, context, pause options and repair plans; address coexisting risk.
7 · Relationships and accessibilityPractice explicit requests and consent-based shared systems at home/work.
8 · Generalization and maintenanceEvaluate benefit versus burden; rehearse setbacks and return-to-system rather than demanding perfect adherence.

In-session treatment mechanics

  1. Start with a concrete review: one desired outcome, one obstacle and the person's appraisal of last week's experiment.
  2. Use real material (a blank simulated form, appointment process, calendar pattern) rather than only talking about intentions.
  3. Offer alternatives: writing, verbal rehearsal, visual sequence or supported choice. Avoid assuming everyone wants an app.
  4. Identify the first step, trigger and environment where skill will be used, then rehearse it.
  5. Scale practice to capacity; note when a task fails because the situation is inaccessible, not the person is “noncompliant.”
  6. Evaluate unwanted effects, including more work for a partner, intrusive alerts and fatigue.

Coexisting presentations and sequencing

Depression/anxiety: integrate targeted care, assess risk and do not confuse a mood or fear-based avoidance cycle with all ADHD-related impairment. Trauma: establish control and choice; do not use coercive accountability systems. Autism/sensory needs: adapt communication, pacing and physical environment. Substance-related needs: coordinate assessment and support with appropriate professionals. Medication: prescribing and titration remain with the qualified prescriber; therapy can track functional goals and barriers with consent.

Do not present DBT-informed regulation, coaching, mindfulness, neurofeedback or generic productivity advice as equivalent in evidence to ADHD-focused CBT or indicated medication.

Clinical sources and evidence limits

NICE NG87: ADHD diagnosis and management (UK, applicable guideline version and updates); AADPA: Cognitive-behavioural interventions; AADPA: Multimodal treatment and support; NIMH: ADHD in adults; JAN: Workplace accommodations.
