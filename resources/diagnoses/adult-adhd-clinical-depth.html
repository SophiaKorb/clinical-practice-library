Adult ADHD: from assessment to usable treatment

Adult ADHD needs more than task-starting tips. This companion covers differential diagnosis, an actionable psychotherapy sequence, everyday functioning, medication collaboration, accommodations, co-occurring presentations and meaningful outcome review. It is for professional education and collaborative planning—not a standalone diagnostic instrument or prescriber protocol.

Clinical scope: Diagnose within qualifications, use current diagnostic criteria and local guidance, individualize treatment, and assess urgent safety, mood, substance and medical concerns independently. The worksheet examples below are original educational aids, not validated measures.

Clinical formulation: what to assess

Use a developmental, contextual and functional interview, not a checklist alone. Explore childhood onset, at least two settings, current impairment, compensatory supports, preferred language, strengths and what prompted assessment now. Under DSM-5-TR, adults aged 17 or older generally require at least five symptoms in a domain, with several present before age 12, and symptoms not better explained by another disorder. These are diagnostic criteria, not a rule that every person must produce school records or a parent informant.

  • History: attention, activation, impulsivity, organization, motor/internal restlessness, emotional responses, educational and occupational trajectory.
  • Impairment: missed obligations, financial administration, safety, driving, health tasks, relationships and exhaustion from compensation.
  • Context: chronic stress, caregiving, shift work, access needs, sensory environment, sleep, trauma and socioeconomic barriers.
  • Corroboration: request consent for collateral where useful; evaluate inconsistency, masking and limitations without treating absence of an informant as disproof.
  • Scales: ASRS can support screening and monitoring, not establish diagnosis; structured interviews such as DIVA-5 require appropriate access and training. Neuropsychological testing is not routinely necessary to diagnose ADHD.

Psychotherapy: a reproducible course

ADHD-adapted CBT has a better evidence base than generic supportive counseling for improving adult ADHD-related functioning. It should be individualized, not reduced to a planner tutorial. An example 8–12-session outline below is illustrative, not a validated fixed-dose protocol.

  1. Engage and formulate: identify personally meaningful impairments, strengths, coexisting conditions and an agreed priority; establish a baseline.
  2. Map the functional chain: trigger → task demands → thoughts/feelings → access barriers → actions → consequences. Identify which link is modifiable.
  3. Externalize memory: choose one trusted capture point, a review cue and a method for recovering after lapses.
  4. Plan time concretely: distinguish duration estimates, transition time, preparation and the earliest actionable step.
  5. Work with avoidance: evaluate fear of mistakes, rejection, overwhelm, boredom and unrealistic standards; use graded behavior experiments.
  6. Emotion and impulse skills: identify escalation cues, pause options, repair strategies and context-sensitive regulation.
  7. Relationships: discuss mutual expectations, consent-based support and non-shaming communication.
  8. Generalize and prevent relapse: transfer skills into the real environment, remove excessive burden and build a plan for interruptions.

Consider structured ADHD-focused CBT resources and supervised adaptations; DBT-informed skills may help emotion regulation but should not be described as an interchangeable or universally established ADHD treatment. Coaching and occupational supports vary in evidence and licensing.

Medication and integrated care

Stimulants and several nonstimulants are evidence-based options for adults; prescribing decisions must account for patient preference, comorbidities, interactions, cardiovascular history, substance-related risk and follow-up capacity. Clinical prescribing and monitoring are governed by the prescriber and local guidance. Psychological treatment may complement medication or be used when medication is declined, contraindicated, inaccessible or insufficient.

Therapy can support shared goals, monitoring of sleep, appetite, distress and functional effects, and coordination with consent. Do not suggest that a therapist initiate, stop or modify medication. Medication response does not prove ADHD, nor does lack of response rule it out. Elevated mood, drastically reduced sleep need or acute psychiatric symptoms require independent clinical evaluation.

Special populations and contexts

Late-identified adults and women: explore internalized symptoms, masking, intensive compensatory labor and changes in demands without treating stereotypes as diagnostic rules. Older adults: consider cognitive, medical and medication contributions. Pregnancy/perinatal period: treatment choices require individualized prescribing discussions. Autism and intellectual/developmental disability: adapt communication and environmental supports; avoid reducing all impairment to one diagnosis. Substance-use disorders: assess collaboratively and coordinate care rather than automatically excluding ADHD treatment. Trauma and complex stress: formulate both attention and safety-related needs. Cultural and economic context: evaluate work, access, language and inequity rather than judging the person by narrow productivity norms.

Treatment plan and measurement example

Collaborative goal: “I want to attend necessary appointments without exhausting myself or relying on my partner.” Baseline: number attended, reminders needed and distress (self-report). Intervention: assess sleep/transport and executive barriers; trial one appointment capture method and a departure cue; practice during session. Review after 2–4 weeks: attendance, effort, unplanned support burden and whether the person wants to continue or revise.

Track target functioning alongside symptoms and quality of life. Reassess if symptoms worsen, the hypothesis no longer fits, treatment adds disproportionate strain or a new medical/psychiatric concern emerges. Never infer treatment success solely from an improved checklist score.
