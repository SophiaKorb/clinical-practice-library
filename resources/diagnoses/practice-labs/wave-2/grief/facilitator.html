Bereaved people, supporters and clinicians adapting to age and culture

Use this material collaboratively

Grief has no required sequence. A person may need ritual, connection, quiet, help with responsibilities, or specialist support. Remembrance and daily living can coexist without forcing acceptance or a timetable.

Keep professional roles clear. Medical or diagnostic decisions, specialist therapies, safeguarding, consent and eligibility rules are handled through the appropriate qualified pathway. These exercises are optional visual supports.

Seven moves for a session

  1. Ask how the person prefers to refer to the loss and whether they wish to discuss it.
  2. Offer genuine choices: remembering, practical help, quiet presence, connection, or no activity.
  3. Present remembrance and daily living as directions people may revisit freely rather than sequential stages.
  4. Explore cultural and spiritual preferences without assuming the clinician knows their meaning.
  5. Assess functional impairment, PTSD, depression, substance concerns and safety through qualified care when indicated.
  6. Help the person select one meaningful support or defer action; do not prescribe exposure or social reconnection.
  7. Revisit on the person's timeline and ask what support was helpful, intrusive or unnecessary.

Distinctions that change the plan

There is no universal five-stage sequence or deadline for grief.

Religious, cultural, family and individual rituals vary; do not impose a universal narrative.

Prolonged grief disorder requires a qualified assessment of persistent distress and impairment in cultural context.

Practical help, trauma treatment, grief counseling and specialized prolonged grief treatment serve different needs.

Illustrative fictional example

A fictional bereaved partner wishes to keep a weekly remembrance ritual and needs help managing household paperwork. They choose both the ritual and a trusted friend's assistance. The clinician does not prescribe 'moving on' or infer a disorder from the amount of sadness.

The scenario is invented and not a claim of effectiveness. Ask which assumptions the person might challenge.
