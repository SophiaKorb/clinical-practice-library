Make the decision accessible—not smaller

The person’s preference is the center of this map. Change the communication, options and support before interpreting difficulty responding as incapacity.

Safety and role boundaryA diagnosis does not establish incapacity or guardianship. Do not equate absent speech, slower responses, apparent agreement or a supporter's preference with the person's own decision. Applicable consent and legal capacity rules must be determined separately.

Four perspectives, without a compulsory order

01

The real decision

Start with a matter the person actually wants influence over, and ask how they wish to participate.

02

Accessible choices

Offer understandable options, including neither, other, later and help; remove unnecessary jargon.

03

Chosen supporters

Identify who helps explain, who is not trusted, and whether any supporter may be steering the answer.

04

Feedback and rights

Check the person's chosen response across modes and time, respect privacy, and escalate rights concerns.

These perspectives coexist. They are not a stage sequence, diagnosis, risk rating, evidence score or substitute for qualified assessment.

Fictional case: examine assumptions

In an invented transition-planning meeting, someone says yes to every suggestion. Staff want to call this agreement. A trusted communication partner discovers that a two-option picture layout, extra processing time and a chance to say 'neither' yield a different preference. No one should treat the new answer as a diagnostic test or a blanket legal finding.

Questions that change the plan

  1. Ask permission to use a shared-choice map and identify the person's preferred communication method.
  2. Offer a real choice, a no/none option, an alternative, and the choice to stop or decide later.
  3. Test whether language, sensory demands, power imbalance, pain or fatigue obscure the person's preference.
  4. Distinguish the person's expressed preference from a supporter's assumption without making a capacity determination.
  5. Identify the qualified decision-maker and legal authority separately for specific treatment, school or financial actions.
  6. Revisit the person's experience after a support is tried, including whether it reduced coercion.

When a shortcut would mislead

Automatic yes

A yes response may reflect acquiescence, pressure or a communication mismatch; ask again accessibly.

Guardian involved

Legal authority and scope must be checked, but the person's voice remains important.

Behavior changes abruptly

Assess discomfort, pain, abuse, environmental conditions and medical needs before inferring willful opposition.

Two optional blank working pages

Work in whatever communication form is appropriate. The paper is not a medical chart or a validated assessment.

Optional work page 01

Understand the question

Speak, point, draw or write; any item may be skipped. Keep completed copies in an approved private location.

1. My actual decision or goal
2. How I want information explained
3. Two choices and my 'other/none' option
4. Who I choose to help—and who I do not
5. What I need to decide without pressure

Optional work page 02

Agree and revisit support

Speak, point, draw or write; any item may be skipped. Keep completed copies in an approved private location.

1. My response in my own communication form
2. What supporters heard versus what I intended
3. Which legal/clinical question needs qualified advice
4. One step I choose, or a decision to wait
5. When we will ask whether it worked
