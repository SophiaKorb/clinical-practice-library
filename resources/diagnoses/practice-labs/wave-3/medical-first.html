A medically anchored, collaborative care-coordination map

Eating concerns: medical care and support in parallel

Keep the need for prompt qualified medical assessment visible while the person, caregivers and team clarify symptoms, function, preferences and practical barriers. This tool never assigns medical risk.

Safety and role boundaryDo not rule out medical instability on appearance, body size, a single BMI or a brief screen. Restriction, purging, rapid change, dehydration, fainting or other health concerns call for qualified medical assessment. The page cannot provide vital-sign thresholds, determine admission, diagnose ARFID, or direct nutrition/medication plans.

Four perspectives, without a compulsory order

01

Medical assessment first

Qualified professionals assess physical effects and acute risk; do not let a worksheet or normal appearance delay indicated care.

02

Understand the pattern

Distinguish fear of weight gain, binge/purge patterns, sensory or fear-based restriction, food insecurity, illness and other causes.

03

Specialist treatment

Coordinate age-appropriate qualified eating-disorder care, family participation where appropriate, and multidisciplinary follow-up.

04

Access and voice

Ask what makes appointments, food, routines and communication manageable, including privacy and consent.

These perspectives coexist. They are not a stage sequence, diagnosis, risk rating, evidence score or substitute for qualified assessment.

Fictional case: examine assumptions

In a fictional example, a teenager has markedly reduced intake but appears outwardly well. The family suspects sensory selectivity; another person assumes body-image concerns. A clinician arranges appropriate medical assessment without waiting for a visual worksheet, while the team asks about eating mechanisms, age-appropriate specialized care, food access, confidentiality, and the young person's voice.

Questions that change the plan

  1. If physical compromise is plausible, follow local medical/emergency procedures before routine exercise; never use this map to delay assessment.
  2. Invite the person's own account of discomfort, avoidance, restriction or distress without moralizing food or appearance.
  3. Separate suspected eating-disorder mechanisms from medical, developmental, sensory and access explanations for professional evaluation.
  4. Determine with qualified staff what medical monitoring and specialty referral are already arranged, not merely suggested.
  5. Offer practical help with transport, scheduling, communication, food access and authorized coordination while avoiding unauthorized meal prescriptions.
  6. Review medical and psychosocial follow-through, the person's goals, and confidentiality/guardian requirements with the proper team.

When a shortcut would mislead

Looks physically well

Appearance or BMI alone cannot establish safety; qualified assessment is needed.

Food avoidance has sensory features

Sensory involvement does not by itself diagnose ARFID or rule out another eating/medical concern.

Caregivers disagree

Include the young person's voice, consider developmental capacity and apply actual guardian/consent and safety rules.

Two optional blank working pages

Work in whatever communication form is appropriate. The paper is not a medical chart or a validated assessment.

Optional work page 01

Understand the question

Speak, point, draw or write; any item may be skipped. Keep completed copies in an approved private location.

1. Which medical concern was referred to qualified staff
2. Who owns urgent review and ongoing monitoring
3. What eating/feeding experience the person reports
4. What has been assessed and what is still uncertain
5. Which communication or cultural support is wanted

Optional work page 02

Agree and revisit support

Speak, point, draw or write; any item may be skipped. Keep completed copies in an approved private location.

1. Which specialized service will make contact
2. Who may share what with whom
3. Transport, appointment, insurance or food-access barrier
4. A concrete next action and named owner
5. How medical follow-through and personal priorities will be revisited
