Trauma and dissociation: choice and continuity

Offer choice about orientation, safety and continuity without requiring memory retrieval, disclosure or compulsory grounding.

Before useThis is not a dissociative-disorder diagnostic instrument, memory-recovery technique or trauma-processing protocol. Do not use leading questions about identity states or unverifiable memories. Grounding and sensory cues can worsen distress for some people; offer opt-out and alternatives. Assess present danger, self-harm risk, psychosis, substances, neurological issues and medical causes where relevant. Specialist PTSD therapies require indicated training.

PTSD treatment path

Discuss indicated trauma-focused psychotherapy, informed consent, preferences and trained referral. This worksheet is not PE, CPT or EMDR.

Dissociation assessment path

Observe longitudinal function, episodic memory gaps and orientation without suggesting a specific cause or identity explanation.

Ongoing danger path

Prioritize immediate protection, safeguarding and concrete access to care; regulation exercises do not resolve coercion.

Use this collaboratively in a session

  1. Name the person's immediate goal and the right to decline all exercises.
  2. Ask about present safety and practical support needs using relevant safeguarding and clinical protocols.
  3. Invite the person to choose communication mode: speaking, writing, pictorial options, pauses or silence.
  4. Offer neutral orientation choices only if welcomed; do not require eye contact, sensory stimulation or recounting trauma.
  5. Map what is reliably known about the next step, handoff or appointment and what remains uncertain.
  6. If specialized PTSD care is being considered, discuss trained evidence-based options and what consent would entail.
  7. Review with the person's language: tolerable, useful, not useful, more disorienting, or unsure; do not rate compliance.
Illustrative fictional example. Fictional example: During an appointment a client becomes quiet and reports feeling disconnected. The clinician first checks present safety and asks whether to stop, sit silently or use a written agenda. The client chooses a pause and a brief written summary of next steps. No claim about the cause of the episode is made.
