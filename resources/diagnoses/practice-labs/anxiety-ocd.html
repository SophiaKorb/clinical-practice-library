Anxiety and OCD: map the maintaining loop

Separate generalized worry, panic, social fears and obsessive-compulsive rituals before choosing a treatment model.

Before useThis is a formulation and shared-thinking aid, not a self-guided ERP protocol. Exposure/ERP requires condition-specific training, consent, supervision and clinical assessment. New or unexplained physical symptoms warrant appropriate medical evaluation. Do not turn uncertainty practice into dismissal of actual safety concerns.

A visual map to discuss—not a diagnosis

Distinctions that change the next step

Worry

Many anticipated problems; test the role of repetitive future-oriented thinking. Do not assume worry itself is a compulsion.

Panic

Physical alarm and future attack fears. Distinguish medical assessment from panic-focused interventions; interoceptive practice is clinician-led.

OCD

Intrusive obsession and compulsive responses, including mental rituals. ERP is a specific qualified intervention; reassurance can become accommodation.

Social anxiety

Anticipated negative evaluation and safety behaviors; social-skills deficits are not an automatic explanation.

Use this collaboratively in a session

  1. Ask what the person wants back: leaving home, engaging at school, enjoying relationships or returning to valued activities.
  2. Invite a concrete recent example, without demanding an intrusive or graphic account.
  3. Name cue, prediction, response, short-term impact and longer-term cost on separate cards; invite corrections.
  4. Identify practical protection and legitimate medical follow-up before treating a response as an anxiety-maintaining safety behavior.
  5. Compare the cycle with the person's own interpretation; label it a hypothesis, not an explanation imposed by the clinician.
  6. Choose either an access/support action or a referral for condition-specific treatment. Do not initiate exposure solely from this page.
  7. Agree on a review question: Did participation improve? Was the plan tolerable and chosen? Did the context change?
Illustrative fictional example. Fictional example: A student asks the same classroom question repeatedly. The team first checks whether instructions are genuinely unclear or inaccessible. If they are clear, the student describes fear of making a mistake; repeated reassurance lowers distress briefly but delays work. The response can be environmental clarity, a collaborative uncertainty plan with trained support, or further OCD assessment—depending on the actual function.

This is an original educational visual facilitation aid. It is not a standardized measure, a diagnostic algorithm or a substitute for condition-specific treatment training.

Two printable working sheets

Print blank pages and complete them together on paper. Do not enter client information into site search or shareable URLs. No local storage, form submission or analytics are used by these pages.

Working sheet 01 · Name and date optional on paper

My fear, uncertainty and response map

  1. Situation or cue (not a full trauma narrative)

  2. The prediction or worry, in my own words

  3. What I did and what the environment required

  4. What helped immediately—and at what cost later

  5. A support or treatment question I want to raise

  6. What participation would look like if life were easier

Working sheet 02 · Review with the person's own words

One chosen experiment or care decision

  1. Is the main need medical clarification, access, specialist treatment or another support?

  2. Who decides and what permission/assent is needed?

  3. My chosen next action—no forced exposure

  4. What might make this step safe and manageable?

  5. Who can help, within their role?

  6. When will we review benefits, burden and changed circumstances?
