Insomnia treatment: what are we comparing?

The words “combination treatment” can obscure two different decisions. Use these source-linked comparisons to prepare a conversation with a trained treating clinician—not to select a medication or prescribe a sleep schedule.

Clinical safety boundary First establish whether the presenting pattern is chronic insomnia rather than sleep-disordered breathing, circadian mismatch, insufficient opportunity, another medical condition or a period of reduced need for sleep with mood activation. Driving risk, dangerous sleepiness, seizures and other safety concerns need appropriate professional assessment. These adult guidelines do not establish treatment for children or adolescents.

Three statements that should not be conflated

Each statement has its own population, comparator and strength of recommendation.

Evidence 1 · AASM 2021 · STRONG

CBT-I as a treatment

AASM 2021 gives a strong recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I for adults with chronic insomnia. This is more than advice about sleep hygiene.

Evidence 2 · AASM 2026 · CONDITIONAL

Combination versus medication alone

AASM 2026 conditionally favors starting CBT-I together with insomnia medication over medication alone, on low-certainty evidence.

Evidence 3 · AASM 2026 · CONDITIONAL

Combination versus CBT-I alone

AASM 2026 conditionally suggests against routine combination treatment compared with CBT-I alone, also on low-certainty evidence. An individual may reasonably value earlier sleep-duration gains differently.

The 2026 combination comparisons are conditional and based on low-certainty evidence. They do not mean that every adult should receive both treatments. Decisions belong to patients and appropriately qualified clinicians considering individual needs, harms, resources and preferences.

Which condition needs to be assessed first?

Confirm that qualified staff have considered apnea, irregular timing, medication/substance effects, mood activation, safety-critical sleepiness and other medical questions. This worksheet cannot make that determination.

Which comparison are we actually discussing?

Medication alone versus adding CBT-I is a different question from CBT-I alone versus adding medication. The same guideline does not make both answers equivalent.

Whose goals and constraints matter?

Ask about daytime function, access to trained CBT-I care, cost, disability accommodations and the person’s preferences without turning them into a medication instruction.

Who is responsible for clinical choices?

Medication and sleep scheduling decisions belong to appropriately qualified clinicians. A case manager can help arrange access and follow-through within consent and role boundaries.

Fictional example: medication alone is not the only option

An adult has persistent insomnia and has previously been offered medication. They want to function better during the day but have limited transportation and no obvious access to trained CBT-I. They ask whether starting CBT-I while taking medication would be better. The team first checks for a qualified diagnosis, medical and psychiatric concerns, sleep-related safety and the person’s treatment preferences. A case manager investigates actual CBT-I access and authorized scheduling. The treating clinician explains the two different AASM comparisons without assuming the patient should start, stop or combine medication.

Do not use a shortcut here

Do not infer a diagnosis

Fatigue, short sleep, apnea symptoms and reduced sleep need require different clinical evaluations.

Do not prescribe from a visual

The aid gives no sleep restriction, medication name, dose, duration or stop/start instruction.

Do not generalize across age

The 2021 and 2026 AASM guidelines discussed here are for adults with chronic insomnia, not pediatric treatment pathways.

Name the clinical question

For adults with possible chronic insomnia. This page neither diagnoses nor chooses medication or sleep restriction.

3. Which comparison does the person want the treating clinician to explain?
4. Does the person have access to trained CBT-I, and what barriers exist?
5. What preference or question should not be lost in the handoff?

Coordinate without prescribing

Source and interpretation

  • AASM 2026: combination treatment of chronic insomnia in adults — two distinct, conditional comparisons; low-certainty evidence and shared decision-making.
  • AASM 2021: behavioral and psychological treatments — strong multicomponent CBT-I recommendation in adults.
  • VA/DoD 2025 insomnia and sleep apnea guideline — distinct medical diagnosis and behavioral treatment recommendations.
