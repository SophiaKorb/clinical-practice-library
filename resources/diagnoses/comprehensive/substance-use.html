Substance use: safety, motivation, mental health and recovery choices

Substance-related needs span risky use, dependence, withdrawal, overdose risk and co-occurring psychiatric conditions. Clinical care should be low-barrier, non-punitive, practical and aligned with informed goals.

Safety and scopeAlcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal can be medically dangerous, opioid overdose requires emergency response, and medication for OUD/AUD is a prescriber service. 42 CFR Part 2 may protect specific substance-use treatment records; confidentiality and consent must be managed according to current law.

Rapid clinical distinctions: do not treat these as interchangeable

Use these prompts to identify the next assessment or service decision. They are not diagnostic algorithms and cannot replace qualified judgment.

Withdrawal safety

Dependence on alcohol or sedatives may carry dangerous withdrawal risk.

Next decision: Use qualified medical assessment rather than unmonitored taper advice.

Opioid overdose risk

Reduced consciousness or respiratory compromise can be life-threatening.

Next decision: Follow emergency response and overdose-treatment pathways.

Co-occurring mental health

Depression, PTSD, psychosis and substance use may reinforce one another.

Next decision: Offer integrated care rather than serial treatment barriers.

Motivation and choice

A person may seek reduction, abstinence, medication or safer use.

Next decision: Employ motivational interviewing and shared decisions.

Record confidentiality

Substance-use treatment records may be protected by Part 2.

Next decision: Use authorized channels, consent and current confidentiality requirements.

Assessment: questions that determine the pathway

  • Ask about substances, amount, pattern, route, last use, withdrawal history, overdose history, safety, and what the person wants to change.
  • Evaluate immediate medical concerns, intoxication, withdrawal and risk before turning to motivational worksheets.
  • Screen co-occurring mood, trauma, psychosis, pain, social instability and medication interactions without demanding single-diagnosis explanations.
  • Explore prior treatment experiences, strengths, social context, stigma, trauma, housing, employment and barriers to access.
  • Clarify person-chosen goals including abstinence, reduction, safer use, medication treatment or health stabilization; document informed consent and confidentiality.

Differential and co-occurring needs

  • Substance intoxication or withdrawal may resemble anxiety, mania, psychosis or attention problems.
  • Psychiatric conditions can both precede and follow substance use; assess independent time course and intertwined harms.
  • A person declining a particular treatment approach is not evidence they lack capacity or motivation to improve safety.
  • Physical pain and prescribed medications complicate treatment; do not abruptly change regimens outside medical scope.

Treatment and service selection

  • Provide motivational interviewing and collaborative planning rather than threats or confrontational techniques.
  • Consider evidence-based medications for alcohol or opioid use disorders via qualified prescribers; accessible treatment entry is often time-sensitive.
  • Use integrated approaches for co-occurring mental health and substance use disorders rather than fragmented referrals only.
  • For selected stimulant-use disorders, contingency management and other evidence-informed approaches may be appropriate within authorized services.
  • Support practical harm reduction, overdose response education and linkage to services consistent with setting and law.

Functional support: a three-step choice

Notice

The person uses to cope with insomnia and worries about withdrawal.

Choose

Identify medical withdrawal risk and the person's desired next step.

Review

Review safety, symptoms, access to care and quality of life.

  • Prepare the person's questions and goals for medical/substance treatment referral.
  • Co-create a low-burden plan for accessing safer supplies, treatment and support where legal and appropriate.
  • Identify what happens before and after use without moralizing or escalating shame.
  • Use consent-based family or peer involvement; protect sensitive information and autonomy.
  • Track reductions in harm, function and the person's chosen outcomes, not only abstinence.

Illustrative plan and clinical case

Collaborative goal

Access a preferred assessment and reduce avoidable harm.

Clinical intervention

Use motivational interviewing and warm referral for medical evaluation, with explicit permission for coordination.

Outcome review

Track connection to care, overdose/withdrawal safety, chosen changes and functional stability.

When to revise

Intoxication, withdrawal danger, overdose or acute psychiatric risk requires higher-level clinical response.

Case reasoning

A person describes using alcohol nightly to sleep and fears withdrawal. The first intervention is not an unmonitored taper; clarify dependence risk and coordinate qualified medical assessment.
