Grief and bereavement: distinguish adaptation, trauma and prolonged grief

Grief is a human response to loss, not automatically a psychiatric disorder. Support can emphasize remembrance, connection, routines and meaning; prolonged grief and depression require their own assessment when severe functional impairment persists.

Safety and scopeDo not turn ordinary grief into pathology because it is intense, lengthy or unfamiliar. Cultural, religious and family patterns shape expression. Prolonged grief disorder has specific diagnostic requirements distinct from bereavement itself.

Assessment and presenting context

  • Ask about the person lost, the relationship, meaning of the death, support systems and what assistance is desired.
  • Assess yearning, preoccupation, emotional pain, avoidance, identity disruption and functional impairment across time and context.
  • Review cultural and religious mourning customs and expectations before interpreting duration or expression as abnormal.
  • Distinguish grief-related changes from major depression, PTSD after traumatic death, anxiety, substance use and suicide risk.
  • For children, assess developmental understanding of death, routines, caregivers, school participation and whether the child wishes to remember or discuss the loss.

Differentiate the clinical pattern

  • Grief commonly fluctuates with reminders and can coexist with moments of positive emotion.
  • Prolonged grief disorder is not inferred from an arbitrary number of weeks; diagnosis needs applicable duration, distress/impairment and cultural-context criteria.
  • Traumatic bereavement can include PTSD re-experiencing and avoidance that require trauma-specific consideration.
  • Major depression and suicidal distress require separate assessment rather than assuming they are simply grief.

Evidence and treatment-selection considerations

  • Offer supportive, culturally congruent bereavement care and practical help for people who want it; not every bereaved person needs formal therapy.
  • For prolonged grief disorder, consider trained grief-focused therapy, targeting adaptation to the loss and renewed engagement with life.
  • When PTSD or major depression co-occurs, use indicated condition-specific therapies and coordinate care.
  • For children, use age-appropriate remembrance, family communication and school support without requiring disclosure or an adult-style narrative.
  • Respect continuing bonds, spiritual practices and personal pacing; no fixed schedule for 'moving on'.

Practical supports and visual formulation

Understand

A family gathering evokes grief and the person withdraws from supportive contact.

Choose support

Ask what support, remembrance or boundaries they actually want.

Evaluate

Review belonging, agency, functioning and whether symptoms demand specialty care.

  • Offer chosen rituals or memory activities; no forced letter writing or disclosure.
  • Help with practical routines and school/work adjustments after loss.
  • Support companions in listening without platitudes or premature reinterpretation.
  • Allow varied rhythms of grief without interpreting moments of enjoyment as disloyalty.
  • Track functional impairment and safety separately from the intensity of love or mourning.

Illustrative collaborative plan and fictional case

Goal

Participate in an important family occasion while retaining the right to remember or step away.

Care approach

Collaboratively prepare choices for support, remembrance and boundaries.

What to measure

Track felt agency, connection and tolerable distress.

When to escalate or revise

Persistent severe impairment, depression, PTSD or suicide concerns require reassessment.

Reasoning case

A child becomes upset on holidays after a family death but otherwise participates in life. Distinguish expected reminder-linked grief from persistent disorder before treatment planning.
