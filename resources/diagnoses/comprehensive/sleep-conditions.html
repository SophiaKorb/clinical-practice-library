Sleep conditions: insomnia, sleep apnea and mood-related sleep changes

Difficulty sleeping is not one disorder. Chronic insomnia, circadian timing, sleep apnea, restless sleep, medication effects and decreased need for sleep with mood activation require distinct assessment and care.

Safety and scopeCBT-I is an evidence-based first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults. Sleep restriction should not be initiated casually or without relevant screening and qualified guidance, particularly with bipolar activation, seizure risk or safety-critical daytime sleepiness.

Rapid clinical distinctions: do not treat these as interchangeable

Use these prompts to identify the next assessment or service decision. They are not diagnostic algorithms and cannot replace qualified judgment.

Chronic insomnia

Persistent sleep difficulty despite opportunity, with daytime impact.

Next decision: Consider qualified multicomponent CBT-I.

Sleep-disordered breathing

Snoring, witnessed apneas or significant daytime sleepiness.

Next decision: Refer for medical sleep assessment rather than sleep hygiene alone.

Circadian mismatch

Sleep may occur well when timing matches physiology but not work/school.

Next decision: Examine timing and environmental constraints.

Mood activation

Reduced need for sleep with high energy, impulsivity or grandiosity.

Next decision: Assess bipolar-spectrum episode rather than prescribing sleep restriction.

Sleep safety

Driving, workplace hazards, seizures or other vulnerabilities alter choices.

Next decision: Review risks before specialized sleep scheduling interventions.

Assessment and presenting context

  • Clarify difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, early waking, sleep opportunity, duration, regularity, daytime function and the person's circadian pattern.
  • Ask about snoring, gasping, observed apnea, excessive daytime sleepiness, parasomnias, restless legs, pain, substance use and medication effects.
  • Distinguish feeling unable to sleep while tired from decreased need for sleep accompanied by elevated energy, increased activity or risk taking.
  • Use sleep diary or appropriate structured assessment while considering shift work, caregiving demands, trauma, disability and housing context.
  • Evaluate safety around driving, falls, occupational functioning and medical comorbidity; refer when sleep-disordered breathing or neurological causes are suspected.

Differentiate the clinical pattern

  • Insomnia is difficulty sleeping despite adequate opportunity and is not equivalent to intentional insufficient sleep.
  • Sleep apnea requires clinical assessment and appropriate sleep testing, not only sleep hygiene.
  • Circadian rhythm problems may reflect timing mismatch rather than nighttime arousal.
  • Manic/hypomanic reduced sleep need is a psychiatric assessment flag, not a prompt to use sleep restriction.
  • Sedative effects, alcohol use and pain require their own assessment.

Evidence and treatment-selection considerations

  • Use multicomponent CBT-I delivered by trained clinicians for chronic insomnia in adults where indicated.
  • Components may include stimulus control, cognitive work, appropriate sleep scheduling and relapse review; do not present basic sleep hygiene alone as equivalent evidence.
  • Coordinate sleep-medicine evaluation for suspected apnea and other primary sleep disorders.
  • Prescriber oversight is required for medication decisions, side effects and interactions.
  • For children and complex medical/psychiatric conditions, adapt or refer using age-specific and safety-appropriate pathways.

Practical supports and visual formulation

Understand

The person spends long periods in bed awake and has increasing worry about sleep.

Choose support

Assess the sleep pattern and select qualified CBT-I or a different referral based on the diagnosis.

Evaluate

Review sleep continuity, daytime functioning and treatment burden.

  • Use a sleep-context diary only when it informs clinical choices and does not increase anxiety.
  • Support a consistent but feasible waking routine when clinically appropriate.
  • Identify noise, shift work, caregiving and environmental constraints.
  • Coordinate care for breathing symptoms, pain and psychiatric activation.
  • Measure daytime alertness and safety along with sleep estimates.

Illustrative collaborative plan and fictional case

Goal

Increase restorative sleep and safer daytime functioning.

Care approach

Complete sleep assessment and trial clinician-guided CBT-I if chronic insomnia is confirmed.

What to measure

Track sleep efficiency with professional guidance, energy, function and adverse effects.

When to escalate or revise

Breathing pauses, dangerous sleepiness or mood activation need medical/psychiatric evaluation.

Reasoning case

A client sleeps four hours and says they are not tired while making large impulsive purchases. Assess possible activation before recommending sleep restriction or routine habit changes.
