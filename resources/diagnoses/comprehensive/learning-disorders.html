Learning disorders: identify the skill, context and evidence-based instruction

Dyslexia, dyscalculia and impairments in written expression are not interchangeable. A child can understand concepts while reading, writing or calculating remains unusually effortful; assessment should establish the domain and what type of instruction and access is needed.

Safety and scopeA single informal perceptual activity, IQ discrepancy or checklist cannot diagnose specific learning disorder. Instructional response, developmental/educational evidence, linguistic opportunities and standardized assessment matter. Learning problems are not moral failure.

Rapid clinical distinctions: do not treat these as interchangeable

Use these prompts to identify the next assessment or service decision. They are not diagnostic algorithms and cannot replace qualified judgment.

Next decision: Assess literacy components; use targeted explicit reading instruction.

Assessment: questions that determine the pathway

  • Document current performance and history separately for decoding/word reading, fluency, comprehension, spelling, written expression, numerical magnitude, calculation and mathematical reasoning.
  • Gather work samples, instruction exposure, classroom data, intervention history, functional impact and the learner's perspective. Ask what is easier when information is read aloud, typed, displayed or demonstrated.
  • Consider reliable standardized achievement testing and language/cognitive processing when clinically indicated, using measures valid for language, disability and culture.
  • Assess vision/hearing access, language proficiency, ADHD, autism, anxiety, chronic absence and instructional opportunity without assuming any single factor excludes coexisting learning disorders.
  • Clarify school eligibility and accommodations separately from clinical diagnosis: educational evaluation and a meaningful school support plan may require multidisciplinary processes.

Differential and co-occurring needs

  • Dyslexia concerns word-level accuracy, fluency and often spelling; comprehension can be further affected but is not the same problem.
  • Dyscalculia-like patterns may involve number magnitude, quantity relationships and arithmetic fluency even when language comprehension is adequate.
  • Written-expression problems may reflect spelling, handwriting, organizing ideas, syntax or executive load and require distinct supports.
  • Poor performance after inadequate instruction or in an unfamiliar language cannot automatically be attributed to disability; differences can coexist.

Treatment and service selection

  • For reading, use systematic explicit instruction in relevant foundational skills and carefully monitor response; do not assume generic visual games teach decoding.
  • For mathematics, teach mathematical language, concrete-representational reasoning, number lines, guided practice and feedback targeted to the deficit.
  • For writing, explicitly teach sentence and composition strategies and needed transcription skills, while providing assistive technology when useful.
  • Pair appropriate instruction with accommodations such as accessible text, speech-to-text, time, alternate response format and reduced copying demands; accommodations are not replacements for instruction.
  • Work with school/education teams and the learner to update goals from actual achievement, participation and burden.

Functional support: a three-step choice

Notice

The student completes oral reasoning but cannot independently decode the assignment.

Choose

Differentiate reading access, explicit decoding instruction and an alternative response format.

Review

Compare decoding gains and participation without penalizing use of access supports.

  • Use readable text, oral content access, audio options and reduced copying where appropriate.
  • Teach one targeted academic skill explicitly rather than a diffuse 'processing' program.
  • Review errors qualitatively: phonological, orthographic, number sense, transcription or planning.
  • Use progress samples in the same skill, not merely grades or task completion.
  • Include the learner's preferences, dignity, school participation and family resources.

Illustrative plan and clinical case

Collaborative goal

The learner wants to read grade-relevant content and demonstrate knowledge without avoidable failure.

Clinical intervention

Coordinate specialized explicit reading instruction plus accessible text and a chosen participation support.

Outcome review

Track decoding/fluency separately from comprehension and class participation.

When to revise

Insufficient progress, language-access uncertainty or new sensory/medical concerns warrant re-evaluation.

Case reasoning

A fifth grader reads with support but completes demanding grade-level work orally. Do not infer global intellectual disability from effortful paper tasks; specify academic processes and adequate evaluation.
