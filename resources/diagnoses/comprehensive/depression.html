Depression: assess severity, support function and select effective care

Depression affects mood, pleasure, energy, thought, sleep, relationships and participation. Treatment should be proportional to severity, selected collaboratively and evaluated for meaningful outcomes as well as symptoms.

Clinical boundaryAssess suicide and self-harm concerns directly and contextually. A score cannot predict safety. Distinct episodes of decreased need for sleep, elevated mood or risk taking require evaluation for bipolar spectrum illness.

Rapid clinical distinctions: do not treat these as interchangeable

Use these prompts to identify the next assessment or service decision. They are not diagnostic algorithms and cannot replace qualified judgment.

Less severe presentation

Function is affected, but person's preferences and context permit several initial treatments.

Next decision: Discuss guided self-help, BA/CBT and other evidence-supported options.

More severe presentation

Marked impairment, psychotic features, inability to meet needs or significant suicidality.

Next decision: Prioritize timely clinical evaluation and potentially combined/stepped care.

Bipolar-spectrum possibility

Episodic activation, little need for sleep and distinct changes in energy or risk.

Next decision: Reconsider unipolar formulation and obtain psychiatric review.

Loss-centered distress

Sadness is closely connected to bereavement; yearning and reminders may predominate.

Next decision: Differentiate ordinary grief, prolonged grief and depressive disorder.

Medical or medication effects

Fatigue, pain, thyroid or other illness and substances affect mood.

Next decision: Coordinate assessment and avoid attributing everything to motivation.

Assessment: ask what matters

  • Ask about mood, anhedonia, neurovegetative symptoms, cognition, hopelessness, psychomotor changes, duration, recurrence and impairment.
  • Explore suicidal thoughts, planning, history, access to means and supports through trained clinical assessment and established emergency pathways.
  • Clarify bipolar activation history and family history, as antidepressant or psychotherapy decisions can change when bipolar disorder is possible.
  • Review bereavement, pain, medication effects, substance use, chronic physical illness, trauma and other explanations.
  • Ask about exceptions: where connection, interest, mastery or meaning remains possible, and what it costs the person to participate.

Differential and co-occurring presentations

  • Bipolar depression can look similar between mood episodes; obtain longitudinal history.
  • Grief and prolonged grief disorder are not automatically major depressive disorder; investigate loss-specific symptoms and cultural context.
  • Sleep disorders, pain and medical illness can worsen depression and executive difficulties.
  • Withdrawal caused by danger, exclusion or overload should not be explained solely by depression.

Evidence-informed treatment selection

  • Use severity- and preference-sensitive choices from evidence-supported therapies such as behavioral activation, CBT and interpersonal psychotherapy.
  • Medication may be appropriate; prescribers manage indications, interactions, monitoring and discontinuation.
  • Behavioral activation targets a collaboratively mapped activity/reward/avoidance cycle, not compulsory busywork.
  • Follow symptom trajectory, safety, valued activity and adverse treatment burden; revise non-response rather than intensifying shame.
  • Use age-specific recommendations for children and adolescents, including family, safeguarding and prescribing rules.

Practical support: identify the bottleneck

Observe

Low mood and fatigue make contact with a valued activity feel impossible.

Collaborate

Choose one small accessible step linked to meaning and capacity.

Review

Review actual reward, social contact, fatigue and safety.

  • Explore when daily tasks fail because of cognition, energy, physical access or fear.
  • Use short, chosen contact or activity experiments and measure their effects.
  • Coordinate sleep, medication and medical care when indicated.
  • Plan support to keep essential needs met during acute impairment.
  • Identify interpersonal supports without assuming that more social contact is always wanted.

Fictional treatment planning and supervision

Desired outcome

Reconnect with one valued relationship without excessive burden.

Intervention

Collaboratively schedule a manageable contact and plan for barriers.

Progress evidence

Track desired connection, energy, mood and fatigue, not only compliance.

Revise / refer

Increasing risk, self-neglect, mania signs or poor response require reassessment.

Clinical reasoning exercise

An adult has stopped returning calls and reports exhaustion. Evaluate safety and physical health, then explore whether an accepted contact experiment is tolerable and meaningful.
