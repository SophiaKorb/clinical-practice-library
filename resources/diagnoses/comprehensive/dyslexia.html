Dyslexia: word reading, spelling, assessment and structured instruction

Dyslexia is primarily a difficulty in accurate and/or fluent word reading and spelling, with causes and associated patterns that vary. Comprehension, knowledge and intelligence can be far stronger than performance on print-based tasks.

Evidence boundaryDyslexia is not established by visual reversals, eye movement claims or an unvalidated screening game. Evaluate language, literacy instruction and achievement; early effective support does not require waiting for a diagnosis.

Assessment and differential

  • Document word recognition, decoding unfamiliar words, oral reading accuracy/fluency, spelling and reading comprehension separately.
  • Review phonemic awareness, phonics knowledge, oral language, instruction history, access to books and the language(s) learned.
  • Compare listening and reading comprehension where appropriate, and seek specific examples of effort, fatigue and avoidance.
  • Consider hearing, vision, ADHD, language disorder, literacy exposure, language proficiency and other co-occurring learning differences.
  • Include learner goals, shame or school avoidance and the intensity/cost of compensation.

Direct instruction and progress evidence

  • Prioritize explicit, systematic literacy instruction matched to demonstrated decoding and language needs, with guided cumulative practice.
  • Teach word-reading accuracy and fluency while preserving access to age-appropriate content and meaning.
  • Progress-monitor the specific skill taught, not only overall grades or completion.
  • Coordinate school specialists and qualified evaluators; avoid calling colored overlays or vision therapy an evidence-equivalent treatment for dyslexia.
  • For older learners, adapt intensity and methods while respecting autonomy and practical literacy goals.

Accommodations and support

Identify

The learner can discuss content but struggles to decode written directions.

Choose

Provide accessible oral directions alongside targeted word-reading instruction.

Review

Measure decoding growth and independent participation separately.

  • Offer audio text, text-to-speech, spelling support or alternative answer formats when useful.
  • Do not substitute an audio accommodation for explicit instruction if the goal includes improving decoding.
  • Avoid publicly correcting dysfluent reading in ways that increase shame.
  • Allow enough time and choose representative measures of actual knowledge.

Worked educational planning example

Goal: Read short unfamiliar word patterns with growing accuracy while continuing to learn grade-relevant content.

Intervention: Use specialized explicit decoding instruction plus agreed content-access methods.

Measure: Track accurate word reading, engagement and fatigue separately.

Review or refer: If progress is limited, review teaching fit, language/attention factors and diagnostic needs.

Clinical case question: An academically capable child confuses letters only sometimes. What would you assess before attributing their difficulties to a visual processing deficit?
