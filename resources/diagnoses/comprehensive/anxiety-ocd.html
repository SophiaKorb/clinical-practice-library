Anxiety and OCD: distinguish fears, avoidance and compulsions

Anxiety disorders and OCD are related but not interchangeable. General worry, social anxiety, panic, specific phobias, health anxiety and intrusive obsessions call for distinct functional models and treatment decisions.

Clinical boundaryExposure and response prevention (ERP) for OCD is a specialist CBT intervention. Unstructured reassurance and ritual accommodation can maintain compulsions; risk, medical symptoms and trauma concerns still require appropriate assessment.

Rapid clinical distinctions: do not treat these as interchangeable

Use these prompts to identify the next assessment or service decision. They are not diagnostic algorithms and cannot replace qualified judgment.

Generalized worry

Many future-oriented topics, difficult-to-control worry and functional burden.

Next decision: Choose GAD-specific CBT or guideline-supported alternative.

Panic and agoraphobia

Abrupt intense fear, bodily alarm and feared future attacks or avoidance.

Next decision: Assess medical contributors and use panic-focused treatment.

OCD

Intrusions and visible or covert rituals driven by feared consequences.

Next decision: Offer trained OCD-focused CBT including ERP; do not just reassure.

Social anxiety

Fear of negative judgment, anticipatory avoidance and safety behaviors.

Next decision: Use social-anxiety-specific CBT rather than generic social skills.

Health anxiety

Repeated symptom checking and reassurance coexist with real medical needs.

Next decision: Coordinate a clear medical plan and psychological care without dismissing symptoms.

Assessment: ask what matters

  • Clarify the feared outcome, onset, duration, context, avoidance and effect on functioning rather than starting from an anxiety score alone.
  • Distinguish generalized worry, panic attacks, social-evaluation fear, specific phobias and intrusive obsessional thoughts with visible or mental rituals.
  • Ask about checking, neutralizing, mental review, reassurance seeking and the short-term relief/long-term cost cycle. Covert compulsions are easily missed.
  • Assess sleep, substance/medication effects, trauma, depression, development, current safety, and support preferences.
  • For intrusive harm themes, distinguish unwanted ego-dystonic thoughts from actual intent while carrying out appropriate clinical risk assessment.

Differential and co-occurring presentations

  • PTSD reminders, OCD obsessional uncertainty and GAD future-oriented worry are not interchangeable patterns.
  • Panic requires consideration of medical and substance-related causes of episodes when indicated.
  • Autistic routines or sensory regulation do not automatically imply OCD; examine fear, agency and compulsion rules.
  • Psychotic conviction and intrusive thoughts require attention to belief quality, insight and longitudinal changes.

Evidence-informed treatment selection

  • For OCD, use condition-specific CBT including collaborative ERP with response prevention of mental and visible rituals; coordinate indicated medication with prescribers.
  • For generalized anxiety and panic, guideline-based CBT and appropriate exposure or applied relaxation components depend on the actual presentation.
  • For social anxiety, evaluate safety behaviors, self-focused attention and predictions of social evaluation using targeted methods.
  • For children and adolescents, involve caregivers developmentally and proportionately, protecting young-person autonomy.
  • Evaluate outcomes across distress, avoidance, ritual time and chosen participation; do not require zero anxiety to call treatment helpful.

Practical support: identify the bottleneck

Observe

A feared possibility prompts avoidance or repeated checking.

Collaborate

Identify one safe, agreed learning opportunity that reduces ritual reliance.

Review

Review prediction, learning, distress and real-life access.

  • Map trigger → prediction → ritual/escape → temporary relief → longer-term cost.
  • Plan alternative responses to uncertainty rather than repeatedly proving feared outcomes false.
  • Coordinate medical monitoring without turning clinically necessary care into prohibited checking.
  • Agree on partner or caregiver roles with the person's consent; monitor whether requests become surveillance.
  • Pair symptom measures with functioning, relationship impact and freedom to act.

Fictional treatment planning and supervision

Desired outcome

Finish a chosen daily task with less repeated checking.

Intervention

Formulate an ERP hierarchy with trained support and monitor covert rituals.

Progress evidence

Track ritual time, chosen functioning, distress tolerance and burden.

Revise / refer

New safety concerns, medical uncertainty or deterioration require revised assessment.

Clinical reasoning exercise

A client repeatedly checks a locked door and seeks reassurance. Assess the exact feared consequence and rituals before treating it as a generalized anxiety or simple planning problem.
