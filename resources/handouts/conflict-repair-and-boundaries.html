Relationships · Boundaries · Repair

Conflict, Repair, and Boundaries

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A guide for clients and families

Healthy relationships do not require never having conflict. The useful questions are whether people can stay reasonably safe, communicate what matters, respect boundaries, and repair harm afterward. Repair is not the same as pretending the conflict did not matter.

Before the conversation

Ask: Are we regulated enough to talk? Is anyone afraid? Is there a power or safety problem that makes direct discussion a bad idea? Would writing, a mediator, or more distance be safer?

A simple conflict map

What happened, in observable terms? _________________________
What mattered to me? _____________________________________
What did I need or hope for? _______________________________
What might the other person have experienced? _______________
What remains uncertain? ___________________________________

A clear boundary has three parts

  1. What I am or am not available for.
  2. What I will do if the situation continues.
  3. A consequence I can actually carry out.

Examples

“I can talk about this, but not while we are yelling. If yelling starts again, I will stop the conversation and try later.”

“I am not available to discuss my medical information with other relatives. If it comes up, I will leave the conversation.”

“I can help with homework for twenty minutes. After that, we will need another plan.”

Boundaries are not mind control

A boundary describes your participation or response. “You must stop feeling angry” is not a boundary. “If objects are being thrown, I will move myself and the children to a safer place” is.

Repair after conflict

A useful repair may include:

  • Naming what you did without rewriting the whole argument.
  • Acknowledging the impact, even if the impact was not intended.
  • Taking responsibility for the part that is yours.
  • Making a concrete change or restitution when possible.
  • Allowing the other person to decide how quickly closeness returns.

Repair sentence starters

“I was wrong to __________________.”
“I can see that it affected you by __________________.”
“My intention was __________, but the impact was __________.”
“Next time I will try __________________.”
“Is there something practical I can do to repair this?”
“I am not ready to agree about everything, but I can take responsibility for my part.”

For parents

Repair with a child does not erase the adult's responsibility to set limits. It shows that authority and accountability can coexist. Avoid making the child comfort the adult after the adult apologizes.

When direct repair does NOT fit

Do not use mutual-conflict language to flatten abuse, coercive control, stalking, intimidation, exploitation, or credible threats into “both sides.” Safety planning and specialized support may be more appropriate. A person is not required to forgive, reconcile, disclose, or resume contact in order for therapy to be successful.

Bottom line

Good repair is specific. Good boundaries are enforceable. Neither requires pretending that everyone experienced the conflict the same way.
