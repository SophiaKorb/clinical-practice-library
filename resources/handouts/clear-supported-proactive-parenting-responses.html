Parenting · Practical framework

Clear → Supported → Proactive Parenting Responses

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A practical guide for matching support to the situation

There is no single “best” response for every child or every moment. A short, direct instruction may be exactly right sometimes. Other situations go more smoothly when adults add preparation, visuals, choices, modeling, or environmental support. The goal is fit, not perfection.

Three Levels of Support

Clear: The adult gives a brief, understandable direction or next step.

Supported: The adult adds one useful support, such as a warning, visual, choice, model, or shared first step.

Proactive: The environment or routine is set up ahead of time so the child has fewer barriers and more usable options.

Giving Instructions

Clear: “It’s time to put the toys away.”

Supported: “In five minutes, it will be cleanup time.”

Proactive: Use a predictable cleanup cue, a short visual checklist, and a clear place for each item.

When Frustration Is Rising

Clear: “Let’s pause.”

Supported: “Do you want a quiet break or help with the next step?”

Proactive: Practice coping options during calm times and make preferred regulation supports easy to access.

Supporting Attention and Task Completion

Clear: “Start with the first problem.”

Supported: Break the task into a smaller step and use a visible timer or checklist if helpful.

Proactive: Reduce unnecessary distractions, prepare materials in advance, and plan work/break rhythms that fit the child.

Transitions

Clear: “It’s bedtime.”

Supported: “When this episode ends, we’ll start bedtime.”

Proactive: Use a predictable routine, transition warning, and visual sequence so the child knows what comes next.

Sensory Overload

Clear: “Let’s move somewhere quieter.”

Supported: Offer a specific option such as headphones, dimmer lighting, fewer people, or a short break.

Proactive: Preview likely sensory demands and bring supports the child already knows how to use.

Supporting Social Participation

Clear: “Your cousin is here.”

Supported: Offer a low-pressure option: “Do you want to say hi, wave, or join when you’re ready?”

Proactive: Preview the setting, identify an exit or quiet option, and help the child know what kinds of interaction are expected without requiring eye contact, small talk, or a particular social style.

How to Choose the Level

Start with the least support that is likely to work, then add support when the barrier is not just willingness. Consider language, attention, motor demands, sensory load, fatigue, anxiety, transitions, learning difficulty, and whether the expectation is developmentally realistic.

More support is not a reward for struggling, and less support is not a test of character. The purpose is to make success more possible and gradually build independence where that is appropriate.

When This Framework Does Not Fit

Do not use a simple parenting-response ladder to explain situations involving immediate safety concerns, major disability-access barriers, severe family conflict, or situations where the adult or child is too overwhelmed to problem-solve. Those situations may require stabilization, environmental change, clinical support, or a different plan.

Bottom Line

Clear communication matters. Support matters. Prevention matters. The useful question is not “Was that the best parenting response?” but “What support would make this situation more understandable, manageable, and respectful for this child and family?”
