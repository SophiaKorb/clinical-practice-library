After School Is a Landing, Not a Launch

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A parent guide to recovery after sustained school demands

The central idea

Some children use most of their available attention, flexibility, sensory tolerance, language, and self-control at school. Home is where the effort finally becomes visible. The child may be safe enough to stop holding everything together.

This pattern is sometimes called after-school restraint collapse. That phrase can be useful, but it is a description, not a diagnosis or a complete explanation.

What helps first

For the first part of arrival, lower demands and reduce language. Offer access to food, water, the bathroom, comfortable clothing, movement, quiet, familiar media, or sensory supports. Let connection remain available without requiring conversation.

Try saying

  • “You are home. You do not have to explain anything yet.”
  • “Do you want snack, quiet, movement, or company?”
  • “I’m nearby. We can deal with the rest when your body has more room.”

What to postpone

Postpone detailed questions, correction, consequences, sibling problem-solving, chores, and homework when they are not immediately necessary. Recovery is not the same as removing every expectation. It changes the order: regulate, reconnect, then address the next manageable demand.

Build a predictable landing routine

Choose a usual arrival sequence with the child. Keep it simple enough to use on difficult days. A workable sequence might be: bathroom, snack, ten minutes alone, movement, then one small check-in.

Watch for the actual barrier

Ask whether the pattern is connected to hunger, sleep, pain, medication timing, transportation, bullying, masking, learning demands, social uncertainty, sensory overload, or anxiety about what happens at home. “They saved it all for me” can describe the timing without explaining the cause.

When the child is ready

Ask one question at a time. Begin with needs, not a school report. Try: “Anything you need help with before tomorrow?” If the child wants to talk, listen before solving.

When not to use this alone

A landing routine does not replace assessment of safety, maltreatment, bullying, self-harm risk, medical concerns, significant school refusal, or demands that regularly exceed the child’s capacity. Aggression still requires clear safety limits. Regulation support and safety boundaries can exist together.
