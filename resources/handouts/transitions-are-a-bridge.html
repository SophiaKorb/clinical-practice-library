Transitions Are a Bridge

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A parent and coach guide to helping someone stop, shift, and begin

A transition is several tasks

Moving from one activity to another may require stopping something rewarding, tolerating an unfinished feeling, shifting attention, remembering the next plan, changing sensory environments, and beginning again. A person can understand the request and still need support to carry out the shift.

Find the breaking point

Observe where the sequence becomes difficult:

  • The person does not know the transition is coming.
  • Warnings increase anxiety rather than helping.
  • Stopping is hard because there is no clear ending.
  • The next activity is vague, unpleasant, unsafe, or too demanding.
  • The body needs movement, sensory support, food, rest, or more processing time.
  • The first step is not obvious.
  • The person can transition but cannot recover quickly afterward.

Match the support to the barrier

For predictability: use a schedule, object cue, written note, preview, or one consistent phrase.

For stopping: create a save point, finishing ritual, photograph, bookmark, or plan for returning.

For working memory: show the next two steps instead of repeating the whole sequence.

For initiation: name or demonstrate the first physical action.

For control: offer a small choice that does not reopen the entire decision.

For sensory change: carry a familiar object, headphones, movement break, or other individualized support.

For recovery: build in a transition buffer before the next demand.

Warnings are individual

More countdowns are not automatically better. Some people benefit from several previews. Others become distressed each time the transition is announced. Test what changes the outcome rather than treating one method as universally correct.

Useful language

“In five minutes, we will save this and put shoes on.”

“You can stop at the end of this page or after this question.”

“The plan changed. Here is what stays the same.”

“First, put both feet on the floor. I’ll tell you the next step after that.”

Measure access, not obedience

A successful transition is not necessarily silent or cheerful. Ask whether the person understood the plan, moved safely, retained dignity, and reached the next setting with enough capacity to participate.

When not to use this alone

Do not treat transition distress as a motivation problem until the next setting has been assessed. A person may be resisting danger, humiliation, pain, sensory injury, bullying, separation fear, or a demand beyond current capacity. Safety and accessibility come before smoother compliance.
