Family · Sibling conflict

Helping Siblings Through Conflict

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A family guide to safety, fairness, boundaries, and repair

Sibling conflict is common. The goal is not to eliminate every disagreement. The goal is to help children stay safe, protect boundaries, solve ordinary problems, and repair relationships without asking one child to tolerate intimidation or repeated harm.

Start With Safety, Not Fairness

Ordinary sibling conflict involves two people who can disagree, separate, and recover without one person being afraid of the other.

Pause joint problem-solving when there is repeated intimidation, coercion, serious aggression, sexual behavior, threats, major power differences, or one sibling is afraid. Those situations need adult protection and individualized assessment, not a “both sides” conversation.

Describe the Pattern Before Solving It

Ask what actually keeps happening:

  • What usually starts the conflict?
  • Are there predictable times, spaces, possessions, transitions, or topics?
  • What does each child do next?
  • What usually makes the conflict stop?
  • When does the same situation go better?

Use observable descriptions rather than labels such as “the mean one,” “the dramatic one,” or “the troublemaker.”

Separate First When Everyone Is Escalated

Children usually solve problems poorly when they are flooded, angry, scared, or overstimulated. Create space. Reduce talking. Help each child regulate separately. Save the detailed conversation for later. Separation is not a punishment when it is being used to restore safety and thinking.

Fair Does Not Always Mean Equal

Different children may need different amounts of privacy, supervision, preparation, sensory support, or adult help.

Fairness means each child’s safety, developmental level, disability-related needs, and reasonable boundaries are taken seriously. It does not require identical rules in every situation.

Teach Repair, Not Forced Apologies

A useful repair can include:

  • Naming what happened without minimizing it.
  • Recognizing the impact on the other person.
  • Restoring or replacing something that was damaged.
  • Respecting a boundary or giving space.
  • Making one concrete plan for next time.

An apology is meaningful when it reflects understanding. A forced “sorry” is not a substitute for repair.

Change the Setup When the Same Fight Keeps Happening

Repeated conflict is information. Consider changing the environment instead of repeating the same lecture.

Possible changes include separate storage, protected personal items, scheduled turns, transition warnings, clearer rules about bedrooms or devices, more adult proximity at high-risk times, and planned ways to ask for space or help.

When to Get More Help

Seek additional support when sibling conflict involves injury, fear, threats, repeated coercion, escalating aggression, major family disruption, or patterns that do not improve with consistent boundaries and support.

A therapist or other qualified professional may need to meet with family members separately before deciding whether joint work is appropriate.

Bottom Line

Do not force fairness before safety. Help children regulate, describe what happened, protect boundaries, and practice repair. When the same conflict repeats, change the conditions around it instead of assuming everyone simply needs another reminder.
