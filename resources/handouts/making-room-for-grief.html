Grief · Reading

Making Room for Grief

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A guide for living with loss without forcing a timeline

Grief can include sadness, anger, numbness, relief, guilt, yearning, distraction, physical exhaustion, humor, anxiety, or long stretches of feeling almost normal. These reactions can arrive in waves and can change over time. There is no single emotional sequence everyone must complete.

Grief is not the same as staying sad all day

People often move in and out of grief. Work, play, sleep, laughter, school, parenting, or ordinary errands can coexist with missing someone. A lighter day does not mean the person mattered less.

The loss changes more than emotion

  • Roles and responsibilities may change.
  • Routines, finances, housing, caregiving, identity, faith, and family relationships may shift.
  • Anniversaries, places, smells, songs, paperwork, and unexpected moments may reactivate grief.
  • Children may revisit the loss differently as they grow and understand more.

Remembering can be active

Some people want photographs, stories, rituals, prayer, music, recipes, visits to meaningful places, or objects connected to the person who died. Others need more distance at first. Continuing a bond does not require pretending the death did not happen.

There is no requirement to find meaning

Meaning can emerge, change, or never feel like the right frame. Therapy should not require gratitude, forgiveness, spiritual explanation, or a lesson from the death.

For children

Use clear, direct language about death and answer the question the child actually asked. Maintain familiar routines when possible. Young children may move quickly between grief and play, ask the same question repeatedly, or temporarily return to earlier behaviors.

When more support may be useful

Seek additional assessment when grief is accompanied by suicidal thoughts, inability to meet basic needs, severe trauma symptoms, psychosis, dangerous substance use, or persistent disabling impairment. Prolonged grief disorder is not diagnosed simply because someone still misses the person intensely. Duration, impairment, and cultural, social, and religious context matter.

Bottom line

The task is not to finish grief. It is to build enough support and room for the loss that life can continue to expand around it.
