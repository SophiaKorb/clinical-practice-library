Parent Regulation · Repair

Instead of Yelling

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A practical plan for parent regulation, clear directions, and repair

Yelling is often a late signal

It may mean the adult is overloaded, has repeated an unclear direction many times, feels ignored, or has run out of workable options. Reducing yelling requires both adult regulation and a better plan for the recurring situation. Shame is not a plan. Annoyingly, it has never once completed a bedtime routine.

Before the difficult moment

  • Choose one or two nonnegotiable priorities.
  • Make the direction observable: say what to do, not only what to stop.
  • Reduce unnecessary words.
  • Check whether the child can do the task under current sensory, developmental, emotional, and executive-function demands.
  • Plan what the adult will do if the first direction does not work.

In the moment

  • Pause and lower volume before adding words.
  • Move closer if safe.
  • Give one clear direction: “Shoes go in the basket.”
  • Allow processing time.
  • Offer a bounded choice when both options are acceptable: “Walk to the bathroom or hop?”
  • If the adult is escalating, say: “I need one minute so I can speak respectfully.”

After yelling

Repair without making the child care for the adult.

  • “I yelled. That was not okay, and it may have felt scary or hurtful.”
  • “The limit is still __________. I am going to say it again more calmly.”
  • “Next time I will __________.”
  • Invite, but do not demand, the child’s response.

Look for the pattern

What time does yelling happen? Which demands repeat? What is the adult carrying? What child skill or support is missing? What can be simplified, moved, practiced, or shared?

When not to use this alone

A communication handout is not enough when there is violence, threats, coercive control, caregiver substance impairment, severe depression, or fear for safety. Seek individualized support and use an appropriate safety plan.
