Making Sleep More Likely

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A practical sleep guide for clients and families

Sleep cannot be commanded. The goal is to make sleep more likely by strengthening the body clock, reducing long awake periods in bed, and building a repeatable wind-down routine. Start with one or two changes rather than trying to create a perfect bedtime.

1. Anchor the morning

A reasonably consistent wake time is often more useful than forcing an exact bedtime. Morning light and daytime activity help the body learn when daytime begins.

Usual wake time: __________________
A realistic wake-time range: __________________
Morning light/activity plan: ______________________________

2. Build sleep pressure during the day

Regular daytime movement can help. Long or late naps may make nighttime sleep harder for some people. If naps are necessary, notice whether timing or length affects the night.

3. Make a short landing routine

Choose a few repeatable cues that tell the brain the active part of the day is ending.

My wind-down cues:
_______________________________________________
_______________________________________________
_______________________________________________

Helpful possibilities include dimmer light, hygiene routine, quiet audio or reading, stretching, preparing tomorrow's items, or a predictable sensory routine.

4. Use the bed for sleep as much as practical

If you are wide awake for a long time and becoming frustrated, it may help to leave the bed briefly for a quiet, low-stimulation activity and return when sleepier. Adapt this for mobility, disability, caregiving, safety, and household realities.

5. Work with thoughts, not against them

Trying hard to force sleep often increases alertness. A useful response may be: “Rest is still useful. Sleep will come when it comes.” Keep a notepad nearby if unfinished tasks or worries repeatedly pull attention.

6. Check common sleep disruptors

Caffeine timing: _________________________________________
Nicotine or other substances: ______________________________
Medication timing or side effects to discuss with prescriber: ______
Pain, reflux, breathing problems, restless legs, nightmares, hot flashes, sensory discomfort, or other body factors: __________________
Environmental barriers such as noise, light, temperature, caregiving, or unsafe housing: ______________________________________

For children and teens

Use developmentally appropriate routines and enough sleep opportunity. Avoid turning bedtime into a prolonged power struggle. Check fear, sensory needs, separation concerns, medication effects, school schedule, and family logistics before assuming “refusal.”

Screens

Screens are not a moral issue. The useful question is whether a particular device, activity, brightness level, or late-night interaction is delaying sleep or increasing alertness. Change what is actually interfering rather than making a blanket rule that cannot be sustained.

When to seek more assessment

Persistent insomnia may benefit from cognitive behavioral treatment for insomnia. Seek medical evaluation when there are concerns such as loud snoring or breathing pauses, unusual movements, severe daytime sleepiness, new or severe sleep disruption, possible mania, medication effects, or another medical condition. Safety concerns need appropriate urgent care.

Bottom line

Protect a stable morning, build a predictable landing, reduce struggle in bed, and investigate barriers that a routine alone cannot solve.
