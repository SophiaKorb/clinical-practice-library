Parenting · Co-parenting

Parenting Across Disagreement

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A practical guide when cooperation is limited

The goal

Children benefit when adults reduce their exposure to conflict. This does not require the parents to agree, be friends, communicate frequently, or run identical homes. The goal is a workable boundary around the child.

Keep communication narrow

  • Use brief, factual, child-focused messages.
  • Separate necessary information from commentary.
  • Use one predictable channel when safe and permitted.
  • Do not use the child to carry messages, gather information, or prove what happened.

What children need to hear

  • “You do not have to choose sides.”
  • “You can care about people in both homes.”
  • “You do not need to report everything that happens at the other home.”
  • “If something affects your safety or body, you can always tell a trusted adult.”
  • “Different homes can have different rules.”

When the child reports conflict

  • Listen before investigating.
  • Do not call the other parent a liar in front of the child.
  • Say: “That sounds confusing. I was not there, so I will not ask you to decide who is right.”
  • Address adult questions through the appropriate adult channel.

Make rules local

Say: “At this house, bedtime is…” rather than “Your other parent lets you…”

Keep expectations predictable in your own home. Do not punish the child for differences between homes.

When a child seems drawn into the drama

Children may ask repeated questions, relay provocative details, or watch adult reactions because the conflict feels important and uncertain. Respond calmly, reduce the emotional payoff, reassure the child that adults will handle adult problems, and redirect them toward their own life.

Low-contact structure

  • Communicate only what is necessary for health, safety, schedule, school, and agreed decisions.
  • Use neutral subject lines and one topic per message.
  • Do not answer insults. Respond only to the child-related information that requires action.

When not to use joint problem solving

Do not assume ordinary co-parenting strategies are appropriate where there is coercive control, stalking, intimidation, violence, serious retaliation risk, or legal restrictions. Follow court orders and obtain individualized legal or safety guidance.
