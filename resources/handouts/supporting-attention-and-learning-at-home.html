Supporting Attention and Learning at Home

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Practical strategies for focus, task initiation, working memory, and executive functioning

Learning at home can be hard when a child has difficulty getting started, holding several steps in mind, shifting attention, organizing materials, or staying with a task. The goal is not to make a child sit still or work like everyone else. The goal is to make the learning task easier to understand, start, and complete.

Start With the Barrier

Before adding more reminders or consequences, ask what is making the task difficult.

  • Is the child unsure what to do first?
  • Are there too many steps to remember?
  • Is the work too easy, too difficult, or too long?
  • Is movement helping the child focus, or has something become distracting?
  • Are noise, lighting, seating, handwriting, reading, language, or other sensory or learning demands getting in the way?
  • Is the child tired, hungry, anxious, frustrated, or already overloaded?

1. Make Directions Short and Visible

Give one or two steps at a time when possible. Put important steps somewhere the child can see them instead of relying only on memory.

Useful supports can include a short checklist, picture sequence, sticky notes, a whiteboard, or a worked example. Ask the child to show or tell you the next step when that is helpful, but avoid turning repetition into a test.

2. Make Starting Smaller

A child may understand the assignment and still have trouble beginning it. Reduce the size of the starting step.

Instead of “Do your math,” try “Open to page 12 and circle the first problem.” You can start the first step together, set up the materials in advance, or use a predictable cue that means work is beginning.

3. Use Movement Intentionally

Movement is not automatically a distraction. Some children think and attend better while standing, pacing, doodling, fidgeting, or changing position.

Notice what actually happens. If a movement helps the child stay engaged and is safe, it may be useful. If a particular activity pulls attention away from the task, try a different movement or change the environment. Harmless self-regulating movement does not need to be stopped simply because it looks unusual.

4. Use a Work-and-Break Rhythm

Long work periods are not always more productive. Try a manageable amount of work followed by a predictable break. The useful interval will differ by child and task.

A timer can help when the child finds timers useful. Other children do better with a natural stopping point such as “finish these two problems, then take a break.” Breaks can include movement, water, a snack, quiet time, or another activity that actually helps the child return.

5. Reduce the Amount the Brain Has to Hold

Keep needed materials easy to find. Use consistent places for supplies, visible schedules, examples of finished work, and written reminders. External supports are not cheating. They reduce working-memory and organization demands so the child can use more energy for learning.

6. Build Independence Gradually

Help enough for the child to experience success, then reduce support as the task becomes more familiar. Independence does not mean withholding useful tools. A checklist, visual schedule, reminder, calculator, keyboard, movement option, or other support may remain appropriate even after the child learns the skill.

When the Parent Has Similar Difficulties

The home system also needs to work for the adult. Keep it simple enough to maintain.

  • Use one visible place for the day’s plan.
  • Prepare only the materials needed for the next task.
  • Use shared reminders or timers when they help both of you.
  • Model repair: “I lost track of what we were doing. I’m checking the list and starting again.”
  • Change a system that repeatedly fails instead of assuming either person needs to try harder.

If It Is Not Working

Repeated difficulty is information. Check the task demands again. The child may need a smaller step, clearer instruction, a different format, more practice, a different environment, or support for a specific learning, language, motor, sensory, emotional, or executive-functioning difficulty.

If learning problems are persistent across settings, significantly interfere with education, or require more support than the family can reasonably provide, consider coordinating with the child’s school, health care team, or other appropriate professionals.

A Note About Homeschooling

These strategies can be used for homeschooling, homework, virtual learning, or other learning at home. Homeschooling may work well for some families, but it is not automatically the best response to attention or learning difficulties and should not replace needed school supports or accommodations.

Bottom Line

Make the task visible, make the first step small, use movement and breaks based on what actually helps, and move memory and organization demands out of the child’s head whenever possible. When a strategy repeatedly fails, change the support before concluding that the child is not trying.
