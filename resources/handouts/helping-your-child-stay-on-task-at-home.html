Attention · Executive Function

Helping Your Child Stay on Task at Home

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Practical strategies for starting, focusing, and finishing

Some children with attention, focus, or executive-functioning difficulties may know what to do and still have trouble getting started, staying with a task, remembering steps, or shifting back after a distraction. The goal is not perfect focus. The goal is to make the task easier to enter, understand, and complete.

Start With the Barrier

Before adding more reminders or consequences, ask what is getting in the way. Is the task unclear? Too large? Boring? Overwhelming? Hard to start? Is the environment distracting or uncomfortable? The best support depends on the barrier.

1. Make the Next Step Obvious

Instead of “Get your homework done,” try “Open the assignment and do the first problem.” Use one or two steps at a time when needed.

2. Reduce Unnecessary Distractions

Keep only the materials needed for the current task nearby. A quieter space, headphones, a simple workspace, or moving visual clutter out of reach may help. The goal is not a perfectly empty room, just fewer competing demands.

3. Use External Supports

Attention and executive-functioning difficulties can make it harder to hold plans in mind. Try:

  • a short checklist
  • a visual timer
  • sticky notes or task cards
  • a written “first, then” plan
  • a visible place for completed work

4. Make Starting Smaller

If getting started is the hardest part, shrink the entry step. Try “Work for five minutes,” “Do one problem,” or “Set up the materials.” Starting often matters more than choosing the perfect amount of work.

5. Build in Movement and Breaks

Some children focus better when movement is planned rather than treated as a reward for sitting still. Brief movement, stretching, standing work, or a short break can help reset attention.

6. Coach the Return, Not Just the Distraction

When attention drifts, calmly point back to the next step. Try: “You got pulled away. What’s the next step?” Avoid turning every distraction into a lecture.

7. Notice Effective Strategies

Praise what worked, not just whether the whole task was finished. Examples: “You started after one reminder,” “You checked your list,” or “You came back after your break.” This helps the child learn which supports are useful.

If a Support Is Not Helping

Change it. A timer, fidget, checklist, music, or movement break can help one child and distract another. Supports should earn their place by making the task easier.

When to Look Deeper

If task difficulty is severe or shows up across school, home routines, self-care, and other settings, consider whether learning difficulties, anxiety, sleep, sensory needs, language, motor demands, medication effects, or other factors are contributing. Attention difficulties may be part of the picture without explaining everything.

Bottom Line

Make the task clearer, the first step smaller, and the supports visible. Independence grows when the child practices successful strategies, not when adults simply repeat the same instruction more loudly.
