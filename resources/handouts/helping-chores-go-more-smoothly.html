Helping Chores Go More Smoothly

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Practical support for building household skills

Chores are learned skills. A child may need help understanding the task, getting started, remembering the steps, tolerating the sensory parts, or knowing when the job is finished. The goal is growing independence with the right amount of support.

Start With the Barrier

Before adding consequences or rewards, ask what is getting in the way. Is the task unclear, too big, too boring, too tiring, physically difficult, sensory-uncomfortable, or hard to start? A plan works better when it matches the actual barrier.

1. Make the Task Concrete

Say exactly what “done” looks like. “Put the books on the shelf” is easier to act on than “Clean up.” If the task has several parts, give one or two steps at a time.

2. Choose a Right-Sized Chore

Match the job to the child’s age, skills, motor abilities, attention, and experience. A child can learn a harder chore over time, but may need modeling, practice, or help at first.

3. Make Starting Easier

Use a predictable cue such as “after snack” or “before screen time.” Give a brief reminder, offer a countdown for transitions, or start the first step together if initiation is the hard part.

4. Use Visual Supports When Helpful

A short checklist, picture sequence, timer, labeled storage spot, or example of the finished task can reduce memory and planning demands.

5. Build a Routine

Doing the same task at roughly the same time can reduce negotiation and help the routine become more automatic. Keep the plan simple enough that the family can actually maintain it.

6. Notice Useful Effort

Specific feedback is more informative than general praise. Try: “You started after one reminder,” “You put every dish in the sink,” or “You asked for help before getting stuck.”

7. Adjust the Support as Skills Grow

Help more when a task is new or difficult. Fade reminders, checklists, or adult assistance gradually as the child becomes more independent. If the task keeps falling apart, change the plan instead of assuming the child does not care.

When a Chore Is Not Working

Check for fatigue, hunger, pain, illness, sensory discomfort, language or motor demands, attention and executive-functioning difficulties, transition problems, or unclear expectations. If difficulties are severe across many daily tasks, talk with the child’s health care or therapy team about whether additional assessment or support would help.

Bottom Line

Clear tasks, realistic expectations, predictable routines, and the right supports usually work better than repeated arguing. Chores can teach contribution and independence, but the teaching process should fit the child who is learning.
