Making a Suicide Crisis Safer

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

What a collaborative safety plan is meant to do

A suicidal crisis can change quickly. Safety planning is meant to create options before risk peaks: ways to recognize that the crisis is building, buy time, connect with people, reach professional help, and make the environment safer.

A safety plan is not a promise

It is not a contract that asks someone to promise they will not die by suicide. A useful plan is collaborative, specific, realistic, and practiced. It tells the person what to do when thinking becomes harder, rather than simply asking them not to act.

Know the early signs

Warning signs are personal. They may include changes in thoughts, emotions, body state, behavior, sleep, substance use, conflict, isolation, or a familiar chain of events. The earlier the signs are noticed, the more options are usually still available.

Use strategies that buy time

Some actions can lower intensity or create distance from the urge without requiring another person first. The goal is not to solve life during the crisis. It is to stay alive long enough for the wave to change and for more support to come online.

Move toward people and places

A person may need ordinary social contact, a safer public place, a trusted person who knows what is happening, or a professional crisis resource. Different levels of risk call for different levels of support.

Make the environment safer before the crisis peaks

Reducing access to firearms, medications, or another method a person might use can create time and space between suicidal thoughts and action. This is most useful when the plan is concrete: what will be secured or moved, who will do it, and for how long.

Use emergency help when the plan is not enough

In the United States, call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If there is immediate danger, a suicide attempt is underway, or the person cannot stay safe while other help is being arranged, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.

For supporters

Take suicidal thoughts seriously without turning the conversation into an interrogation. Ask directly about safety, follow the agreed plan, reduce access to lethal means when indicated, and stay connected. Do not make the person manage the supporter's panic.

Bottom line

A safety plan is a tool for the dangerous part of the curve. It works best when it is created collaboratively before or during a manageable period, kept easy to find, shared with the right people, and reviewed after it is used.
