Fairness and Flexibility

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A teen guide for deciding what matters and what to do next

Fairness Matters

Noticing unfairness can reflect empathy, strong values, careful reasoning, or experience with being misunderstood. Flexibility does not mean pretending something is fair. It means choosing a response that protects what matters without letting one unfair moment control everything that follows.

Four Questions

  1. What happened, using facts rather than conclusions?
  2. What feels unfair about it?
  3. Which value is underneath my reaction: respect, equality, honesty, safety, loyalty, predictability, or something else?
  4. What response is most likely to help that value?

Choose the Size of the Response

Let it go for now: The cost of acting is higher than the benefit.

Ask for clarification: “Can you explain how that decision was made?”

Make a request: “Could we use the same rule for everyone?”

Document and revisit: Write down what happened and bring it up later.

Get support: Ask a trusted adult to help when power, safety, or repeated discrimination is involved.

A Flexible Sentence

“I still think this is unfair, and I can choose __________________ because __________________ matters to me.”

Parent Companion

Start with the valid part before asking for flexibility. Try: “I can see why that felt inconsistent.” Then separate agreement from understanding: “I may see part of it differently, but I want to understand your reasoning.”

Avoid

Do not call the teen rigid simply because the issue is inconvenient. Do not demand perspective-taking before establishing the facts. Do not use flexibility language to excuse discrimination, unsafe behavior, broken promises, or arbitrary adult power.

Best Time to Use

Use after arousal has come down enough for reflection. During acute escalation, reduce demands and return to the issue later.
