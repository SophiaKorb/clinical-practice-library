Understanding Behavior in Autistic Children

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A practical guide for parents and caregivers

When behavior is intense, repeated, or hard to understand, it is easy to wonder whether a child is “doing it on purpose” or trying to control the situation. A more useful question is: What is this behavior doing for the child right now?

Autistic children can learn that certain behaviors change what happens around them, just like any other child. That does not make the child bad or manipulative. Behavior usually makes more sense when we look at its function, the demands of the situation, and the skills or supports available.

Start With Curiosity, Not Blame

Instead of asking, “Why is my child doing this to me?” try asking:

  • What happened right before this?
  • What changed after the behavior?
  • Is the child trying to get something, avoid something, communicate something, or regulate something?
  • Is the task clear and developmentally realistic?
  • Could sensory overload, anxiety, fatigue, hunger, pain, illness, or a change in routine be contributing?

Common Reasons Behavior Escalates

  • Communication is hard. The child may not have an easy way to ask for help, a break, more time, or a change.
  • The environment is overwhelming. Noise, light, crowds, transitions, uncertainty, or too many instructions can increase distress.
  • A skill is missing or overloaded. Waiting, shifting attention, tolerating frustration, planning steps, or stopping a preferred activity may be genuinely difficult.
  • The child has learned that a behavior works. If yelling reliably delays a demand, the behavior may continue because it has been effective, not because the child is malicious.
  • Something physical is wrong. Sudden or unusual behavior can sometimes reflect pain, constipation, sleep problems, medication effects, illness, or another medical issue.

What Helps in the Moment

  1. Keep safety first. If anyone could be hurt, reduce immediate danger and simplify the environment.
  2. Regulate before teaching. Use fewer words, a calm voice, extra processing time, and less stimulation when possible.
  3. Describe what you see without accusing. For example: “You look overwhelmed. We can pause, then figure out what you need.”
  4. Hold necessary boundaries calmly. Understanding the reason for a behavior does not mean every request must be granted.
  5. Save problem-solving for later. New skills are easier to practice after everyone is regulated.

Teach the Replacement, Not Just the Rule

During calm moments, practice the behavior you want the child to use instead. Examples include:

  • asking for a break
  • asking for help
  • using a visual choice
  • requesting more time
  • following a short checklist
  • using a sensory support
  • practicing a transition with a timer or countdown

Make the replacement behavior easier and more effective than the behavior you are trying to reduce.

A Useful Reframe

The goal is not to decide whether a behavior is “manipulation.” The goal is to understand what the child is trying to accomplish, what makes the situation hard, and what safer or more effective skill can meet the same need.

When to Get More Help

Seek additional clinical or medical support when behavior is new, rapidly worsening, causing injury, interfering significantly with daily life, or difficult to understand despite consistent supports. A functional behavior assessment or other individualized evaluation can help identify patterns and guide intervention.

Bottom Line

Behavior has a context and a function. Responding with curiosity, clear boundaries, and skill-building is usually more useful than assigning a moral label. Compassion and accountability can exist at the same time.
