Making Plans Easier to Start and Finish

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A functional guide for adults dealing with initiation, working memory, time, and follow-through

Knowing what to do is not the same as being able to start, sequence, remember, shift, and finish it on demand. When the problem is executive functioning, more self-criticism usually adds load without adding skill.

Name the actual bottleneck

  • Initiation: I know the task, but crossing the starting line is hard.
  • Working memory: the plan disappears while I am doing it.
  • Time: I underestimate duration, lose track, or wait for urgency.
  • Sequencing: too many possible next steps compete with each other.
  • Attention: competing input keeps winning.
  • Shifting: stopping one task and moving to another has friction.
  • Overwhelm: the task feels too large or emotionally loaded to enter.

Externalize what your brain is being asked to hold

Use one visible place for the current plan. A calendar, written list, task card, timer, whiteboard, or app can work. The tool matters less than whether you reliably look at it.

Shrink the entry step

Do not begin with “finish the project.” Begin with a physical action: open the file, put the form on the desk, write the first heading, place the medication by the water, or send the first scheduling message.

Use time anchors, not just intentions

Attach tasks to a time, place, or existing routine. “Call the dentist” is a wish. “At 12:30, sit in the car after lunch and call the dentist” is closer to a plan.

Reduce switching costs

  • Leave a visible restart note before stopping.
  • Save the exact next action, not just the project name.
  • Group similar tasks when that reduces setup time.
  • Use a transition buffer when moving between demanding roles.

Make the environment do some of the remembering

Put supplies where the task happens. Reduce avoidable noise or visual competition when useful. Reinforce spoken information with written information. Shorter work periods with movement or breaks may work better than one long block.

If the plan keeps failing

Treat the failure as data. The step may be too large, the cue may be invisible, the tool may be in the wrong place, the task may be emotionally loaded, or another problem such as sleep, anxiety, depression, pain, learning difficulty, medication effects, or caregiving demands may be doing more of the damage.

Bottom line

The goal is not to become a person who remembers everything internally. The goal is to build a system that makes the important next action easier to see and easier to start.
