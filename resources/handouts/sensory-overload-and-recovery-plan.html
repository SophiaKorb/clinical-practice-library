Sensory Overload and Recovery Plan

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A practical guide for clients and families

Sensory overload happens when the amount or intensity of input exceeds what a person's nervous system can manage at that moment. The same environment may be manageable one day and overwhelming another because sleep, pain, hunger, illness, stress, medication, expectations, and cumulative demands change capacity.

Overload is not a character problem

Signs can include covering ears, leaving, shutting down, becoming less verbal, irritability, pacing, crying, freezing, agitation, repetitive movement, needing pressure or movement, or difficulty processing instructions. Different people show overload differently.

For parents and helpers

During overload, use fewer words, reduce demands when possible, and protect safety and dignity. Do not require eye contact, explanations, apologies, or insight while processing capacity is low. Discuss patterns later, when the person can participate.

Important limits

Do not assume every escalation is sensory. Check pain, illness, injury, medication effects, sleep, hunger, trauma cues, communication barriers, environmental danger, and task demands. Sensory tools should be individualized. Do not force weighted items, touch, restraint, or other sensory input. Persistent or complex sensory and functional concerns may benefit from occupational therapy or other appropriate evaluation.

Bottom line

First reduce unnecessary load. Then add supports. Problem-solve after recovery, not in the middle of overload.
