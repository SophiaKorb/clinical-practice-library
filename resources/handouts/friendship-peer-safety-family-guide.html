FRIENDSHIP & PEER SAFETY

A family guide for autistic children and teens

What this guide is for

Autistic children and teens can want friendship deeply and still find hidden social rules, teasing, exclusion, group dynamics, or “fake friendship” hard to read. The goal is not to make a young person act less autistic. The goal is safer, more reciprocal relationships and better ways to notice problems and ask for help.

Healthy friendship usually feels two-way

A healthy friend generally:

  • wants to spend time with you, not only use your help or belongings;
  • accepts “no” without punishment or pressure;
  • can disagree without humiliating you;
  • respects privacy, physical boundaries, money, passwords, and personal information;
  • lets you have other friends and interests;
  • can repair misunderstandings.

Warning signs

Pay attention when someone repeatedly:

  • makes jokes that are only funny to everyone else;
  • asks you to do things that could get you in trouble;
  • pressures you to give money, food, answers, passwords, photos, or favors;
  • threatens to stop being your friend if you say no;
  • excludes you on purpose and then blames you for being upset;
  • touches you, follows you, posts about you, or shares private information after you asked them to stop.

Bullying is not the same as ordinary conflict

Conflict can happen between people with roughly equal power who can disagree and recover.

Bullying involves repeated harm, a power imbalance, targeted humiliation, threats, exclusion, coercion, or fear.

You do not have to “improve your social skills” before adults take bullying seriously.

What to do when something feels wrong

Move toward safety first.

Save messages, screenshots, dates, or names when cyberbullying or repeated incidents are involved.

Tell a trusted adult what happened using concrete facts.

Ask for a plan, not only advice. Useful plans may include safer transitions, identified adults, supervision, a reporting method, seating or schedule changes, and follow-up.

Social skills should increase freedom

Useful skills include:

  • starting and ending conversations;
  • asking directly what someone means;
  • saying no;
  • asking for space or help;
  • noticing whether effort is reciprocal;
  • repairing misunderstandings;
  • leaving an unsafe or humiliating situation.

Eye contact, stillness, small talk, and hiding harmless autistic behavior are not the same thing as friendship or safety.

For parents and caregivers

Ask specific questions instead of “How was school?” Try:

“Who did you spend time with?”

“Did anyone make a joke you did not understand?”

“Did anyone ask you to keep a secret from adults?”

“Was there a moment you wanted to leave?”

“Who felt safe today?”

Take reports seriously even when the social details are incomplete. Help the child reconstruct what happened without turning the conversation into an interrogation.

When school is involved

An IEP or Section 504 plan can sometimes support safer access through supervision, predictable check-ins, social communication support, safe transitions, and clear reporting procedures.

The school still has responsibility for the behavior of peers. Do not make the autistic student the sole target of intervention when the problem is bullying.

Bottom line

Teach relationship skills that improve choice, safety, reciprocity, and self-advocacy. Do not teach compliance, masking, or tolerance of mistreatment as “social skills.”
