Anxiety · Gradual practice

When Avoidance Makes Anxiety Bigger

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A gradual-practice guide for clients and families

Anxiety often pushes people to escape, avoid, delay, seek repeated reassurance, or arrange life so the feared situation never happens. Those responses can bring quick relief. The problem is that repeated avoidance can teach the brain, “I got through this because I escaped.” Gradual practice gives the brain a chance to learn something new.

The goal is not zero anxiety

Practice is successful when you approach something important with enough support to stay engaged. Anxiety may rise, fall, stay steady, or come in waves. You do not have to feel calm for the practice to count.

1. Name the pattern

Situation I tend to avoid: ______________________________
What I predict will happen: _____________________________
What I usually do to feel safer right away: _______________
What does avoidance cost me over time? __________________

2. Build a practice ladder

Choose steps that move toward the goal without making the first step impossible.

Easier practice: _______________________________________
Medium practice: ______________________________________
Harder practice: _______________________________________
Goal situation: ________________________________________

3. Decide what support helps

Useful support can include a clear plan, a support person nearby, a shorter first attempt, sensory access, communication supports, or knowing how to pause safely. Support should make participation possible, not secretly remove every chance to learn.

Support I want: ________________________________________
A safety behavior or reassurance habit I may gradually reduce: ______________________________________________

4. During practice

Notice the urge to escape without treating the urge as an order. Stay long enough to learn something when it is safe and reasonable. Focus on what you are choosing to do, not on forcing anxiety down.

What I noticed: ________________________________________
What actually happened: ________________________________
What did I learn that was different from my prediction? ____

5. Repeat and adjust

Learning usually comes from repetition. Repeat a step until it feels workable, then consider the next step. If a step is consistently overwhelming, make it smaller rather than calling the practice a failure.

For parents and helpers

Do not surprise, shame, threaten, or physically force exposure. Validate distress without automatically helping the person escape every safe challenge. Praise effort, willingness, and recovery rather than appearing calm.

When this guide does NOT fit

Do not use gradual exposure to override credible danger, abuse, bullying, discrimination, medical symptoms, sensory pain, or a reasonable boundary. Trauma-focused exposure, OCD/ERP, panic treatment, eating-disorder exposure, and severe phobias may need a clinician-guided plan. If anxiety is connected to immediate safety risk or severe impairment, use a fuller clinical assessment.

Bottom line

The target is not “prove nothing bad can happen.” The target is learning that uncertainty, discomfort, and difficult feelings can often be handled while moving toward a chosen goal.
