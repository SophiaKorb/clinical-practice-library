Limits, Consequences, and Repair

CPL education draft · For review by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Be clear about what needs to stop, teach what should happen instead, and make room for restitution. A child can need understanding and still need accountability.

Four jobs—not one

  • Safety: Stop immediate danger, protect people, and restrict access to a risk when needed. This is not a bargaining tool.
  • Limit: State the expectation in a way the child can understand: “We ask before using someone else's things.”
  • Consequence: Use a reasonable, predictable response tied to the behavior or broken agreement. Explain how the child can get back on track.
  • Repair: Help address the actual impact—return something, replace it, clean up, apologize when genuine, or make a better plan.

A consequence does not teach an unavailable skill by itself. Pair accountability with modeling, rehearsal, and any needed communication, sensory, or executive-function support.

What to do after a problem

  1. Stop and stabilize. First make everyone safe and reduce the intensity of the moment.
  2. Name the behavior, not the child's identity. “You took the item without permission,” not “You're a thief.”
  3. Listen and check capacity. What happened? Was the rule understood? What support or skill was missing? Do not treat guesses as facts.
  4. Choose a proportional response. Make it specific, possible to carry out, and short enough to review.
  5. Practice a replacement. Rehearse asking, waiting, borrowing, returning, or getting help.
  6. Repair and restore trust. Be explicit about what will count as progress.

Examples

Taking something that isn't yours: Return or replace it, make a plan for asking before borrowing, and supervise access temporarily if necessary. Restore ordinary access in predictable steps rather than declaring the child permanently untrustworthy.

Throwing an object during frustration: Remove the object and help everyone become safe. Later practice a break request, change the demand if it exceeded capacity, and repair damage if appropriate.

Not finishing a hard task: Check difficulty, language, fatigue, anxiety, and access needs before assuming defiance. A task broken into supported steps may be a better intervention than loss of privileges.

Reasonable consequences have guardrails

  • Keep basic needs, medical care, disability accommodations, education access, dignity, and trusted relationships out of the punishment system.
  • Do not use humiliation, physical punishment, deprivation, threats, or forced affection/apologies.
  • Do not remove every privilege at once; specify the behavior, period, support, and route to restoration.
  • A reward can reinforce a new skill. It cannot substitute for teaching that skill or ensuring supervision.
  • Invite the child's perspective and choices when developmentally appropriate. Adults remain responsible for safety.

One useful script: “I understand why you wanted it. Taking it without asking isn't okay. We'll return it, practice how to ask, and make a plan for next time. Here's how we can rebuild trust.”

Three questions for the parent

What behavior needs to change? ______________________________
What skill or support would make the replacement behavior possible? ______________________________
What repair is appropriate, and how will the child know trust is being restored? ______________________________

Safety boundary: Repeated serious aggression, credible threats, weapon access, abuse, or exploitation require individualized assessment and safety planning rather than a routine consequence chart.
