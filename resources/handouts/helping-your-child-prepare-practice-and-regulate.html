Helping Your Child Prepare, Practice, and Regulate

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Practical support for routines, transitions, and overwhelming moments

Regulation is not the same as looking calm. A child can be regulated while moving, using sensory supports, communicating differently, or taking space. The goal is to help the child notice what is happening, stay safe, communicate needs, and use supports that make the situation more manageable.

Start With the Situation

Before teaching a coping skill, ask what is making the situation hard. Consider uncertainty, sensory load, language demands, pain or illness, fatigue, hunger, transitions, attention, motor demands, and whether the expectation is realistic for this child.

1. Prepare: Make What Comes Next More Predictable

Helpful options include:

  • a short preview of what will happen
  • a visual schedule or picture sequence
  • a countdown or transition warning
  • showing where the child can take a break
  • bringing familiar communication or sensory supports

Preparation should reduce uncertainty, not require the child to tolerate an unsafe or inaccessible situation.

2. Practice: Rehearse Useful Skills During Easier Moments

Practice works best when the child is regulated enough to learn. Depending on the child, practice might include:

  • asking for help, a break, more time, or a change
  • using a visual or communication device
  • practicing one part of a new routine
  • trying a breathing, movement, grounding, or sensory strategy
  • rehearsing how to leave or pause an overwhelming situation

Keep practice brief. Model first when helpful, then reduce support as the skill becomes familiar.

3. Support: Respond to What the Child Needs in the Moment

When distress is rising:

  • use fewer words and allow processing time
  • reduce unnecessary demands or stimulation when possible
  • offer familiar choices rather than many new instructions
  • make communication supports easy to reach
  • help the child move toward safety or a lower-demand space

A coping strategy is useful only if it helps this particular child. Deep breathing, quiet spaces, movement, pressure, music, headphones, talking, or being left alone can help different people. Do not force a strategy simply because it is usually considered calming.

Afterward: Learn From What Happened

When everyone is regulated, ask what helped and what made things harder. The goal is not to grade the child’s behavior. Use the information to adjust preparation, expectations, environment, communication, and supports next time.

When More Help Is Needed

Seek additional medical or clinical support when distress is new or rapidly worsening, there is injury or serious safety risk, the child cannot participate in important daily activities despite support, or behavior may reflect pain, sleep problems, medication effects, or another health concern.

Bottom Line

Regulation support is not about making a child appear calm or compliant. Prepare for predictable barriers, practice useful skills when learning is possible, and respond to distress with safety, communication, and individualized support.
