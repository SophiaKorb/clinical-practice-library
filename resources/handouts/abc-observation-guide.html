Behavior · Pattern finding

ABC Observation Guide

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A simple way to notice patterns around behavior

ABC tracking can help families and clinicians describe what happens before, during, and after a repeated behavior. It is a pattern-finding tool. It does not prove why a behavior happened, and it should not be used to assign blame or motive.

What ABC Means

A = Antecedent: What happened immediately before?
B = Behavior: What did the person actually do or say?
C = Consequence: What happened immediately after?

Keep the Description Observable

Instead of: “He was being manipulative.”
Write: “When asked to turn off the tablet, he yelled ‘no,’ dropped to the floor, and pushed the tablet under the couch.”

Instead of: “She wanted attention.”
Write: “After the adult began talking on the phone, she called the adult’s name six times and pulled on the adult’s sleeve.”

Looking for Patterns

After several examples, look for repeated situations rather than single events. Ask:

  • Does the behavior happen around particular demands, transitions, people, times, or environments?
  • Are there signs of overload, pain, fatigue, confusion, or communication difficulty?
  • What usually changes after the behavior?
  • Are there times when the same situation goes better? What is different then?

Do Not Over-Interpret the Data

ABC notes can suggest possibilities, but they do not establish a diagnosis or prove behavioral function. The same behavior can have different causes in different situations. Consider medical, sensory, developmental, trauma-related, communication, learning, and environmental factors.

Safety Comes First

Do not delay urgent safety or medical care in order to collect data. If behavior involves injury, significant danger, sudden major change, possible abuse, severe pain, or another urgent concern, address that first.

When ABC Tracking Is Useful

Use it when a repeated, observable problem needs better description and you want information that can guide problem-solving with a therapist, physician, school team, behavior specialist, or other support person.

Bottom Line

Describe first. Interpret later. Good ABC notes help people ask better questions without pretending the chart already contains the answer.
