Transitions · Support planning

Making Transitions Easier

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A support plan for shifting activities, places, people, or expectations

Why transitions can be hard

A transition asks the brain and body to stop one pattern, hold the next plan in mind, manage uncertainty, shift attention, and tolerate losing access to something. Sensory changes, fatigue, anxiety, language processing, executive-function demands, and past experiences can add difficulty.

Before the transition

  • Make the next step concrete.
  • Use a visual, object, written note, calendar, timer, or short spoken cue that fits the person.
  • Give only as much warning as helps. Some people need several previews; others become more anxious with repeated countdowns.
  • Clarify what stays the same and what will happen after the transition.
  • Prepare needed items before the stopping point.

At the transition

  • Use one short cue.
  • Allow processing time.
  • Show the first physical action: “Feet on the floor,” “Save the file,” or “Put the card in finished.”
  • Offer a small choice that does not reopen the whole decision.
  • Carry a familiar object, sensory support, or transition activity when useful.

After the transition

  • Allow recovery.
  • Notice what helped without turning success into a lecture.
  • Adjust the next plan based on where the sequence broke down.

Developmental adaptations

Young child: use pictures, objects, songs, and the same short phrase.

School-age child: use a visual sequence, timer, and a clear first-then plan.

Teen: collaborate on timing, privacy, and reminders that do not feel infantilizing.

Adult: use calendar prompts, environmental cues, transition buffers, and written closure steps.

When not to use

Do not treat every difficult transition as a motivation problem. Assess whether the next setting is unsafe, painful, humiliating, sensory-hostile, or beyond the person’s current capacity. A transition tool should improve access, not force compliance with harm.
