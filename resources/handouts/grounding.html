Grounding

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Coming back to the here and now

Grounding is a short-term skill for bringing more attention to the present moment when your mind or body feels pulled into a flashback, dissociation, panic, intense emotion, or overload. It is not a test of whether you are “calm enough,” and it does not require you to stop feeling what you feel.

What Grounding Is Trying to Change

During overwhelm, attention can become captured by an internal experience, a memory, a prediction, or a sense of unreality. Grounding gives attention something concrete and current to organize around. The target is orientation and choice: enough contact with the present to decide what to do next.

Five Ways to Ground

Orient: Name where you are, the date or time, what you are doing, and one fact about what is happening right now.

Look and listen: Describe neutral details in the room: shapes, colors, objects, sounds, signs, or how things are arranged.

Use touch or movement: Notice your feet on the floor, the chair supporting you, the weight or texture of an object, stretch, stand, or walk.

Use a thinking task: Count objects, name items in a category, read something aloud or silently, spell a word backward, or describe the next three steps of a familiar routine.

Connect: Use a trusted person’s voice, a pet, a familiar place, or brief contact with someone who helps you stay oriented.

The Senses Are Options, Not Requirements

The familiar 5-4-3-2-1 exercise works for some people, but no grounding method is universal. You do not need to close your eyes, focus on breathing, tolerate touch, use every sense, or pay attention to your body if those things make distress worse.

If Body-Focused Grounding Makes Things Worse

  • Keep your eyes open if that feels safer.
  • Shift from breath or body sensations to external details in the room.
  • Use facts, counting, reading, or naming objects instead of interoception.
  • Choose neutral or pleasant sensory input rather than intense cold, pain, or startling stimulation.
  • Stop the exercise if it is increasing panic, dissociation, shame, or sensory overload.

Grounding Is Not the Same as Relaxation

Sometimes grounding lowers distress. Sometimes you remain upset but become more present and able to choose. That still counts. The goal is not to prove that everything is safe or to make the emotion disappear.

Grounding Does Not Replace Action

If you are in actual danger, need medical care, are being threatened, or need to leave an unsafe situation, the next step may be protection, help, or emergency action rather than staying in place and grounding.

Practice Before You Need It Most

Grounding is easier to retrieve when you have tried it during lower-intensity moments. Build a small menu of methods that fit your sensory preferences, mobility, setting, and communication style.

Bottom Line

Grounding asks: Where am I now? What can I notice now? What can I do next?
