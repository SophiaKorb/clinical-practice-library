Reducing Harm When Substance Use Is Part of the Picture

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

A practical guide for lowering immediate risk while keeping treatment goals self-directed

Harm reduction starts with a simple idea: people deserve practical ways to stay alive and healthier even when substance use is still happening. A person's goal may be safer use, cutting down, medication treatment, abstinence, or something that changes over time.

Start with the highest-risk part of the pattern

  • Using opioids or other depressants with alcohol, benzodiazepines, sleep medicines, or other sedating drugs can dangerously slow breathing.
  • Using more than one substance makes effects less predictable.
  • After a period of not using, tolerance may be lower and overdose risk can rise.
  • Unregulated drugs and counterfeit pills can contain fentanyl or other unexpected substances.
  • Using alone makes it harder for someone else to give naloxone or call for help.

Build an overdose-safety layer

  • Carry naloxone when opioid exposure is possible, and make sure people nearby know where it is and how to use it.
  • Avoid using alone when possible. If another person is present, they should be able to notice an emergency and respond.
  • Use fentanyl test strips or other drug-checking tools when available. A negative result does not make a drug risk-free.
  • When the supply, strength, or tolerance is uncertain, using less than usual and allowing time to observe effects lowers risk compared with taking a large amount at once.
  • If an opioid overdose is possible, naloxone should be given and 911 called.

Do not make mixing more dangerous

Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, and other sedating drugs can combine to suppress breathing. Stimulants and depressants do not cancel each other out. They can make the overall effect harder to predict and can increase cardiovascular and overdose risk.

Treatment and harm reduction can coexist

Medication treatment for opioid use disorder, substance-use treatment, peer support, syringe services, wound care, primary care, and mental-health care can all be part of harm reduction. Needing practical safety support does not disqualify someone from treatment, and entering treatment does not remove the need for overdose prevention.

Stopping suddenly is not always safe

Abruptly stopping heavy alcohol use or regular benzodiazepine use can cause dangerous withdrawal in some people, including seizures or delirium. If physical dependence may be present, make a medically informed withdrawal plan rather than assuming that stopping immediately at home is always safer.

Look beyond the substance itself

Risk is also shaped by housing, violence, pain, isolation, trauma, sleep, mental health, medication access, money, transportation, and whether the person has people they trust. A realistic plan works with the person's actual life.

Bottom line

The first goal is not to win an argument about substance use. It is to identify the next change that meaningfully lowers the chance of overdose, injury, infection, or other serious harm.
