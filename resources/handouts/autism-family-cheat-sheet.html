Autism – A Family Cheat Sheet

Created by Sophia Cohon, PhD

Understanding autism, common misconceptions, co-occurring conditions, and resources for families.

Your child is the same child they were before the diagnosis.

An autism diagnosis doesn't change who your child is. It provides a framework for understanding how your child learns, communicates, builds relationships, processes sensory information, and experiences the world.

The purpose of a diagnosis is not to place limits on a child. The purpose is to increase understanding, improve access to support, and identify strategies that help children thrive.

What Is Autism?

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects how a person:

  • Communicates
  • Socializes
  • Processes sensory information
  • Learns
  • Regulates emotions
  • Adapts to change

Autism is called a spectrum because it affects people in different ways and to different degrees.

An autistic child may be highly verbal or minimally speaking. They may enjoy large groups or prefer one close friendship. They may excel academically while struggling socially, or demonstrate exceptional abilities in one area while needing significant support in another.

There is no single autistic profile.

Common Characteristics of Autism

Social Communication Differences

Autism can affect:

  • Conversational reciprocity
  • Interpreting facial expressions
  • Understanding body language
  • Perspective-taking
  • Understanding unspoken social rules
  • Recognizing sarcasm, figurative language, or humor

Many autistic children want friendships and meaningful relationships but may approach them differently. Difficulty with social communication does not mean a lack of interest in other people.

Sensory Processing Differences

Many autistic individuals experience the world differently through their senses.

May be overresponsive to

  • Loud sounds
  • Bright lights
  • Clothing textures
  • Smells
  • Food textures

May be underresponsive to

  • Pain
  • Temperature
  • Hunger
  • Movement

Sensory experiences are neurological, not behavioral. What appears to be an overreaction may actually be a nervous system responding exactly as it was designed to respond.

Repetitive Behaviors and Routines

Many autistic children prefer predictable routines, use repetitive movements, enjoy repetition, develop rituals, or experience distress when routines unexpectedly change. Predictability often creates a sense of safety.

Restricted Interests and Special Interests

Strong interests may include animals, art, computers, maps, mathematics, music, trains, or video games.

Special interests often support learning, communication, emotional regulation, self-confidence, and identity development. Special interests are frequently strengths rather than problems.

Behavior Is Communication

Instead of asking, “How do I stop this behavior?” try asking, “What is this behavior communicating?”

Behavior may reflect anxiety, pain, fatigue, sensory overload, frustration, confusion, a need for predictability, or a desire for connection.

Development Is Often Uneven

Autistic children frequently demonstrate an uneven developmental profile. The example below is illustrative rather than a scoring system.

A child may demonstrate exceptional abilities in one area while needing significant support in another.

Common Misconceptions

MythReality
Autistic children don't want friends.Many autistic children deeply value relationships.
Good eye contact means good social understanding.Eye contact and social understanding are different skills.
Meltdowns are tantrums.Meltdowns often reflect nervous system overload.
Strong language skills mean “mild autism.”Verbal ability does not predict support needs.
Stimming should always be stopped.Stimming often supports regulation.
Autism is caused by poor parenting.Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition.
Sensory issues are behavioral problems.Sensory differences are neurological.

Commonly Co-occurring Conditions

  • ADHD
  • Anxiety disorders
  • Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)
  • Trauma-related disorders
  • Depression
  • Intellectual disability
  • Language disorders

A Translation Guide for Families

Instead of hearing…Try thinking…
“They're manipulating me.”“They're trying to solve a problem.”
“They're refusing.”“Something may be getting in the way.”
“They're overreacting.”“Their nervous system may be overwhelmed.”
“They're obsessed.”“This interest may help them regulate and learn.”
“They're not listening.”“They may be processing information differently.”
“They're immature.”“Development may be uneven.”
“They're lazy.”“Executive functioning may be impaired.”

California Resources

California Department of Developmental Services – Regional Center eligibility and services

California Regional Centers assess developmental-disability eligibility and coordinate services. Autism is one qualifying condition when California’s developmental-disability criteria are met.

California Early Start

Early-intervention pathway for infants and toddlers with developmental delays, disabilities, or qualifying risk.

Medi-Cal Behavioral Health Treatment

For eligible Medi-Cal members under 21, medically necessary behavioral health treatment may include ABA and other evidence-based behavioral interventions and is not limited to children with an autism diagnosis.

Five Things to Remember

  1. Your child is not giving you a hard time. They're often having a hard time.
  2. Development is uneven.
  3. Behaviors that seem irrational often make sense when communication, sensory processing, anxiety, and executive functioning are better understood.
  4. Progress is rarely linear.
  5. Connection is usually more powerful than correction.
