Suspected Opioid Overdose

Created by Sophia Korb Cohon, PhD

If you are not sure whether it is an overdose, treat it like one and get help.

Look For

  • Cannot wake or respond
  • Slow, shallow, irregular, or stopped breathing
  • Choking, gurgling, or unusual snoring while unresponsive
  • Discolored lips, nails, or skin
  • Very small pupils may be present

Act Fast

  • Give naloxone if available
  • Call 911
  • If breathing is absent or very weak, follow the 911 dispatcher's directions for CPR or rescue breathing; use your training if available
  • Place them on their side if they are breathing
  • Stay until emergency help arrives

Naloxone

  • Naloxone can reverse an opioid overdose
  • It is safe to give naloxone when an opioid overdose is suspected, even if opioids turn out not to be involved
  • More than one dose may be needed
  • Follow the product instructions; repeat a dose if needed as directed

Prepare Beforehand

  • Carry naloxone when opioid exposure is possible
  • Make sure others know where it is
  • Avoid using alone when possible
  • Know that unregulated drugs can contain fentanyl

Emergency note. The person still needs emergency medical evaluation even if they wake up after naloxone. Naloxone is one step, not the whole response. Call 911 and stay with the person. Most states have Good Samaritan laws that may offer some legal protection, but protections vary.

Official source: CDC: What to Do If You Think Someone Is Overdosing; SAMHSA: Opioid overdose prevention and reversal.
